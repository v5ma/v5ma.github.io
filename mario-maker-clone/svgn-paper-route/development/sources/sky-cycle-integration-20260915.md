We merge them by making the side-scroller synthesis serve Sky Cycle's existing identity, not replace it. Sky Cycle should remain a momentum-driven bicycle/unicycle delivery adventure with an enjoyable ground route, an enormous optional aerial network, interconnected curves and partial loops, whip-grappling, discoveries, and a route editor. The new methodology should determine how those ingredients become memorable levels.

After checking the repository's current roadmap and design documents, I would adjust the emphasis of my previous answer: Sonic and Motocross Maniacs should remain the foundation of how Sky Cycle moves. Mario should guide how it teaches. Metroid should guide how places become more meaningful through discovery. Castlevania should guide how enemies and traversal interact. Those influences should not receive equal weight everywhere.

In particular, we should not turn Sky Cycle into a game about repeatedly stopping at locked doors, searching every wall, or fighting enemies in narrow corridors. That would undermine the direction you already established.

The combined design promise should be: "Ride a complete adventure on the ground, discover an interconnected adventure above it, and gradually learn to connect the two into your own route."

That preserves your original goal while giving us a much stronger standard for level design.

The ground route must be a good game by itself.

Your requirement that ground riding remain viable is more important than simply ensuring a strip of terrain reaches the finish. The ground route needs its own beginning, development, surprises, delivery opportunities, encounters, and satisfying conclusion. Otherwise, it becomes an emergency lane underneath the game we actually cared about designing.

I would design the ground adventure first, with aerial geometry temporarily hidden. Can a new player enjoy riding through the neighborhood? Do its slopes, obstacles, deliveries, landmarks, and occasional choices create an engaging journey? Does the chapter become more interesting without suddenly requiring expert techniques?

Only after that works should we add the extensive sky adventure.

This does not mean making ground riding flat or trivial. A ground route can include rolling terrain, modest jumps, bridges, readable enemies, braking decisions, and optional shortcuts. It means ordinary completion must not depend on advanced loop transfers, high-to-high whip releases, or a bonus challenge.

Then the aerial network becomes an invitation rather than an obligation.

The sky route must be a connected adventure, not a collection of stunt props.

Your earlier goal was much more ambitious than placing a few optional ramps above the street. You wanted long, connected sections, partial loops that launch into other loops, and enough continuity that the player could undertake an extensive sky journey.

We should retain that ambition. What changes is how we construct it.

Instead of asking how many curves we can fit into a district, we ask what each curve enables next. A rising runway establishes speed. An open curl converts that speed into height. A receiving cradle offers recovery or continuation. A braking choice redirects the player toward a different branch. A whip peg creates an optional connection between two already meaningful places.

The existing FLOW-ROUTES document already moves in this direction. It describes an authored sequence through the clocktower curl, receiving cradle, canal fork, High Garden, Bellflower hook, reversing cradle, and finish approach. Cloudpost adds an optional upper-to-upper grapple connection. We should build on that work rather than return to disconnected geometry.

The important distinction is between complexity that produces choices and complexity that merely fills the screen.

A district can eventually contain many connected surfaces, but each local view should make the next decision understandable. The whole network can be intricate without every individual moment being confusing.

Most importantly, a short introductory branch should not accidentally commit the player to that entire network. Sunrise's Market Pocket Park already establishes the right pattern: try a low balcony, experience a successful ride, and return to the road. Its development notes specifically record rejecting an earlier version that unintentionally fed into the larger sky network.

That is exactly how Mario-style teaching belongs inside Sky Cycle: the player gets a small, complete experience before being invited into a much larger one.

We should organize route choices around player intentions.

I would think in terms of a reliable delivery route, an expressive aerial route, and occasional discovery detours. These are design roles, not three mandatory lanes repeated across every screen.

The delivery route prioritizes continuity, readable encounters, and access to the ordinary finish.

The aerial route prioritizes momentum, height, transfers, and the satisfaction of maintaining a line.

A discovery detour prioritizes something worth noticing: a hidden postal stop, an unusual view, an environmental interaction, or a connection that makes the district easier to understand.

These routes should repeatedly see and reconnect with one another. From the road, the player might watch the curve they could eventually ride. From an upper gallery, they might recognize the market they crossed earlier. A controlled descent might return them near a familiar landmark rather than deposit them somewhere anonymous.

That is how the world becomes interconnected in the player's mind, not just in the collision data.

The lower route should not always be the bad route, either. It might offer a delivery, a sheltered discovery, or a better approach to a later launch. Choosing to brake should sometimes be an intelligent decision rather than an admission of failure.

We should use Mario's progression within each movement idea.

For Sky Cycle, teaching should mostly mean revealing possibilities in the existing controls, not repeatedly adding new buttons or equipment.

A chapter might explore the relationship between approach speed and landing position. Its first example would have a wide receiving surface and a clear road continuation. Later, the player could choose between two receiving surfaces. Later still, braking before the launch could reveal an alternate connection. The optional expert sequence could combine that knowledge with a whip release.

The beginner and expert would be engaging with the same physical idea at different depths.

This is preferable to a sequence that introduces a ramp, immediately follows it with an unfamiliar grapple, adds a moving hazard, and then demands a precise landing. That piles up unfamiliar demands before the player has developed useful expectations.

Each chapter should have a central movement question. Waterwheel Boulevard could explore speed, height, and controlled descent. Copperleaf Gardens could explore visibility, route discovery, and reconnecting through layered spaces. Those would be proposed identities to develop through playtesting, not assumptions that their current layouts already accomplish them.

The chapter's conclusion should bring its central idea together. Ground players should get a satisfying conclusion using ordinary riding, while aerial players can reach that same destination through a more ambitious combination.

We should borrow Metroid's changing understanding before borrowing ability locks.

This is the biggest correction I would make to my previous synthesis.

Sky Cycle does not need a new ability every time we want exploration to matter. It already has speed, braking, jumping, boosting, two-sided rail riding, and whip-grappling. Much of its progression can come from realizing what those actions make possible.

A player might initially see a curved structure as scenery above the road. Later, they recognize its entry ramp. Later still, they discover that its underside connects to a recovery route. Their understanding changes even though the controls remain the same.

Environmental changes provide another avenue. Tideglass already contains a strong example: operating the sluice lowers Mirror Pool and enables an optional waterline rail, while the continuous dry promenade remains available for ordinary completion.

That is the kind of exploration we should develop further. The player notices something, understands a local mechanism, changes the environment, and gains a new route.

We do not have to introduce swimming, underwater locomotion, or a new grapple upgrade to make that interesting. Those would be separate feature decisions.

My earlier Stormglass Aqueduct example, including its proposed moving-machinery grapple upgrade, was illustrative. It should not displace the existing chapters or become an assumed requirement. We can achieve the same design benefits by improving the routes and interactions Sky Cycle already possesses.

Where revisiting a place is useful, the return should offer a different experience: a faster connection, a newly understood line, an optional delivery, or a changed environment. Mandatory repetition through unchanged terrain should not become the price of exploration.

We should make delivery shape the level rather than sit on top of it.

This is where Sky Cycle can become more distinctive than a compilation of other platformers.

The neighborhood should explain why routes exist. The road serves ordinary doorsteps. A rooftop branch might serve a postal balcony. A canal route might reach maintenance buildings. An aerial express line might connect distant delivery areas more efficiently.

These are proposed ways to give geography a purpose, not a requirement to add a new mission system before improving the level.

Delivery placement can also create interesting movement choices. A straightforward target can teach throwing while riding. An optional target can reward a controlled approach or a well-timed throw. A cluster of ordinary deliveries can justify a quieter section between faster passages.

But we should avoid requiring several difficult actions simultaneously before they have been introduced separately. Jumping, maintaining a grapple, and making a precise delivery at once belongs in an optional mastery challenge, not an early completion requirement.

The existing separation between completing a route and earning additional records is useful here. Market Pilot, the Keeper seal, exploration stamps, and career badges give us places to recognize different accomplishments without making every player complete every challenge.

We should preserve their actual award conditions, particularly accepted authored finishes, rather than handing out progress merely because someone touched a rail or entered an editor preview.

Castlevania's contribution should be encounter composition, not constant stopping.

An enemy should change a riding decision.

It might encourage an earlier jump, make a particular approach less attractive, or create a reason to choose between the road and an elevated bypass. Its placement should interact with the terrain instead of simply occupying empty space.

However, we should not put an enemy on a receiving deck where the player cannot see it before committing to the launch. Nor should we repeatedly place combat interruptions at the end of every satisfying acceleration.

A good encounter should allow preparation. The approach might widen, climb, or otherwise provide time to read what is happening. The player can then decide whether to slow down, jump, attack, or use another route.

The whip needs particular care because it is part of Sky Cycle's movement identity. Level design should make intended grapple targets legible and avoid layouts where nearby targets make the player's intended action ambiguous.

The objective is to make riding more thoughtful without making it less fluid.

Recovery should be designed as carefully as success.

For every important aerial transfer, we should design the intended landing and the likely near misses together.

A short jump might reach a lower rolling receiver. An early release might return to the road. A late release might reach a wider shelf that gives the player space to recover. These outcomes can preserve the feeling of participating in an adventure even when the ideal line breaks.

That does not mean every possible mistake must be safe. Expert branches can carry clearly communicated risks. It means introductory and intermediate sections should not rely on a single narrow trajectory while leaving everything around it undefined.

Sky Cycle's Ride Lab already gives us an important technical foundation: evaluating a continuous ride with carried position and velocity, rather than proving isolated jumps after resetting the rider at each platform.

We should preserve that discipline. A receiving curve is not successful merely because the player touches it. It must also produce a usable next state. Can the player continue, brake, recover, or choose another line after landing?

Forgiveness should primarily come from understandable geometry and reasonable timing windows, not invisible steering, unexplained velocity changes, or snapping the rider onto the intended track.

The enormous world needs memorable districts, not endless repeated track.

Your scale goal should remain intact, but the unit of expansion should be a distinctive connected district.

Each district should have a recognizable landmark, a clear delivery purpose, a movement theme, a ground adventure, and optional connections above or around it. The landmark should help the player understand position and progress.

A waterwheel should be more than background decoration. We might first see it from the approach, ride beneath it, later cross near its upper structure, and finally look back at it from the chapter's conclusion. The same landmark would connect several experiences.

This also reconciles the interconnected-world ambition with the portal system. We should build continuous geography within authored routes while treating portal destinations honestly as separate route journeys under the current architecture.

The current portal system begins a new run; it is not seamless traversal carrying unfinished deliveries through one continuously simulated world. We should not confuse a destination selector with having already built that larger world.

Tideglass can remain a distinct, coherent destination. Future water destinations should earn their existence through different riding and exploration experiences, not merely different water shaders.

For the next substantial redesign, I would use Waterwheel Boulevard rather than invent another destination.

The proposed experience would be: deliver through a working waterside neighborhood, learn how momentum changes your route, and discover an aerial express connection that repeatedly rejoins the streets.

The opening would establish the waterwheel as the destination landmark while giving the player a complete road-level delivery sequence. Above it, an enticing curve would be visible without being necessary.

The first optional ramp would provide a shallow, forgiving branch with an unmistakable road return. It would teach that leaving the ground is an opportunity, not an irreversible commitment.

Farther along, an approach would support a genuine choice. Maintaining speed could reach an upper curve. Braking could select a lower canal-side branch. Remaining on the ordinary road would still produce a complete journey.

The upper route would then develop into a longer connected sequence. A whip link could offer an advanced continuation, but ignoring it would leave another valid line. Likely misses would feed into designed recovery surfaces where appropriate.

A quieter area would provide space to notice an optional delivery or a connection back toward a familiar part of the neighborhood. Exploration would happen somewhere the player could comfortably observe it, not halfway through a high-speed blind jump.

The conclusion would bring the routes together near the waterwheel. Ground riders would finish through a satisfying rolling approach and final delivery opportunity. Aerial riders could arrive through the chapter's most expressive transfer sequence. Both would have experienced a complete version of the same place.

That is a practical merger of the influences without losing Sky Cycle.

The editor should help us author these relationships.

We should preserve the existing independent Bezier controls and editable documents. The methodology should improve authoring, not replace it with rigid prefabricated levels.

A useful editor enhancement would let a designer associate a launch with its intended receiver, recovery area, approach conditions, and next connection. These would be annotations and rehearsal aids, not guarantees that arbitrary edited geometry remains playable.

Reusable examples could demonstrate a forgiving road-to-sky entrance, a braking-selected fork, a high-to-high whip connection, or a safe descending return. Designers should still be able to adjust every curve.

The central authoring question would become: "What ride does this arrangement support?" rather than merely, "Do these endpoints look connected?"

Changes to existing authored chapters must also preserve route identities, saves, medals, controller remaps, and Workshop drafts. A redesigned campaign layout should not silently overwrite someone's edited copy.

Camera, controller, and XR requirements belong in the layout from the beginning.

Your preference for seeing more of the world fits this methodology. The view should show enough of the approach, receiving surface, and recovery route to support a decision. But simply shrinking the rider indefinitely would make threats and contacts harder to read.

We should frame the decision the player is making, not try to display the whole district at once.

The same layout must remain understandable in the normal side-on presentation, the supported 2D fallback, and the existing seated XR diorama. Background architecture must not conceal actual riding surfaces or make decorative structures look like available platforms.

The familiar direct controls should remain intact. Jumping, boosting, throwing papers, and using the whip should not acquire additional menu steps because a new level introduces a more elaborate sequence.

The current XR implementation and its physical-device qualification are separate issues. The level-design plan should accommodate XR, but it should not treat automated stereo rendering checks as proof of Quest comfort or physical controller usability.

The production change is to make chapter quality the next major content gate.

The current roadmap still has important verification maintenance and the bounded water/audio pass ahead of the chapter-quality work. Those tasks remain useful, especially because sound can communicate machinery, a newly revealed route, and arrival without overwhelming the music.

But the next major geometry effort should produce one reference-quality chapter, not another batch of surfaces or another destination.

We should test the ordinary road route with someone unfamiliar with the design, test an intermediate detour, and test the ambitious aerial line. We should preserve unsuccessful attempts because they reveal unclear choices, awkward arrivals, and mistaken expectations.

We should also resolve the recorded uncertainty around the complete optional canal sequence before using it as a template for further content. A successful ordinary finish does not establish that every optional branch works.

Once the chapter is enjoyable, readable, recoverable, and compatible with existing progress, we can expand the world using what it teaches us.

The essential merger is this: Sky Cycle keeps its original ambition, but every piece of that ambition receives a purpose. The road delivers the adventure. The sky delivers expressive mastery. Exploration connects the two. Enemies and deliveries give the route meaning. Teaching, recovery, and readable geometry make that depth accessible.

The result should feel less like a prototype with more track attached and more like a place whose possibilities gradually open up as the player becomes a better rider.
