The strongest synthesis is to use Super Mario World to teach the player, Metroid to make exploration meaningful, Castlevania to make encounters deliberate, and Sonic to make movement mastery exhilarating.

The important part is not putting all their mechanics into the same room. It is giving each design tradition the job it does best. A side-scroller should not demand maximum speed, careful searching, precise combat, and unfamiliar platforming simultaneously. It should move between those experiences deliberately, while keeping the controls and the world's rules consistent.

My proposed design principle is this: build a world that is enjoyable to cross immediately, interesting to investigate, and increasingly expressive as the player understands it.

Super Mario World's greatest contribution is the relationship between simple actions, varied situations, and meaningful discovery.

Its designers were not merely adding more obstacles. In their contemporary interview, Miyamoto emphasized movement that felt instinctively right and rejected impressive technical effects when they damaged the game's balance or flow. Hideki Konno also described the Switch Palace blocks as an important difficulty-adjustment mechanism. Meanwhile, secret exits could reveal new routes and shortcuts on the world map. Exploration therefore affected both where the player could go and how difficult later traversal would be. ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/supermarioworld/ "Super Mario World – 1990 Developer Interview - shmuplations.com"))

The lesson I would take is that a level should develop an idea, rather than merely contain many ideas.

Consider a proposed level about platforms that fall after being touched. First, the player encounters one above safe ground. Then several appear in a row. Later, a familiar enemy changes when it is safe to jump. An optional collectible requires a more ambitious route. The conclusion lets the player combine what they have learned in a satisfying sequence.

That is a useful teaching structure, not a requirement that every Mario level follows an identical formula. The underlying objective is to change the question while preserving enough familiarity for the player to reason about it.

A weak progression makes the same jump longer five times. A stronger progression asks the player to understand the platform, judge its timing, choose a landing, combine it with another system, and finally exploit it.

Super Mario World also suggests that different ambitions should coexist. Reaching the exit, finding a secret, preserving a power-up, and executing a beautiful run need not be the same task. For our designs, the main route should be satisfying without requiring every collectible, while optional goals should expose deeper uses of the movement system.

The biggest thing to borrow is not the appearance of its platforms. It is the way a small collection of dependable actions can support an enormous variety of situations.

Metroid's greatest contribution is making new abilities change the meaning of familiar places.

Nintendo describes the series as exploratory action through a maze-like world, where acquiring weapons and abilities expands the places the player can visit. That distinction matters: progression is not just receiving stronger equipment. It is changing the relationship between the character and the environment. ([Nintendo](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/News/2021/July/Metroid-Dread-Report-Vol-3-Seven-points-that-define-the-2D-saga-2007780.html "Metroid Dread Report Vol. 3: Seven points that define the 2D saga | News | Nintendo UK"))

For this synthesis, Super Metroid is the reference I would emphasize. The design question is not simply, "Where should the next locked door go?" It is, "What should the player notice now that will become useful knowledge later?"

A good obstacle creates a memorable question. A high opening implies that height will matter. A narrow passage suggests a different body shape or movement method. A conspicuous overhead attachment suggests that something could connect to it. A dangerous channel establishes a place the player would like to cross.

An upgrade becomes more satisfying when it answers a question the player already possesses.

For our levels, I would distinguish ability barriers from skill barriers and knowledge barriers. An ability barrier requires something the character does not yet possess. A skill barrier is already possible, but difficult. A knowledge barrier becomes possible when the player understands an existing interaction.

Those categories should not be visually indistinguishable. Otherwise, players can waste time attempting an impossible jump because the game has presented it like an execution challenge.

I would also impose a rule on backtracking: revisiting an important space should change the player's experience of it.

The return might be faster. A previously intimidating enemy might now be easy to bypass. A new route might reveal how two areas connect. An environmental change might expose something hidden. A new ability might turn the old obstacle into a useful tool.

Walking through unchanged corridors merely to reach a newly unlocked door is the least interesting version of this structure. The stronger version lets the player experience their own growth through the same geography.

Metroid contributes a world that accumulates meaning, rather than a collection of rooms that expire after completion.

Castlevania contributes two different strengths, and we should keep them distinct.

The original action-platforming tradition is useful for studying the relationship between movement, attack range, weapon choice, enemies, and constrained terrain. The original game's basic vocabulary includes jumping, crouching, whipping, and using secondary weapons. Its comparatively compact action set is a useful starting point for thinking about encounter construction: the challenge can come from how familiar actions fit a particular space. ([任天堂ホームページ](https://www.nintendo.co.jp/clv/manuals/en/pdf/CLV-P-NABQE.pdf "Castlevania"))

My main lesson from that tradition is to treat enemies as part of the level's geometry.

A platform determines where the player can stand. An enemy's attack determines when that position is safe. A projectile can temporarily close a jump route. A guard can make a particular landing undesirable. A flying enemy can turn an otherwise simple gap into a timing decision.

For example, imagine a guard beyond a small gap. On its own, that is straightforward. Add a low projectile that encourages jumping, and the guard now threatens the obvious landing. The encounter asks the player to coordinate movement and attack, rather than simply press the attack button more often.

However, the player should be able to see enough of the setup to make that decision. An enemy concealed beyond the camera is not equivalent to a clearly presented tactical problem.

The Symphony of the Night tradition contributes a different emphasis: exploration, character development, and alternative ways to overcome resistance. Igarashi explained that the shift toward an explorable castle partly addressed a problem with increasingly difficult stage-based action games: skilled players finished quickly, while beginners could struggle to get through them. He also identified character growth as a way to help players experience the adventure through to its conclusion. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-castlevania-i-s-koji-igarashi-offers-advice-to-today-s-metroidvania-devs "Castlevania's Koji Igarashi offers advice to today's Metroidvania devs"))

For our synthesis, I would combine deliberate encounter composition with multiple responses. A player might fight a difficult guard, use a different tool, discover an alternate entrance, or return with an expanded movement ability.

That does not mean every enemy requires several solutions. It means the entire game should not depend on one narrow form of competence.

The mistake to avoid is borrowing Castlevania's atmosphere without borrowing its spatial discipline. A room full of enemies is not automatically a designed encounter. Each enemy should alter a decision, establish a rhythm, protect something meaningful, or interact with the terrain.

Sonic's greatest contribution is making the route itself an instrument the player learns to play.

The original developers explicitly described the tension between speed and conventional obstacle placement. Yasuhara explained that simply moving quickly into enemies would make the game fail, and that balancing the opening zone required repeated redesign. They also described distinct purposes for different zones: Green Hill introduced fundamentals, Marble emphasized timing, Spring Yard emphasized pinball-like interactions, and Star Light emphasized the sensation of speed. Sonic was not designed around maximum velocity at every moment. ([Shmuplations](https://shmuplations.com/sonic/ "Sonic the Hedgehog – Developer Interviews - shmuplations.com"))

The lesson is to compose movement, not merely increase its speed.

A satisfying sequence might build velocity down a slope, convert it into height, offer a choice at the apex, and then turn the landing into the beginning of another movement. The player is shaping a trajectory rather than repeatedly starting from zero.

For our designs, speed should work both as an immediately enjoyable sensation and as a reward for improving. A beginner should get exciting moments without needing to memorize everything. An expert should connect those moments into longer, more intentional runs.

This is where overlapping routes become valuable. A successful jump can preserve a fast line. A slightly mistimed jump can drop the player onto a slower but playable route. A deliberate detour can trade time for discovery.

I would borrow that as a failure model: a mistake can change the journey instead of ending it.

We should not turn this into a rigid rule that the upper route is always best and the lower route is punishment. Route value can depend on the player's goal. One path might favor speed, another exploration, another safety, and another access to an optional challenge.

Yasuhara's broader design process is also useful here. He described developing individual situations around what the player does and what happens as a result, then building those concepts into the larger level. That supports designing memorable interactions first, rather than drawing an enormous landscape and hoping enjoyable movement appears inside it. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-psychology-the-full-hirokazu-yasuhara-interview "Game Design Psychology: The Full Hirokazu Yasuhara Interview"))

The mistake to avoid is equating Sonic with automatic spectacle. A loop that plays itself can provide a brief flourish, but repeated automation is not a substitute for meaningful control over approach, launch, route selection, and recovery.

I would add two other side-scrollers to strengthen this combination.

Celeste contributes generosity beneath precision. Maddy Thorson documents techniques such as allowing a jump briefly after leaving a ledge, remembering a jump pressed shortly before landing, and correcting certain corner collisions. These mechanisms slightly widen timing or positioning windows in the player's favor. My takeaway is to demand good decisions and practiced execution without making tiny input discrepancies the central challenge. ([Maddy Makes Games](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/index.html "Celeste & Forgiveness"))

Shovel Knight: Specter of Torment contributes an especially useful example of combining combat, traversal, and optional mastery. Yacht Club Games describes adjusting enemy and platform placement to enable advanced shortcuts while preserving the intended main route. In one example, a small height adjustment lets a skilled player use an enemy interaction to reach an upper platform. The additional depth comes from relationships among existing elements, not from adding another major mechanic. ([Yacht Club Games](https://www.yachtclubgames.com/blog/specter-of-torment-level-design-deep-dive-3-5/ "Specter of Torment Level Design Deep Dive (3/5) - Yacht Club Games"))

Putting these ideas together, I would design the game at three connected scales.

At the smallest scale, a single jump or encounter should have a readable purpose. The player should be able to identify the opportunity, understand the risk, and recognize why an attempt succeeded or failed.

At the level scale, several encounters should form a progression. An idea is introduced, developed, interrupted by a contrasting experience, and brought back in a more expressive form.

At the world scale, routes, abilities, shortcuts, and environmental changes should make earlier experiences relevant to later ones.

Mario is especially useful for the progression within a level. Metroid is especially useful for relationships across the world. Castlevania is especially useful for encounters within a space. Sonic is especially useful for the movement connecting those encounters.

This division also resolves their apparent contradictions.

A momentum route should not repeatedly demand that the player stop and inspect identical walls for secrets. A careful combat encounter should not be entered at a speed that makes its opening attack unreadable. An exploration junction should not disappear before the player can recognize that there was a choice.

Instead, use transitions that communicate a change of pace. An uphill approach can reduce speed before a tactical encounter. A broad landing can provide room to observe a junction. A sheltered alcove can support investigation without blocking the main movement line. A clear downhill exit can signal that the next section invites acceleration.

The player can move between different kinds of attention without feeling that the game has suddenly changed its rules.

For the individual level, I would start with one sentence describing its central experience. "Use momentum to climb a broken aqueduct" is stronger than "a water level with enemies, collectibles, and platforms."

Then I would build a short playable sequence that demonstrates the idea safely. After that, I would change the situation rather than immediately introduce another mechanic.

For example, a grapple can first cross a gap. Later, the same grapple preserves momentum around a corner. Later still, the player chooses between a safe release and a higher-risk release. A final sequence combines those already understood relationships.

This creates depth through recombination. It also gives the player a chance to feel knowledgeable.

The rhythm should include relief. A quiet landing, a short stretch of uncomplicated movement, or a view of the next destination can make the following challenge more effective. Continuous maximum difficulty leaves little room for anticipation, discovery, or the pleasure of using a mastered skill.

Secrets should also have different functions. Some can reward observation immediately. Some can reward difficult movement. Some can establish a future destination. The important distinction is that optional mystery should not make the basic rules of the world unreliable.

A hidden passage can be surprising. Whether an ordinary-looking floor is solid should not be constantly uncertain unless that uncertainty is a deliberately taught theme.

For Sky Cycle, the direction we have discussed is a particularly good fit: grounded riding, ramps, optional loops, and whip-grappling can support this synthesis without forcing every player into extreme aerial traversal.

Here is how I would apply it to a proposed stage called Stormglass Aqueduct.

The stage's central idea would be converting forward momentum into height. The player begins on a broad maintenance road, with the destination visible above: a tower connected to the road by broken aqueduct machinery. A sealed canal and an inaccessible moving mechanism establish questions for later.

The first ramp would have a safe landing regardless of whether the player maintains much speed. A stronger approach would reach a small upper reward. The player learns the relationship between speed and height without being punished for not understanding it immediately.

The next stretch would offer a grounded continuation and an optional upper line. Fixed grapple pegs would allow skilled players to carry momentum between ramps. Missing the upper line would return the player to the road, not to a death screen. The routes would reconnect before the level's next major idea.

A guard encounter would arrive only after a visible slowing section. The boardwalk would widen, giving the player room to observe the enemy. Its attack would create a decision about jumping, striking, or using a nearby elevated route. The guard would not stand blindly at the end of a high-speed launch.

The proposed upgrade would let the tether attach to a clearly distinguished class of moving machinery, rather than only the fixed pegs used so far. A safe workshop encounter would demonstrate this immediately. The new attachment type should be identifiable through shape and animation, not color alone.

Now the player could use the moving mechanism seen near the entrance. Doing so would open a shortcut and change the aqueduct's circulation. Water would move through a different channel, exposing a new passage and activating a traversal feature.

That is the Metroid contribution: a new capability changes familiar geography. It is also the Mario contribution: discovery has a structural reward, not merely a collectible count.

The climax would combine a ramp, a moving attachment point, and a choice of release positions. The lower continuation would remain viable. The upper continuation would allow an uninterrupted chain toward the tower. The player would decide how ambitious to be using mechanics the stage had already explained.

After completion, the shortcut would make the aqueduct useful as a connection between destinations. A replay could be about smoother movement, a newly accessible side route, or a delivery objective, rather than repeating the same slow introduction.

That proposed level would contain all four influences, but they would reinforce a single identity. It would not alternate arbitrarily between a Mario imitation, a Sonic imitation, and a Castlevania imitation.

The production method matters as much as the concept.

Before building a large world, I would make a compact movement test area containing the actual situations the game needs: shallow and steep slopes, short and long jumps, low ceilings, moving platforms, grapple releases, landing transitions, and recovery routes. The purpose would be to establish dependable movement distances and timing before committing to elaborate layouts.

The camera belongs in that test area. A route can be physically possible but unreadable at its intended speed. In a hypothetical example, a character traveling at 1200 pixels per second crosses 240 visible pixels in 0.2 seconds. That is not much time to recognize a new obstacle, select a response, and execute it. We should evaluate visibility along the actual trajectory, not merely whether an obstacle fits somewhere on the screen.

For controls, the most frequent actions should remain immediately available. A traversal chain should not require opening a menu to select its essential tool. Simultaneous actions should also be physically comfortable on the controller. Igarashi makes the underlying point directly: an interesting concept cannot communicate its appeal when the controls prevent players from realizing it. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-castlevania-i-s-koji-igarashi-offers-advice-to-today-s-metroidvania-devs "Castlevania's Koji Igarashi offers advice to today's Metroidvania devs"))

Then I would build one representative level using simple placeholder geometry and test it with unfamiliar players. The important observations would include where they hesitate, which routes they notice, what they believe an obstacle requires, where they lose momentum, and whether they understand their failures.

Deaths alone would be an inadequate metric. A player might die repeatedly while learning an enjoyable challenge. Another might survive while feeling lost, bored, or unable to express an intention.

I would separately test the beginner route, the expressive route, and the exploration route. The beginner route should work without advanced techniques. The expressive route should reward mastery without depending on accidental physics. The exploration route should reward curiosity without requiring indiscriminate searching.

For an interconnected world, I would also test progression states. Every one-way drop, persistent shortcut, upgrade, and environmental change needs an escape and save-state check. A clever alternate route should not strand the player because they arrived without an item the designer assumed they possessed.

Finally, I would resist scaling up until the representative level succeeds. Ten additional mediocre levels would not resolve an unclear grapple, an unreadable camera, or an unsatisfying landing. Expanding a proven structure is much safer than multiplying unresolved problems.

The final synthesis is therefore not "more platforms, more speed, more secrets, and more enemies." It is a clear relationship among learning, movement, discovery, and consequence.

The player should be able to think, "I understand this place better now, so I can do something better here."

That is the experience I would make the entire side-scroller deliver.
