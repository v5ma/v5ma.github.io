Vesperfall level-design reset, September 15, 2026.

This is the replacement-level design brief and production directive, not a playable release or a claim that replacement levels have shipped. The user has explicitly identified level design as the central weakness and requested a new approach rather than continued expansion of the original gameplay-demonstration layouts. The proposed first replacement is The Returning Bell. Preserve the existing archery, direct Xbox arrow shortcuts, physical first-person VR, optional hand-tracking menus and player progression. Keep AR available as a distinct presentation, not a substitute for first-person VR.

The user-supplied place-mastery methodology is the basis of this brief. Its central distinctions are spatial knowledge, knowledge of what a space allows, and knowledge of how its inhabitants and mechanisms operate. The chapter names, situations and production decisions below are Vesperfall-specific proposals derived from that methodology; they are not implemented features or descriptions of commercial reference-game levels. Scope is Vesperfall only. This document does not authorize replacing sibling games or applying Sky Cycle's different movement design here.

The priority changes from accumulating features to making one memorable place.

The next content milestone is an authored replacement opening, associated with the existing V49, V50, V52, V57, V64 and V76 work. Water lifecycle, sound, performance and input fixes remain necessary where they support that opening, but room count, enemy count, shaders and checklist completion do not determine its quality. The current roadmap.json and AAA-PRODUCTION.xlsx still describe the released 0.13.0 implementation and its prior next-release proposal; no task is credited as complete by this design document. At the implementation milestone, synchronize the canonical next-release priorities and workbook together. Until then, this dated user directive takes precedence over the older recommendation to do water/audio expansion before level design.

Source inspection explains the distinction we need to address. districts.js creates a connected nine-room seeded arrangement. architecture.js adds upper circulation, and dominions-world.js surrounds it with sixteen further districts and repeated gallery arrangements. oath-model.js adds a staged encounter sequence to selected rooms in that generated world. These are functioning systems, not empty placeholders, but they do not by themselves constitute a newly authored opening built around one interlocking spatial problem. These observations are from source inspection, not a new blind player study or a measured finding about enjoyment.

The design target is place mastery.

The chapter's learning statement is: I can read the cathedral's court, gallery and service passages as one connected place, use its mechanisms to change my approach, and return by a route I now understand. The player should repeatedly move through the same meaningful space under different circumstances rather than repeatedly enter different-looking copies of the same encounter. Discovery provides usable knowledge; that knowledge enables a different plan. A route exists to change observation, exposure, shot angle, access, retreat or timing, not merely to add distance.

The ordinary place and its disruption explain the objective.

Before its abandonment, the complex received travelers through a small refuge, led processions into the bellcourt, moved singers through an overlooking gallery, and serviced the tower through a screened maintenance passage. The return gate connected that service passage to the refuge. Bell machinery and movable screens regulated the court's ceremonial sightlines. Now the processional signal is silent, the return gate is barred from inside, and defenders occupy the court and tower approaches. The first objective is to restore the signal and return to the refuge. Defeating every incidental enemy is not a substitute for that objective. Required encounters, should the graybox establish that any are needed, must be named explicitly rather than inferred from an all-enemies-dead rule.

The opening gives the player a question before demanding a fight.

The player enters a sheltered threshold beside a cracked stone basin and a distinctive pale statue. The silent bell tower is visible beyond the court. A barred service gate sits close to the refuge, with a visible passage behind it but no way to open it from this side. From a protected overlook, the player can see a defender's warning and recovery, the choir gallery, an ordinary ground route and a plausible alternative ascent. A brief instruction establishes the objective, but the architecture supplies the first plan. The initial view must not disclose the entire return loop or rely on a full-map overlay to explain it.

The chapter has three materially different approaches.

The ground approach is direct and offers a sequence of credible cover positions. It lets the player learn enemy commitment and move between attacks, but it exposes the central crossing and gives poorer views into the tower. The gallery approach takes longer and exposes a different lane, but offers observation, side angles on the court and access to the screen mechanism. The Blink approach uses nearby visible, validated landing surfaces to move quickly between these routes. Its tradeoff is aiming and landing commitment, plus the loss of the previous cover position; it must use the current cooldown and ammunition rules rather than invent a new teleport cost. At least one ground/stair route reaches the objective without requiring finite special ammunition, optional unlocks or a perfect Blink.

The routes must reconnect during the problem.

The west cloister climbs into the gallery. The gallery overlooks the already visited court and joins the tower approach. The ground route reaches the same tower through a lower ambulatory and ordinary stairs. A service descent returns from the tower side to an undercroft, then reaches the gate beside the starting refuge. Gallery-to-court landing opportunities let players revise their plan midway through an encounter. The provisional spatial grammar has a local combat loop and a distinct lasting return shortcut; the two must not be credited as the same achievement. A player who legitimately reaches the objective from an alternate route must receive the same objective recognition without crossing an invisible preferred-route trigger.

The return gate is the first lasting spatial revelation.

The player first sees the gate's familiar side next to the basin and statue. Reaching the service side after ascending and descending the cathedral should reveal those same landmarks before any explanatory message. An interior latch then opens the actual collision-backed gate, shortening the return and creating a useful retreat or resupply connection. Opening persists in that expedition's checkpoint. It is not a cosmetic door, a teleport disguised as a corridor, or a notification awarded for walking through another anonymous hall. Subsequent visits should exploit the connection rather than repeat its discovery. Campaign-wide persistence, if later added, needs its own explicit profile and migration policy; do not silently promote an expedition shortcut into a permanent unlock.

One reversible mechanism changes how the space can be used.

A winch on the gallery operates a bank of heavy screens around the bellcourt/tower approach. From the winch, the player sees the screen movement and its consequences. In one state the screens protect a lower crossing but block a useful shot angle. In the other they expose that crossing while opening a gallery-to-bell sightline or a flank. Neither state is universally better. The first graybox should choose one physically realizable configuration and prove the tradeoff, rather than adding unrelated remote switches. Screen state is reversible; the home gate is the separate permanent change. Mechanisms use existing interact/aiming conventions. They must not require a new menu or reassign frequently used controls.

Mechanism changes belong to the authoritative model.

Visible screen and gate positions must agree with collision, arrow sweeps, line of sight, floor support and any route graph used by enemies. Movement and projectile changes cannot be made in a visual wrapper alone. A closure cannot trap an actor inside a solid or sever every recovery route. Implement a blocked closure, a safe opening-only interaction or another explicitly tested response. Rebuilding art must not reset the expedition, duplicate enemies/rewards, reposition the player's tracked head, or erase foot-contact and effect lifecycles incorrectly. No dynamic water-level simulation, swimming or moving lift is needed for this first chapter.

Defenders create opportunities rather than a population quota.

Use a small deliberate cast with roles tied to the architecture. A cantor's committed volley creates a window to cross or Blink. A frontal defender makes side angles and reconnections valuable. A charging opponent makes the lower route and its escape geometry matter. A gallery is not automatically a safe perch: a ranged defender or an exposed transition can challenge it using readable existing rules, not arbitrary damage or enemies that suddenly climb unsupported routes. The first graybox may use existing acquisition, windup, commitment and recovery behavior. Patrol, investigation, distraction and return-to-post behavior are proposed extensions only; do not describe them as present until implemented and tested. Important opportunities recur or can be deliberately triggered; no objective should fail because an unseen one-time schedule expired while the player explored.

Information and pacing are authored with the geometry.

Every important alternative has an observation point, a recognizable destination and a clue to its use. The tower bell, basin, statue, screen silhouettes and maintenance markings provide stable orientation anchors, including when approaching from a new elevation. Sound can reinforce an audible mechanism or unseen approach, but essential information cannot depend only on audio or color. A sheltered opening leads to a manageable crossing, a quiet service interval, a higher observation point, a more demanding combination of traversal and shooting, and the return revelation. These are pacing intentions, not mandatory triggers or a claim that the route has a measured duration. Quiet intervals allow the player to consolidate their mental map. Do not turn the whole chapter into constant combat or constant branching.

Failure and replay must be designed, not accidental.

A missed optional Blink should leave the player on a tested lower route or prevent the shot from moving them, according to the existing landing rules; do not invent unsupported aerial rescue. Lower recovery stairs reconnect to the objective without reloading. A failed encounter can remain dangerous, but the consequence should follow the encounter design rather than missing floor geometry. Neither a one-time switch nor a consumed supply can make the chapter unsolvable. Replay should allow different approaches and an efficient known route. Mandatory tutorial gestures and repeated travel through solved material should not become the price of using what the player learned.

First-person VR shares the chapter's actual gameplay space.

Desktop/Xbox and first-person VR should use the same authoritative layout, objectives and outcomes. VR readability must be evaluated from tracked eye height and physical bow use, not an overhead camera. Test overhead draw clearance, close cover, rail occlusion, seated and standing reach, panel placement, motion comfort and landing visibility. Preserve down D-pad for Blink, left D-pad for available damage arrows, menu/quiver navigation priority, handedness and the existing Quest controller action contract. Never animate the tracked camera to force the reveal. The player should be able to notice it naturally. Hand-pinch spatial UI remains available, but hand-only bow combat is not part of the existing contract and must not be implied by a new level.

AR remains an explicit alternative, with an AR-native extension proposed.

The released game already contains stationary, unscored AR Sanctuary. It suspends the expedition, uses passthrough, disables artificial locomotion/Blink and restores the paused expedition afterward. Preserve this mode. The proposed extension is an Architect's Table: a small, bounded representation of the new cathedral in the user's visible surroundings, with layer inspection and readable route relationships operated by controller rays or pinch UI. It should show only discovered geography by default, or become a full architectural study after completion, so it does not reveal the chapter's first shortcut before discovery. This is a proposal, not an implemented tabletop mode or a claim of a physical surface anchor.

A separate, designed stationary AR vignette could reuse a chapter's bell, cover-choice or shield-timing problem without pretending that a real room is a cathedral. AR must not coat the real floor with fictional water, require walking through furniture, imply that an unscanned virtual support can bear weight, or inherit unrestricted first-person locomotion. Full-scale portals, room scanning and real-world occlusion are different technical scopes and are not assumed here. Check immersive-ar support at runtime. Placement, recentering, exit, controller/hand transitions and safe restoration require explicit tests. The first AR extension remains unscored unless a later design explicitly adds bounded shared rewards and save tests. Physical Quest 3 passthrough, tracking, comfort and performance remain hardware gates.

Replacement must preserve old saved geography.

Do not rewrite the layout behind the existing hollow-dominions-1 generator identifier. Existing checkpoints reconstruct their world from that identifier, seed and depth and validate positions against regenerated geometry. Keep that legacy implementation available to restore those expeditions and to support explicitly retained practice/challenge modes. New chapters need a distinct, immutable layout identifier, such as returning-bell-1, plus bounded mechanism and discovery state. Reconstruct gate/screen state before validating a restored position or in-flight projectile against geometry. Preserve profile keys, atomic reward receipts, earned equipment and stale-tab protection. Starting a replacement chapter must retain the existing confirmation before discarding a different saved expedition. Do not clear localStorage, silently relocate players, or make a new save claim its predecessor's layout identity.

The user-facing replacement is a deliberate cutover, not endless mode accumulation.

After acceptance, new players should enter the authored opening by default. The old demonstration layouts should no longer define the principal new-player journey. Legacy saved runs still reopen their exact old geography, and retained training/challenge modes remain reachable without being presented as the new campaign. This is replacing the default content experience while preserving functioning systems, not creating an unrelated second game. Do not call an optional graybox that leaves the old default unchanged a completed level replacement.

Production starts with a spatial brief, then a gameplay blockout.

Record the physical, conditional, behavioral and information descriptions together beside the new level source. Build The Returning Bell using the actual movement, bow, shield, enemies, collision and Blink predictor. Treat doors, stairs, covers, observation points and landing surfaces as gameplay geometry from the first playable build. Do not add the chapter to the legacy random-room expansion and call that authorship. Art and sound passes follow a successful spatial/encounter pass, while immediately necessary visibility and feedback are present from the beginning. Only after the chapter proves its relationships should modular generation vary secondary details or combine authored arrangements under explicit contracts.

Acceptance requires different kinds of evidence.

Automated checks must cover route connectivity in every supported mechanism state, reversible changes, physical stair traversal, full player-footprint clearance, actual arrow sweeps and Blink landings, finite-resource recovery, legitimate alternate-route completion, nonduplicate rewards, legacy and new save restoration, interrupted interactions, controller-only navigation and AR suspend/restore. A graph check is not a collision test, a model test is not a rendered-device test, and neither proves enjoyment. Use fixed regression seeds and varied samples for any remaining procedural detail without claiming universal coverage.

Unfamiliar-player observation must establish whether players can identify a plausible objective, observe before committing, recognize another approach, explain what the mechanism changed, recognize the return connection and get back without continuous developer coaching. Returning players should demonstrate learned agency and reduced unnecessary repetition. Record confusion and the player's own explanation of the place, not only completion time. Representative physical Quest and Xbox play, seated/standing reach, sustained performance and accessibility checks are separate gates. Do not mark these complete from emulated input or screenshots.

The completion rule remains strict.

A replacement release requires actual integrated source, synchronized release/roadmap/workbook metadata, preserved old-save fixtures, software regression results, a reviewed playable route, a commit on current master that preserves concurrent sibling work, and public GitHub Pages byte/launch verification. Where human or physical-device gates remain open, label the build accordingly rather than certify them. This brief establishes the direction and the next chapter; it does not change release.json, the running game or the canonical task statuses. The next implementation session should begin here and build the first replacement chapter rather than resume unrelated feature accumulation.
