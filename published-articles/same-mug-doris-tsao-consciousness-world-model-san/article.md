# How Does the Brain Know It's Still the Same Mug?

An August Self Aware Networks paper distinguishes the brain's model of you from the learning process that makes it. Doris Tsao's new proposal brings that distinction into focus.

**SVGN.io News Interactive | Neuroscience**

In an [August 13, 2026 Self Aware Networks preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/21928537), Micah Blumberg drew a line between the brain's model of you and the process that learns from what you do. "The term self refers to the whole active learning loop whose contents, dispositions, and consequences remain mutually causal," he wrote on page 2. A learned self-model can take part in that loop, he explains on page 5, without being the whole thing. The distinction matters because what you do next helps create the evidence from which you learn.

Pick up a mug and turn it. The handle disappears behind the cup, but you know it's still the same mug. Your brain has to keep track of the object as the image changes. And in this case, the image changes partly because you turned it. A theory of the scene in your head has to explain how it stays recognizable. A theory of the full learning loop also has to explain how your action changes the next input and how the system learns from what comes back.

## The old notes made two different claims

In a [June 8, 2022 Git record](https://github.com/v5ma/selfawarenetworks/blob/63d76b0b1b5ea1cfbfdd06994ddd180c2e8ee5ea/a0348z%20ctpr.txt#L21), Blumberg described the mind as a process and felt identity as something the brain produces. A [June 10, 2022 note](https://github.com/v5ma/selfawarenetworks/blob/f219cf478aeacbe903467a3ca274b00e2e2de085/a0079z.md#L17-L23) makes a separate point: a network can use patterns in its own activity to build a predictive representation of the world. A [June 18, 2023 passage](https://github.com/v5ma/selfawarenetworks/blob/19ab84002b6bcd922c24f8c468f3ef0fbd1d5343/a0056z.md#L19-L27) likewise distinguishes the world from the perspective the brain constructs. These dates identify preserved wording, not independently verified dates of first public release. The world-model passages do not, by themselves, specify a separate symbol for the self.

## The handle disappears. The mug doesn't.

Doris Tsao takes up the persistence problem in [an Astera essay published November 9 and updated November 10](https://astera.org/understanding-and-engineering-consciousness/). She proposes that the brain maintains representations of objects, the environment and the self, even as its immediate inputs change. She calls those persistent representations symbols. An object-tracking unit could keep the mug's relatively stable features apart from where it is and how it is turned. The system would predict both what it should look like and what it is, then use discrepancies to correct its model.

Stability does not mean the system stops moving. In Tsao's account, activity keeps circulating between brain areas. The larger representation stabilizes because those areas keep predicting and correcting, not because individual cells freeze or stop influencing one another. Her model also includes the body's state and intentions, so it can anticipate how a planned action would change the scene. She draws explicitly on [the 2022 work she wrote with Thomas Tsao on object segmentation and tracking](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2204248119). That is a published antecedent for the tracking idea, not a demonstration of the whole 2026 proposal.

## Where the two accounts meet, and where they part

The strongest overlap is in what the systems do. Both accounts build a model of the world and body from changing signals. Both use predictions and new evidence to revise it. And in both, actions help determine what evidence comes back. The model is part of an ongoing exchange with the world, not an image that sits on a shelf.

The difference is what each account asks us to identify. Tsao connects conscious experience to the larger representation that settles while the activity maintaining it continues. In Blumberg's [Paper 104](https://zenodo.org/records/21928537), the self is the entire process that includes the current representation, the action, its consequences and what the system learns for next time. A model of yourself can matter to both accounts. Neither the shared roles nor that difference in scope establishes that the models implement the same mathematical rule or the same biological circuit.

Paper 104's synthetic studies and formal checks do not establish that a biological brain works this way. Tsao's proposal to change a particular perception by intervening in a neural representation is likewise a plan for future experiments, not evidence that a lab can already write a particular thought or experience into the brain.

The mug suggests an experiment. A subject could see matched views of a mug turning, once under their control and once not. Researchers could measure both whether the mug is recognized and what the subject learns for the next turn. Both accounts give action and its consequences a role. To tell the accounts apart, researchers would need each to make an explicit, different prediction about what changes and when.
