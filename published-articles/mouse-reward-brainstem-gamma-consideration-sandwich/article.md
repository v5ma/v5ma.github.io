# What Changes in a Mouse's Brain When the Reward Arrives

The Gamma Consideration Sandwich asks what makes a signal matter to the circuit receiving it. A cortex-to-brainstem study finds one place where the answer depends on the animal's state.

**SVGN.io News Interactive | Neuroscience**

In a paper Zenodo lists as published on July 29, 2026, Micah Blumberg proposed that the effect of a signal can depend on the receiving circuit's state and timing. The [Self Aware Networks paper](https://zenodo.org/records/21693949) calls one version of that idea the **Gamma Consideration Sandwich**. A receiving circuit has to take into account what it senses, what it expects, and what action the body just took or what feedback that action produced. The proposal is that the timing and state of the receiving circuit help determine how those three inputs work together.

Think about turning your head. The world moves across your field of view, but your brain can expect that motion because you made it happen. A similar shift without that expectation might mean something moved in front of you. The model is not just a claim that neurons fire in rhythm. It asks how a circuit decides what incoming evidence means when the body is already doing something.

## Before the reward, after the reward

A [November 6 bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.05.764652v1) from Timothy A. Machado and colleagues turns part of that question into a measurement. A mouse can lick while seeking a reward, and it can lick after getting it. The movements belong to the same broad class, but the authors distinguish the two motivational conditions. They recorded at the same time in secondary motor cortex, a movement-related cortical area, and the motor medulla, a part of the brainstem involved in movement.

Activity in the two areas was more strongly correlated during reward-seeking licks than during licks after reward. The medulla's activity also lined up more tightly with individual licks than the cortical activity did, and it used a phase-based population code to represent lick direction. The authors report a light-based neural intervention that provided evidence of causal dependence, and recurrent-network models that supported their interpretation. These findings are reported in the preprint abstract.

## A relevant result, not the whole mechanism

Here is the point of contact with SAN: communication between two parts of a movement circuit did not look the same in both behavioral states. That is a narrower claim than saying the Gamma Consideration Sandwich has been demonstrated. The study reports a movement-related neural population phase code; the abstract does not identify it as gamma phase or specify the full variable mapping needed for GCS. A deeper match in the role of phase remains an open possibility.

Nor does the paper's name mean the SAN model requires the same arrangement of frequencies in every part of the brain. The July paper explicitly treats the name as a proposed interaction, not a universal frequency stack, and warns against copying its provisional cortical layer arrangement into motor areas with a different structure. The relevant question is whether the experiment tests the same role for receiver state, not whether its authors used the word gamma.

A follow-up aimed at testing the Gamma Consideration Sandwich could vary the receiving circuit's neural timing while keeping movement and motivation comparable. It would ask whether that timing changes how predictions, sensory evidence and action feedback update the circuit's estimate of the body's state and its surroundings, and whether the update affects the next movement. A code for lick direction alone cannot answer that question.

The mouse result is already interesting without those extra claims. The next time we ask where the brain sends a motor command, we also have to ask what state the receiving circuit is in when the message arrives. The answer may change before the reward does.
