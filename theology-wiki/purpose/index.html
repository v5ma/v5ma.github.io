The purpose letter · November 9, 2026

What I hope to accomplish

The letter below is preserved in full as selected for this page, with its original wording and paragraph order. The reading map that follows is a new guide connecting it to the existing wiki.

Your question is fair. What I hope to accomplish is to get closer to what Jesus actually taught and what his teachings could mean for the world today. I believe Jesus was a radical teacher of righteousness who wanted justice, peace and healing in this world. If we can recover a clearer understanding of his message, I think it could help us work toward those things now.

The Bible itself speaks of things being revealed or understood in the future. I think we may be living in a time when new discoveries and insights can help us understand the past more clearly. That’s why I’m looking at the biblical texts alongside archaeology, writings outside the Bible and the work of historical-critical scholars. I’m comparing when sources appeared and how their accounts agree or differ.

What has frustrated me is that I sometimes feel my reading is dismissed because it doesn’t agree with the orthodox interpretation. I’m not asking you to abandon what you believe. I’m asking whether we can look at the evidence and consider that a different interpretation may be possible.

Our conversation began with questions about what Jesus taught, what the earliest Christians believed about resurrection, and how Paul’s writings compare with the later Gospel accounts. It also involves how we weigh biblical texts alongside archaeology, writings outside the Bible, and historical scholarship. Now you’ve asked what I hope to accomplish by exploring those questions.

I'm searching for the Unorthodox Interpretation that changes the Orthodox Interpretation.

What did the earliest sources say? How did they change over time. What did early Jesus himself teach? How was his teaching corrupted by others in the texts that we received. How do the bible texts that were rejected from the bible by the Empire illuminate the conflict inside the New Testament itself.

What I hope to accomplish is to find an unorthodox interpretation that challenges and potentially changes the orthodox interpretation. I’m asking what the earliest sources say, how their accounts and ideas changed over time, and what Jesus himself may have taught before later writers interpreted or altered his message.

I’m also interested in whether texts outside the Bible, including texts that were excluded from the canon, can illuminate disagreements within the New Testament itself. I want to compare those writings with archaeology and historical-critical scholarship, rather than assume that the interpretation handed down by the church is the only way to understand the evidence.

For me, this matters because I believe recovering a clearer picture of Jesus’s teachings about righteousness, justice, peace and healing could help us put those teachings into practice in the world today.

From the letter to the library

How each part contributes

The letter gives the inquiry a direction: recover what was taught, examine how interpretations changed, and ask what that understanding calls us to do. The wiki’s eight topic collections approach these questions from different places.

They also contain competing hypotheses, independent religious traditions and arguments that may challenge one another. The connections below explain why a section belongs in the inquiry while leaving those differences available for examination.

01 / Jesus, ethics & authority

What did Jesus teach, and how did his message change?

This is the letter’s starting point. I compare teachings about righteousness, wealth, mercy and peace with resurrection traditions, Paul’s letters and later Gospel narratives. The Teacher of Righteousness inquiry tests a stronger proposal about the identity and chronology behind the received biography. These investigations ask how a teacher’s message survives, and where later interpretation may have displaced it.

02 / Ancient texts & origins

What survives from the past, and what can it establish?

Manuscripts, archaeological contexts, divine names and competing chronologies let me investigate what earlier witnesses actually preserve. The work on Exodus, Egypt, priestly communities and the survival of texts gives the Jesus inquiry a longer history. I keep the date of a surviving copy separate from a work’s composition, its narrated events and a proposed historical reconstruction.

03 / Traditions in conversation

What changes when we listen beyond our own canon?

The wiki extends beyond Christian origins. Samaritan, Jewish, Christian, Muslim, Mandaean, Buddhist, Zoroastrian and other traditions bring distinct texts, practices and claims to the inquiry. Comparative studies investigate shared inheritances, rival continuations and possible transmission while keeping real disagreements visible. A living tradition has its own history and concerns; its value is not limited to what it might confirm about Jesus. Coverage ranges from developed studies to short introductions and research prospects.

04 / Gnosticism & awakening

How could recovering a teaching change the person who reads it?

The inquiry moves from recovering words to examining transformation. Thomas, Nag Hammadi, the Temple-trauma hypothesis and Cognitive Gnosticism offer different ways to investigate knowledge, recognition and inward change. Christic Self-Formation Theory develops a constructive account of how an inward model of Christ can shape conduct and be corrected. Historical claims and contemporary models remain distinct even where they illuminate one another.

The practical question is how an interpretation helps people become more responsible, compassionate and open to correction.

An inquiry that can be corrected

How to read the inquiry

The letter states a purpose and a theological conviction. Its questions about corruption, exclusion and empire call for investigation of specific texts, communities and decisions. Each historical explanation has to account for both supporting evidence and evidence that resists it.

  1. Read the witness. Distinguish what a source says from the historical explanation inferred from it. Compare canonical and noncanonical texts with attention to genre, dating and transmission.
  2. Keep the alternatives visible. An earlier source, a later surviving copy, a shared theme and a demonstrated line of transmission answer different questions. A minority interpretation deserves examination on its evidence.
  3. Ask what changes. Trace how a reading affects conduct, institutions and the work of repair, and what new evidence would make us revise it.

Read the expanded inquiry into unorthodox interpretation, canon and empire, then consult the editorial method, comparative claim audit and source atlas.
