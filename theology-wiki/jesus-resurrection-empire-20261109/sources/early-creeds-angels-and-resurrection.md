# Early creeds, angels and resurrection evidence

The question is whether early traditions establish the later orthodox interpretation, or instead preserve a more diverse history. The passages themselves have to decide how far either argument can go. Personal judgments about an apologist or a critic do not substitute for checking a source.

## Early testimony does not supply every later detail

First Corinthians, usually dated to the mid-50s CE, predates the canonical Gospels. In 15:3–8 Paul transmits received material about Christ’s death, burial, resurrection, and appearances. Scholars commonly identify an earlier formula, especially in verses 3b–5, but disagree about its extent and subsequent additions. It is misleading to label all of verses 3–8 an independently preserved creed from a precisely established year. Paul’s own appearance belongs to his presentation of the tradition; the date and circumstances of its original formulation remain reconstructed.

Paul does not state where or when he received every element. Galatians 1:18–19 describes a visit to Cephas and James, but does not identify that visit as the occasion on which this entire formula was transmitted. A proposed date within a few years of the crucifixion is a historical reconstruction; “within months” is not a date printed on a surviving creed. [Galatians 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians+1&version=NRSVUE).

The passage contains no explicit account of women discovering an empty tomb, touching wounds, or Jesus eating. That absence leaves the earliest surviving summary less narratively specific than later accounts. It does not establish that Paul rejected those details. Equally, importing them into Paul as though he narrated them obscures the evidence. His use of the same appearance verb for earlier witnesses and himself establishes a shared category of revelation or encounter, not necessarily identical sensory experiences. [Primary text: 1 Corinthians 15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15&version=NRSVUE).

Luke explicitly presents Jesus as touchable and eating fish, while also narrating his disappearance at Emmaus. John describes wounds, an invitation to Thomas to touch them, appearances despite locked doors, and a shoreline meal. Importantly, John never explicitly says Thomas accepted the invitation or that Jesus himself ate the breakfast. These narratives combine continuity with Jesus’ former body and extraordinary capacities; they are not descriptions of merely returning to ordinary mortal existence. [Primary text: Luke 24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+24&version=NRSVUE); [primary text: John 20–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+20-21&version=NRSVUE).

One interpretation sees increasing narrative emphasis on tangible embodiment. Another understands the Gospels as elaborating what resurrection already implied. Chronological differences alone cannot determine whether later details preserve memory, develop earlier traditions, or serve theological purposes.

Even a very early formula directly establishes early proclamation, not independent verification of everything proclaimed. Conversely, early proclamation cannot be dismissed simply because it was meaningful to a believing community. No fixed minimum number of decades guarantees or excludes narrative development.

## Paul’s spiritual body is not straightforwardly an immaterial ghost

Paul’s contrast between natural and spiritual bodies should not simply be translated into the modern opposition between matter and immateriality. Dale Martin interprets the spiritual body against ancient conceptions of pneuma as a refined material substance. Other interpreters understand the contrast primarily as bodies sustained by ordinary life versus bodies transformed and empowered by God’s Spirit.

Both readings challenge the assumption that resurrection means unchanged biological resuscitation. They differ over the nature of continuity and transformation. Paul’s seed analogy allows substantial transformation while retaining continuity; it does not, by itself, settle whether the original corpse remains behind. James Ware argues for bodily resurrection within the early formula, providing an important counterweight to readings that regard Paul as indifferent to the corpse. These are competing interpretations; “spiritual” cannot do the work of a full historical argument.

## Philippians and Colossians: elevated Christology, uncertain reconstructed dates

Philippians is generally accepted as Pauline and dated within Paul’s lifetime, commonly to the 50s or early 60s, depending on the imprisonment proposed. Philippians 2:6–11 describes Christ’s humiliation, death, and exaltation. It does not explicitly narrate resurrection. Its movement from divine form to human likeness supports a preexistence reading, while Adamic interpretations emphasize refusal to seize equality and obedient humanity. Neither its literary form nor its theology independently establishes a date in Christianity’s first few years. [Primary text: Philippians 2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2&version=NRSVUE).

Colossians 1:15–20 presents Christ through creation, reconciliation, and primacy among the dead. Its preexistence language is particularly pronounced, although interpreters debate the role of Jewish Wisdom traditions and how directly the passage anticipates later metaphysical doctrine. Colossians’ Pauline authorship is disputed, making dating more uncertain: a Pauline date in the 50s–60s and a later first-century composition represent different scholarly models. [Primary text: Colossians 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1&version=NRSVUE).

Calling either passage a pre-Pauline hymn combines separate hypotheses: that it is a hymn, that it existed independently, and that it predates Paul’s use of it. Peppard, and subsequently Edsall and Strawbridge, challenge the routine identification of such passages as inherited hymns. Their criticism does not prove that no traditional material exists. It means that stylistic elevation and modern indentation cannot establish an early source or its date.

## Angelic hospitality complicates simplistic tests of embodiment

Hebrews 13:2 encourages hospitality by invoking people who unknowingly entertained angels. Genesis supplies a natural background: Abraham’s visitors eat in 18:8, and the two explicitly identified angels eat Lot’s feast in 19:3. Genesis 18’s relationship between the visitors and the divine presence is narratively complex, so “three ordinary angels” is an oversimplification. [Primary text: Hebrews 13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+13&version=NRSVUE); [primary text: Genesis 18–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+18-19&version=NRSVUE).

These stories undermine the universal claim that eating necessarily demonstrates an ordinary human constitution. Within biblical narrative, heavenly visitors can appear human and participate in meals. They do not establish that Luke intended Jesus to be an angel: Luke specifically distinguishes him from the spirit the disciples fear they are seeing.

Mark 12:18–27 compares resurrected people with heavenly angels in a dispute about marriage. The immediate point is that earthly marital arrangements do not govern resurrected life. The passage does not state that people become angels or specify that they have no bodies. Thus angelic eating and angel-like resurrection are compatible literary motifs, but combining them into a complete resurrection physiology exceeds what either passage says. [Primary text: Mark 12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+12&version=NRSVUE).

## What the evidence changes

An early proclamation constrains a late-invention theory without deciding how every appearance should be understood. Angelic hospitality complicates a simplistic test of bodily presence without turning Jesus into an angel. The historical-Jesus passages belong in the separate [argument-development chapter](how-the-argument-developed.html). The central task is to compare actual claims rather than assume that “early,” “spiritual,” “bodily” or “hymn” settles the interpretation.

### Access limits

Precise dating claims for the reconstructed Philippians/Colossians material need their own explicit scholarly argument. The evidence above supports uncertainty, not a confidently dated first-generation creed. Broad letter-date ranges here are orientation, not newly established chronological findings.
