# Searching for the interpretation that changes the interpretation

## The purpose of the inquiry

The central idea is direct: “I'm searching for the Unorthodox Interpretation that changes the Orthodox Interpretation.” I want to know what the earliest sources say, how they change over time, what Jesus himself may have taught, and whether later writers and institutions transformed or corrupted that teaching. Texts outside the received Bible belong in that investigation because they may illuminate conflicts already visible inside it.

This is more than a request to make room for an alternative opinion. The aim is to find an interpretation strong enough to challenge a received reading and, where the evidence warrants it, change it. The ethical stakes are equally direct. I understand Jesus as a radical teacher of righteousness, justice, peace and healing. Recovering that message matters because of what it could help people do in this world now.

That aim supplies the question, not a shortcut to the answer. “Corruption” is a serious historical hypothesis: it asks what changed, who changed it, how it changed, and whether a recoverable earlier teaching was displaced. A difference between sources is the beginning of that inquiry. Demonstrating deliberate alteration, institutional selection or political accommodation requires further evidence. This wiki also collects perspectives that contradict one another. Inclusion is not a declaration that every interpretation is my settled belief.

## What counts as an unorthodox reading?

Here “orthodox” means an interpretation treated as authoritative within a particular Christian tradition. It does not mean only the Eastern Orthodox churches, and it does not imply that all Christians interpret every passage alike. Christian traditions disagree internally about scripture, authority, salvation, resurrection and historical method.

The methodological challenge arises when the conclusion is protected from revision in advance: a biblical passage is allowed to count only if it supports an already authorized interpretation. A critical reading asks whether the same evidentiary standards apply to passages that support the proposed reconstruction and those that challenge it. Otherwise an unorthodox interpretation can become just as closed as the orthodoxy it opposes.

Reading a Gospel critically does not require either accepting every sentence as a direct historical report or discarding the whole work. A text can preserve earlier memories, scriptural interpretation, literary invention and later theological framing together. Each claim needs an argument. Historical plausibility, literary dependence and a writer's interests can inform that argument; agreement with my preferred conclusion cannot do all the work.

James D. Tabor's [Paul and Jesus: How the Apostle Transformed Christianity](https://www.simonandschuster.net/books/Paul-and-Jesus/James-D-Tabor/9781439134986) (2012) and his work on Christian origins has been a major influence on this project. His emphasis on difference is a starting point for comparison rather than an authority that ends it. The [earlier resurrection chapter](../jesus-ethics-20261105/early-resurrection-accounts-and-source-criticism.html) already compares Tabor's reading with other accounts of Paul's transformed body. Strong disagreement among historical scholars belongs in the inquiry, including disagreement among scholars who do not write to defend church orthodoxy.

## Put the dates in separate columns, even when writing in prose

Five kinds of date must remain distinct: the event a narrative depicts; the possible origin of its traditions; the composition or revision of the work; the production of the surviving manuscript; and the modern discovery or publication of that manuscript. Collapsing those dates can make an apparent sequence look more certain than it is.

1. **Second Temple Jewish evidence:** the Dead Sea Scrolls are a diverse body of Jewish manuscripts, mainly from the third century BCE through the first century CE. They illuminate scriptures, interpretation, law, sectarian identity and eschatological expectation in the world from which Christianity emerged. They are not a lost Christian New Testament. The [Library of Congress overview](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/scrolls/intr.html) provides an accessible starting point.
2. **Paul before the surviving Gospels:** the undisputed letters date to the 50s and early 60s CE. First Corinthians 15 already transmits resurrection proclamation. A formula received by Paul can be earlier than the letter; its exact first wording and date remain separate questions.
3. **Gospel composition:** a conventional critical outline places Mark around 70 CE, Matthew and Luke commonly in the 80s–90s, and John toward the end of the first century. These are debated ranges, with wider proposals especially for Luke–Acts and the stages of John. They date surviving literary presentations, not the first possible existence of every idea they contain. Matthew and Luke's use of Mark also means that four accounts are not automatically four independent witnesses. The [Boston College teaching introduction](https://www.bc.edu/content/bc-web/schools/stm/sites/crossroads/resources/mini-course/death-of-jesus/introductory-materials.html) explains the Paul–Synoptics sequence. See the [existing source-critical chronology](../jesus-ethics-20261105/early-resurrection-accounts-and-source-criticism.html#begin-with-documents-not-a-reconstructed-film).
4. **Noncanonical Christian writings:** works such as Thomas and other writings preserved at Nag Hammadi require individual dating arguments. The generally fourth-century Coptic codices can contain translations of older compositions. A late copy may preserve early material; that possibility does not make all its contents early. The [Nag Hammadi introduction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/nag-hammadi-codices-and-their-ancient-readers/introduction/333084781B98EFDB225C0C6EC60D1F84) distinguishes the manuscripts from their reception and readers.

The comparison should record agreements as carefully as differences. Shared language can result from common scripture, a common social world, direct borrowing or later convergence. Similarity alone does not establish that one community directly transmitted its teaching to another.

## What did empire actually exclude?

The question about “texts rejected by the Empire” needs to be tested work by work. Around 180 CE, [Irenaeus defended a four-Gospel collection](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103311.htm), long before Constantine. His symbolic defense in Against Heresies 3.11.8 documents a boundary being asserted, not universal agreement throughout Christianity. [Eusebius, Church History 3.25](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm), distinguishes accepted, disputed and rejected works in the early fourth century. [Athanasius's Festal Letter 39](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/2806039.htm), from 367, gives the earliest surviving list matching the familiar twenty-seven-book New Testament exactly and distinguishes it from other instructive books.

There is no documented Nicaean vote in 325 that selected the New Testament canon. This does not make canon formation politically innocent. David Brakke's study connects Athanasius's boundaries with struggles over legitimate teaching and ecclesiastical authority in fourth-century Egypt. The precise question is how communities, bishops, institutions and imperial conditions affected which writings were preserved, read, authorized or condemned. A claim that a particular imperial order suppressed a particular work requires evidence for that order and its effects.

Nor should the discovery of the Nag Hammadi codices be turned into a confident story that Athanasius personally ordered those very books buried. Their ownership and deposition remain debated. Exclusion, loss, limited circulation and deliberate destruction are different historical processes. Identifying which occurred is part of recovering the conflict rather than assuming its mechanism.

## Revelation, discovery and the possibility of correction

The conviction that new insights can reopen inherited readings also has a theological dimension. Daniel 12:4 and 12:9 speak of sealing words until an appointed time; Revelation 20:12 depicts books opened in judgment. These passages can inspire reflection about delayed understanding. Their immediate literary settings do not establish that they predict modern archaeology, Nag Hammadi or this project. Treating present discoveries as revelatory is a theological interpretation to argue for, not a dateable archaeological finding. [Daniel 12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel+12&version=NRSVUE); [Revelation 20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+20&version=NRSVUE).

An interpretation becomes more persuasive when it explains both the evidence that prompted it and the evidence that resists it. For every proposed revision, the useful questions are: What passage changes meaning? Which earlier witness supports the change? What rival reading best explains the same evidence? What discovery or counterexample would require revision? The purpose remains ambitious: to recover a reading capable of changing inherited Christianity and helping people practice justice, peace and healing now.
