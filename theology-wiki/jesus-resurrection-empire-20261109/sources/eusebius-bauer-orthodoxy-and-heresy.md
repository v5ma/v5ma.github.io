# Eusebius and Walter Bauer: who gets to call the beginning orthodox?

Eusebius of Caesarea tells the history of Christianity as the continuing transmission of apostolic truth, threatened by teachers who distort it. Walter Bauer asks what happens when that claim is treated as a historical question rather than the starting assumption. Did the communities later called orthodox always come first, everywhere? Were the people later called heretics necessarily small offshoots of an already dominant church?

The decisive book was Bauer's *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity*, first published in German in **1934**, not 1930. It challenged the traditional account through regional investigation. It did not establish that every alternative was earlier, that every region followed the same sequence, or that institutional success alone explains every Christian belief. [1]

This page follows the contrast without replacing one simple origin story with its mirror image. It connects [[constantine-consolidation-and-christian-diversity|early Christian diversity and imperial consolidation]] with [[canon-coercion-and-nag-hammadi-survival|canon and textual survival]]. The historical question is how communities claimed continuity, disputed one another's legitimacy, and acquired unequal power to preserve their accounts.

## Eusebius: the first three centuries seen from the fourth

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, is conventionally called the “father of church history.” His ten-book *Ecclesiastical History*, also called *Church History*, is foundational because of its sustained narrative and its preservation of earlier documents and authors. Its subject reaches from Christianity's beginnings to Constantine's victory over Licinius in 324. “The first 300 years” is therefore a useful shorthand, not a precise boundary or a claim that Eusebius witnessed the whole period. The work developed in stages; the dating of its earlier editions is debated. [2]

Its opening announces the organizing principle: **succession from the apostles**. Eusebius will identify church leaders and teachers, preserve memories of persecution and martyrdom, and name the innovators whom he regards as corrupting Christian truth. He also acknowledges that the evidence is incomplete and that he is selecting material from scattered earlier accounts. This is an interpretive history, not a complete archive of every community. [3]

The distinction matters for reading him fairly. Eusebius is neither a neutral observer outside Christianity nor merely a source to discard because he has commitments. His quotations preserve evidence that would otherwise be lost. The same narrative that makes a case for continuity also gives later historians material with which to question that case.

## Apostolic continuity and heresy as corruption

The traditional model can be summarized as a sequence:

1. Jesus communicates true teaching to his apostles.
2. The apostles transmit it to churches and recognized successors.
3. Faithful teachers preserve that inheritance across generations.
4. Heretical teachers introduce deviations from an already existing truth.

In this model, orthodoxy is primary in authority and origin; heresy is derivative. It makes the legitimate church the continuous central subject of the story. But the theological assertion that the church preserves truth is not the same proposition as a demonstrated numerical majority in every place and decade.

Eusebius did not invent the model. In *Church History* 3.32.7–8 he reports **Hegesippus's** account of a church kept pure while apostolic witnesses remained, followed by increasingly open error after their passing. In 4.7.1–5 he interprets false teaching as demonic opposition and presents church writers as defenders of the apostolic inheritance. These are the sources' judgments about legitimate Christianity, not independent statistics about its population. [4][5]

There are earlier explicit arguments. Around the late second century, Irenaeus appeals to publicly transmitted teaching, episcopal successions, Rome and Polycarp in *Against Heresies* 3.3. Tertullian's *Prescription Against Heretics* 31–32 argues that truth precedes its corruption and challenges opponents to demonstrate apostolic succession and agreement in doctrine. He also allows that newer churches can share apostolic standing through agreement in teaching. Succession is thus an argument about recognized inheritance, not merely a list of officeholders. [6][7]

Read beside [[sacred-inheritance-and-rival-continuations|sacred inheritance and rival continuations]], this becomes a recurring historical problem: a community may sincerely claim that it alone preserves the founder, while its rivals make competing claims. Recording the claim does not yet settle the comparison.

## Eusebius's own evidence complicates a perfectly uniform church

Calling this an “orthodoxy first” narrative does not mean Eusebius depicts every detail as settled from the beginning. *Church History* 3.25 distinguishes accepted, disputed and rejected writings. James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2–3 John appear among the disputed books, and Revelation's status receives qualified treatment. The history itself preserves disagreement about the textual inheritance. [4]

Nor should its culmination in Constantine's success be described simply as a finished Nicene church proving it had always ruled. The history's concluding events concern 324; the work does not narrate the Council of Nicaea in 325. The relationship between Eusebius, imperial favor and later doctrinal definitions needs its own chronology. [2]

Two statements can therefore be true together: Eusebius constructs a continuous, legitimate church opposed by corruptions, and Eusebius preserves evidence of conflicts, uncertainties and changing boundaries. The question is not whether he mentions diversity, but how his narrative explains and ranks it. [[how-the-argument-developed|The developing argument about Jesus, resurrection and empire]] similarly separates the sequence of surviving witnesses from a later account of what all earlier witnesses supposedly meant.

## What Bauer changed in 1934

Bauer's German title was *Rechtgläubigkeit und Ketzerei im ältesten Christentum*. A second German edition appeared in 1964, edited and supplemented by Georg Strecker. The English translation, edited by Robert A. Kraft and Gerhard Kroedel with the Philadelphia Seminar on Christian Origins, appeared in **1971**. Those later supplements belong to the book's reception, not automatically to Bauer's original 1934 argument. [1]

His central challenge was methodological: a classification inherited from the eventual dominant church should not decide in advance which local Christianity was original. A group later condemned as heretical might have represented an early or locally predominant form of Christianity, rather than a late breakaway from an already established orthodox community. His opening strategy accordingly turns to Edessa and Egypt, where the New Testament does not supply the same foundation narrative available for some other regions. [1]

This changes the question from “How did outsiders depart from the original mainstream?” to “What communities were present, how did they relate, and when did a particular boundary become effective?” It does not require treating all claims as equally old or equally well supported. It requires investigating those matters rather than obtaining the answer from the label “heretical.”

## Four regional tests, not one worldwide reversal

### Edessa: later foundation stories and an uneven record

In chapter 1, Bauer questions the traditional apostolic-foundation account associated with Abgar and reconstructs the importance of Marcionite and Bardaisanite Christianity before the consolidation of the ecclesiastical tradition he calls orthodox. Later Manichaean activity also belongs to the region's contested history; it must not be projected backward as the founding form of first-century Christianity. [8]

The difficulty is the distance between the events and much of the surviving testimony. The *Chronicle of Edessa* supplies very little information about Christianity before the fourth century, while naming figures whom later church writers rejected. Its account of a flood in 201 nevertheless mentions a Christian church building: sparse evidence is not no evidence. That unevenness helped motivate Bauer's challenge. It is also a reason for caution: sparse documentation cannot by itself fix either the first arrival of Christianity or the size of each community. Edessa belongs to the Syriac-speaking world, in present-day southeastern Turkey; it should not be confused with modern Odessa. [9]

### Egypt: the earliest visible evidence is not a census

Bauer's Egyptian reconstruction emphasizes the thin early record for the later ecclesiastical succession and the prominence of teachers and writings subsequently classified as heterodox. He associates firmer ecclesiastical organization with Demetrius and his successors. Significantly, he also describes a period in which the categories were not yet clearly separated. [10]

The crucial inference is from **surviving literary visibility** to **historical priority or predominance**. A text's survival proves that the text existed; its circulation may establish something more. Neither fact automatically counts its adherents. Critics have also asked whether Bauer underweighted evidence for traditions later accepted as orthodox. The regional problem remains an investigation of particular witnesses, not a choice between a wholly orthodox Egypt and a wholly “Gnostic” one. [10][13]

### Asia Minor: contested authority, with important exceptions

Bauer reads Ignatius's demands for unity and obedience as evidence that authority was contested. An exhortation to agree with a bishop cannot simply be counted as proof that everyone already did so. Yet Bauer's own chapter 4 opening allows probable majorities aligned with Ignatius in **Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles and Philadelphia**. “Bauer proved heresy came first everywhere in Asia Minor” misstates his argument. [11]

Letters are interventions in particular disputes, and their rhetoric needs interpretation. A forceful warning can reveal a serious opponent without revealing either side's exact membership. This is one reason Thomas A. Robinson's later critique concentrates on the geographical reconstruction of Asia Minor. [16]

### Rome: an early center of influence, not a fourth-century invention

Rome occupies a different role in Bauer's account: an early center of the Christianity that would expand its authority beyond its locality. His discussion follows relations with other churches, the use of letters such as *1 Clement*, and material and persuasive influence. Even his account of that expansion includes limits and resistance, rather than instant universal control. [12]

This is not a theory that Constantine invented Christianity's succession claims, scriptures or institutions. The relevant connections and disputes predate imperial patronage. The later role of coercion belongs beside, rather than in place of, the earlier history of networks, argument and community formation. See [[constantine-consolidation-and-christian-diversity|the chronology of consolidation]] and [[competing-crosses-and-imperial-capture|the imperial-capture hypothesis]].

## Before Bauer, and after him: a continuing scholarly argument

It is too strong to say Eusebius's model went undisputed for 1,600 years and disappeared when one book arrived. Nineteenth-century historical criticism had already challenged simple accounts of unchanged transmission. Ferdinand Christian **Baur**, a different scholar from Walter **Bauer**, interpreted Christian doctrine through a historical model of development; Adolf von Harnack likewise investigated the historical formation of dogma. These were not identical to Bauer's region-by-region thesis, but they prevent 1934 from being treated as the invention of critical church history. [14]

Bauer's influence was also uneven and gradual. The reception appendix to the English edition records limited early impact in the English-speaking world and an expanding debate. Daniel J. Harrington's 1980 review documents that reception and warns against simply equating orthodoxy/heresy with majority/minority. The English translation of 1971 was an important stage, not the book's first publication. [13][15]

Several criticisms deserve to remain visible:

- **H. E. W. Turner:** *The Pattern of Christian Truth* (1954) proposes that continuity can involve relatively stable commitments expressed through flexible and developing theology. Acknowledging development need not mean that every community began without shared substance. [13]
- **Thomas A. Robinson:** *The Bauer Thesis Examined* (1988) challenges regional conclusions, especially for Asia Minor. An influential thesis can be tested at the level of geography and source interpretation rather than accepted as a package. [16]
- **Later work on categories and identity:** a survey by David W. Jorgensen describes continuing approaches to boundary-making, social relations and the language of “proto-orthodoxy,” alongside outright criticism of Bauer. The field is not adequately represented as unanimous acceptance or unanimous rejection. [17]
- **Critiques of “Gnosticism”:** Michael Allen Williams questions treating diverse materials as a single coherent religion defined by a fixed set of stereotypes. Recovering plurality on one side while flattening every alternative into one “Gnostic church” would reproduce the problem in reverse. [18]

“Proto-orthodox” can be a useful retrospective label for earlier positions ancestral to later orthodoxy. It becomes misleading when it implies that all those people already belonged to a uniform, fully organized party. Likewise, “heresy” can identify an ancient accusation without serving as the historian's verdict about religious truth. The analyst must say whose classification is being used, at what date, and for which community. [17]

## Nag Hammadi and the limits of what survives

The Nag Hammadi codices, discovered in 1945, widened access to writings previously unknown or known much less directly. They make comparison less dependent on opponents' descriptions. Their importance does not turn them into a demographic survey of second-century Egypt. The composition of a work, the copying of a surviving manuscript, its ownership and its eventual deposition are different historical questions. Recent research continues to debate the late-antique readers and settings of the collection. [19]

The discovery therefore strengthens the evidence for textual and theological diversity without automatically validating every regional claim in Bauer. Nor does it prove that Eusebius personally suppressed these particular books, or that all of them belonged to one persecuted organization. [[laughing-jesus-apocalypse-of-peter|The Coptic Apocalypse of Peter]] and [[gospel-of-truth-cross-and-knowledge|the Gospel of Truth]] should first be read as distinct works with distinct accounts of salvation and authority.

Source loss cuts both ways. Hostile quotation, selective copying, political pressure and ordinary material decay can distort what remains. The surviving archive cannot be assumed to represent all earlier voices in proportion to their numbers. But missing evidence cannot establish the exact teaching or popularity of a lost community either. A gap is a reason to qualify a reconstruction, not permission to supply whichever community the reconstruction needs.

Eusebius himself illustrates the double problem: his selections shape later memory, while his preservation of earlier writers makes much critical reconstruction possible. [[manuscripts-movements-and-survival|Manuscripts, movements and survival]] and [[canon-coercion-and-nag-hammadi-survival|canon, coercion and Nag Hammadi]] distinguish these mechanisms without reducing every loss to deliberate destruction.

## What this changes for the inquiry into Jesus

The choice is not between “the eventual winners preserved everything unchanged” and “the eventual losers preserved the one authentic teaching.” Institutional victory does not establish religious truth. Neither does defeat, marginality or rediscovery. Historical work can test dates, dependence, transmission, social relations and representations of opponents; those tests do not automatically settle a theological judgment.

For this wiki, Bauer's challenge makes the search for [[purpose-and-unorthodox-interpretation|an interpretation that changes the interpretation]] more demanding. A neglected witness deserves examination because of what it preserves, not merely because it was excluded. A canonical witness cannot be dated to the fourth century simply because a later institution promoted it. [[early-creeds-angels-and-resurrection|Early creeds and resurrection evidence]] keeps early attestation visible; [[ransom-revelation-and-defeated-rulers|ransom, revelation and defeated rulers]] keeps differences within and beyond the canon visible.

The useful questions are concrete: **Who claims continuity? What is the earliest recoverable evidence? Which boundaries are already visible, and which are being urged into existence? What changed when a community gained institutional or imperial power?** Each answer may vary by region, text and period.

Eusebius and Bauer remain worth reading together because their contrast exposes the difference between a community's account of its legitimacy and a historian's reconstruction of its formation. Recognizing that difference does not end the argument. It makes the argument possible without assuming its conclusion.
