# How the argument developed: Jesus, resurrection and empire

## Keep the full question in view

The inquiry cannot be reduced to a disagreement over what early Christians believed about resurrection. It includes what Jesus himself may have taught, what his earliest followers proclaimed, how Paul interpreted Jesus, how later Gospel writers narrated him, and how religious and political power affected the interpretations that survived. The central hypothesis is deliberately challenging: the historical Jesus may oppose forms of resurrection theology that displace or transform the teaching he lived and demanded.

That hypothesis raises several distinct questions. Did Jesus expect a general resurrection? Did he predict his own resurrection? What did Paul mean by a spiritual body? How do the Gospel appearance stories differ? What saving meaning did writers assign to Jesus's death? Does a later theology replace ethical transformation with assent to a claim about Jesus? Evidence about one question does not automatically settle the others.

This chapter traces the development of the argument rather than rewriting it as a simpler position. The [earlier chapter on whether Jesus would oppose the resurrection gospel](../jesus-ethics-20261105/would-jesus-oppose-the-resurrection-gospel.html) develops the ethical thought experiment. The [source-critical comparison](../jesus-ethics-20261105/early-resurrection-accounts-and-source-criticism.html) gives the fuller textual chronology. Here the focus is what each challenge forces the argument to explain.

## First movement: compare the surviving presentations

The opening observation is chronological. Paul's letters are earlier than the surviving canonical Gospels. Mark's earliest recoverable ending at 16:8 reports that Jesus has been raised and promises a meeting in Galilee, but does not narrate an appearance of the risen Jesus. Matthew includes encounters and the women grasping his feet. Luke emphasizes touch and eating; John presents wounds, locked doors and recognition. The later ending Mark 16:9–20 must be distinguished from 16:1–8. [Mark 16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+16&version=NRSVUE); [Matthew 28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+28&version=NRSVUE).

The proposed explanation is development: perhaps later writers gave an increasingly tangible narrative form to earlier revelation or heavenly transformation. That is a real historical question. It becomes weaker if recast as the claim that there was no resurrection belief before 70 CE. First Corinthians 15 directly contradicts that claim. It also becomes weaker if “spiritual body” is silently translated as “no body.” Ancient understandings of spirit and body do not map neatly onto a modern matter-versus-ghost distinction.

The narrower observation remains important: Paul’s earliest surviving extended account of resurrection appearances does not narrate the same scenes that later Gospels narrate. Competing explanations include remembered traditions preserved later, theological elaboration, literary construction, and different purposes or audiences. Composition order helps formulate the alternatives; it cannot alone choose between them.

## Second movement: ask what Jesus taught, not only what writers believed

Mark 9:30–32 portrays Jesus predicting his death and resurrection while the disciples do not understand. A harmonizing reading treats the prediction as evidence that Jesus himself authorized the later proclamation. Historical criticism asks an additional question: how much of the wording or timetable can be traced before the crucifixion, and how much reflects the narrator's post-Easter perspective? The disciples' misunderstanding has a literary function. Calling it a narrative device does not, by itself, demonstrate that the prediction was fabricated. [Mark 9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+9&version=NRSVUE).

John 12:27 presents Jesus troubled by the approach of his hour while affirming his purpose. Compared with the Gethsemane accounts, it helps show how authors shape a portrayal of Jesus facing death. It is neither a direct recording of his inner state nor evidence that he felt no distress. Historical reconstruction must weigh literary setting, inherited tradition, contextual plausibility and dependence rather than authenticating or rejecting a sentence merely because it is useful. [John 12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+12&version=NRSVUE).

Mark 12:18–27 introduces another distinction. Jesus' answer to the Sadducees supports resurrection and compares the raised with angels in a discussion of marriage. It challenges a simple continuation of earthly social arrangements. It does not explicitly define the material composition of resurrected bodies. The question about heavenly embodiment therefore remains open to the fuller [comparison with angel narratives](early-creeds-angels-and-resurrection.html#angelic-hospitality-complicates-simplistic-tests-of-embodiment).

## Third movement: early traditions make the challenge more demanding

First Corinthians 15:3–8, Philippians 2:6–11 and Colossians 1:15–20 complicate a theory in which resurrection or an exalted understanding of Christ began only with late Gospel writers. Paul's explicit statement that he received material is strong evidence for tradition earlier than the letter. The proposed hymn sources behind Philippians and Colossians involve additional, disputed literary judgments.

An early date matters because it constrains a reconstruction. It does not independently verify a miracle or prove that a later creed captures every original meaning. Nor does it remove the need to distinguish a document's date from an inferred source's date. The focused [creeds chapter](early-creeds-angels-and-resurrection.html) examines those claims passage by passage, including why precise claims such as “within months” need more than the label “pre-Pauline.”

Arguments from suffering or martyrdom require the same discipline. If someone endured death for a belief, that may support sincerity. It does not by itself identify the cause of an appearance experience or establish all later narrative details. Broad claims that every apostle could escape execution simply by denying a witnessed resurrection need individual historical evidence. No such universal history is established by 1 Corinthians 15 itself.

## Fourth movement: locate imperial pressure inside the Gospel narratives

The imperial question concerns Matthew, Mark, Luke and John themselves, not only a fourth-century church supported by Constantine. Communities under Roman power could frame Jesus and his followers as less threatening, distribute responsibility for his death, or reinterpret kingship and victory. A hypothesis of imperial accommodation therefore addresses the conditions of narrative composition and reception.

Pilate supplies a concrete test. Mark presents him satisfying a crowd; Matthew adds handwashing; Luke repeatedly emphasizes innocence; John dramatizes authority and loyalty to Caesar. Those patterns must be compared with Philo and Josephus, with literary dependence among the Gospels, and with readings in which a governor who knowingly condemns an innocent person indicts Roman power. The [Pilate chapter](imperial-pressure-and-pilate.html) distinguishes ideological influence from an unsupported claim of Roman state authorship.

## Ethics is central without becoming a straw man

The ethical concern is that a gospel about Jesus can eclipse the life Jesus taught. Mark 10:17–31, Mark 12:28–34 and the wider teaching on wealth, love and service make that concern substantive. But Mark also gives Jesus's death a saving meaning in 10:45 and 14:24. Those passages are necessary counterevidence to a claim that saving-death language enters only with later Gospel writers. [Mark 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+10&version=NRSVUE); [Mark 12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+12&version=NRSVUE); [Mark 14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+14&version=NRSVUE).

Likewise, a Christian can affirm faith and salvation while treating mercy and justice as essential consequences of loving God. An ethical critique should address the actual relationship being proposed between faith, conduct and salvation, rather than assume that every orthodox believer dismisses doing good. Conversely, objecting to the displacement of justice is not automatically an attempt to earn admission to heaven through moral bookkeeping.

The point of this inquiry is to recover an unorthodox reading capable of changing the inherited interpretation. Its strength lies in explaining the difficult texts as well as the attractive ones, and in showing precisely how a reconstruction changes what people are asked to believe and do.

## Sources and access notes

- This is an edited argument map, not a private-conversation transcript. It preserves the ambitious hypothesis while distinguishing observations, proposed explanations and unresolved questions. Alternative readings are analytical comparisons, not assertions that each is a settled personal belief.
- Primary passages are linked in their relevant sections. Gospel wording is evidence for each author's presentation before it is evidence for a verbatim saying of the historical Jesus.
