# Dan McClellan on texts, history, and the words of Jesus

## Attribution and scope

This page summarizes **Dan McClellan's arguments** in a supplied transcript of his response to an apologetic critique of his conversation with Joe Rogan. It is an editorial summary, not a text written or approved by McClellan, and it does not imply his endorsement of the Theology Wiki or its other hypotheses.

The source supplied for this study is a transcript received on November 14, 2026. That is the date of this editorial intake, not an independently established recording or publication date. The original response video's title, URL and the identity of the other speaker have not yet been independently verified. The transcript alternates the critic's recorded remarks with McClellan's replies; those voices must not be collapsed. The full transcript is not republished here. An original recording would permit checking transcription, context and timestamps.

**Central distinction:** preserving a text accurately is not the same as establishing that its account of an earlier event is historically accurate. McClellan applies that distinction to the saying about a sword and family division, and then to the broader methods used to reconstruct Jesus's teaching.

## 1. Textual reliability is not historical reliability

McClellan distinguishes two questions that the response he is criticizing treats as interchangeable:

- **Transmission:** Did later copyists add, omit or alter a sentence in Matthew?
- **Historical attribution:** Did Jesus himself say what Matthew places on his lips?

Agreement among manuscripts can support a conclusion about the wording of a Gospel. It does not by itself demonstrate the origin of that wording in Jesus's own speech. A composition may be transmitted faithfully while containing interpretation, literary invention or inherited traditions whose historical basis remains uncertain.

This is not an argument that manuscript evidence is useless. It identifies the question that such evidence answers. Equally, it does not follow that a saying absent from a contemporary record must be invented. Historical attribution requires an additional argument, not a shortcut in either direction.

The same distinction matters in the wiki's [Dead Sea Scrolls dating study](dead-sea-scrolls-dating.html): the age or preservation of a physical witness and the identification of the people or events it describes are different claims.

## 2. The worked example: a sword and a divided household

The discussion concerns Matthew 10:34–36 and the family-division material in Luke 12:51–53, with Micah 7:6 as an important scriptural comparison. Matthew uses the sword image; Luke's corresponding passage explicitly speaks of division and specifies opposing members of a household. Read [Luke 12:49–53 and its translation notes](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/12) alongside the other passages rather than assuming that the versions have identical wording.

McClellan's preferred explanation is that this tradition helped followers of Jesus understand conflict with their families: opposition could be presented as something their movement's founder had anticipated. He considers a post-Jesus setting for the saying more explanatory than direct attribution to Jesus. He also allows that the tradition could predate the surviving Gospels or contain different stages of development.

His argument is therefore not that a medieval copyist inserted the line. It is an argument about the formation and use of a saying before or during Gospel composition. Nor does he claim to have demonstrated that Jesus could not have said it.

**The strongest limitation:** a saying's usefulness to a later community does not establish that the community invented it. Communities also preserve, adapt and apply remembered teaching. A persuasive historical case must compare those possibilities rather than infer origin directly from later function. Recognizing this limitation preserves McClellan's methodological distinction without treating his preferred reconstruction as a proven event.

## 3. Biblical echoes do not identify the speaker

The critic appeals to the saying's connection with earlier scripture. McClellan replies that the availability of a scriptural theme to Jesus also makes it available to later transmitters and Gospel writers. An allusion can help explain a passage's imagery without deciding who first used it in this particular form.

That is a useful boundary for the wiki's [Dead Sea Scrolls and inherited Jewish concepts](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/dead-sea-scrolls-jewish-diversity-and-empire.html). Shared vocabulary may locate writings within a religious environment. It does not, without further evidence, establish direct contact, a single author or the identity of their protagonists. Compare also [clouds, a thousand years, and symbolic language](../clouds-and-millennium-20261109/clouds-and-millennium.html): a recognizable idiom does not settle every historical or theological question about its use.

## 4. Multiple witnesses may depend on one another

McClellan challenges the assumption that Matthew supplies an established eyewitness account and Luke independently confirms it through interviews. He points to literary relationships among the Gospels and asks what those relationships permit us to infer. If one author uses another, repetition is not automatically independent corroboration.

He applies a similar test to later Christian writers who repeat the saying. Such references can document its circulation and reception. They do not necessarily provide a separate chain back to Jesus. A proposed earlier source must itself be argued for; it cannot simply be assumed whenever a later quotation resembles a Gospel.

**Editorial qualification:** the transcript's claims about Gospel order, Luke's use of Matthew, and possible dependence on Josephus should not be flattened into a single scholarly consensus. Markan priority and the hypothesis that Luke used Matthew are distinct propositions. The Two-Source model proposes independent use of Mark and a hypothetical sayings source, Q; the Farrer model retains Markan priority while proposing Luke's use of Matthew. Mark Goodacre's [argument distinguishing Markan priority from the Two-Source theory](https://markgoodacre.org/Q/monopoly.htm) is a directly accessible example of the debate. It is an argument for a position, not a poll of all scholars. McClellan's later dating of Luke and interest in Josephus remain attributed positions in this summary.

This source-dependence problem connects with [Paul, James and contested authority](paul-james-and-contested-authority.html), which separates participant testimony, later narrative and subsequent reception, and with [early resurrection accounts and source criticism](../jesus-ethics-20261105/early-resurrection-accounts-and-source-criticism.html).

## 5. What can historians recover about Jesus's words?

McClellan stresses that readers encounter Jesus through mediated accounts, not a recording of his speech. In this response he takes a strongly skeptical position about identifying particular Gospel sayings confidently as Jesus's own words. That strong conclusion should be identified as his position rather than silently made the definition of historical research.

Three questions help avoid overstatement:

1. Can a historian recover the exact wording of a saying?
2. Can a historian assess whether it plausibly preserves the substance of an earlier teaching?
3. Can a historian explain how the surviving author shapes that teaching for a particular audience?

Confidence need not be identical across these questions. Uncertainty about verbatim words does not logically establish that nothing about a person's teaching can be reconstructed. Conversely, a plausible general portrait does not authenticate every individual speech. The wiki's [Pilate study](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/pilate-from-roman-prefect-to-christian-memory.html) makes a related distinction between evaluating literary portrayal and claiming access to a historical actor's private feelings.

## 6. Presuppositions, faith, and supernatural explanations

McClellan disputes the suggestion that critical scholarship is simply a project of unbelief or hostility to Christianity. He notes that religious scholars participate in critical historical work. A scholar's affiliation does not settle whether an argument succeeds.

He also says that supernatural explanations should be subjected to comparative evidential standards rather than granted an exemption. As summarized here, this is a demand for a justified inference, not a demonstration that supernatural events are impossible. The key question is what distinguishes the proposed explanation from competing accounts and why the same standard would or would not apply to another tradition's claim.

His larger methodological point is that evidence does not arrive with its interpretation already attached. Readers bring assumptions, values and expectations. The remedy is to make premises visible and revisable, not to claim that one side has no presuppositions at all. This standard applies to the Theology Wiki's preferred reconstructions as much as to apologetic defenses of traditional readings.

## 7. Historical inquiry is not a criminal trial

McClellan rejects an analogy that treats biblical accounts as defendants entitled to a verdict of reliability until disproved. The legal presumption of innocence serves a particular ethical and procedural purpose. It does not establish a historical proposition's probability.

A historian may conclude that an explanation is better supported, less supported or presently undecidable. An inability to disprove a claim is not the same as positive evidence for it. But lack of conclusive verification is not automatically proof of falsity either. The result can be a bounded judgment rather than total acceptance or rejection of a text.

## 8. Description is not endorsement

McClellan distinguishes what an ancient author may have meant from whether a modern reader should accept or imitate that position. He says his readings sometimes identify ideas in biblical texts that conflict with his own moral commitments. This challenges the assumption that a sympathetic interpretation must make every text agree with the interpreter's present values.

The wiki's [study of Revelation and symbolic violence](revelation-and-symbolic-language.html) applies a similar distinction: recognizing a literary symbol or an ancient community's perspective does not remove the ethical question about how its imagery is used now.

The transcript also contains pointed assessments of the critic's motives. Those are McClellan's rhetorical judgments, not facts about another person's mind established by this summary. A reader can evaluate the methodological disagreement without adopting those personal judgments.

## A practical method for this wiki

The following checklist is an editorial application of the discussion, not a quotation or a protocol authored by McClellan:

1. State the claim precisely: transmitted wording, historical event, speaker attribution, reception or modern application.
2. Identify what the witness actually establishes and its distance from the proposed event.
3. Check whether apparently separate witnesses share a source.
4. Compare preservation, adaptation and invention rather than treating one as the default conclusion.
5. Record the strongest alternative explanation and what evidence could change the assessment.
6. Distinguish interpretation from moral endorsement and disagreement from accusations about intent.

This develops the wiki's [evidence and editorial method](evidence-and-editorial-method.html). It also provides a check on discussions of [Eusebius, Bauer and competing origin stories](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/eusebius-bauer-orthodoxy-and-heresy.html): critical examination of one received narrative does not automatically verify its replacement.
