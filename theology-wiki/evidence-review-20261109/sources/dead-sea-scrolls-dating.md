# Dead Sea Scrolls Dating and the Teacher of Righteousness

## Evidence: manuscripts and identifiable passages

The Dead Sea Scrolls should be cited as individual manuscripts, not as a single chronological witness. **1QpHab**, the Habakkuk commentary from Qumran Cave 1, supplies a particularly important reference point for the Teacher of Righteousness. In **VII 4–5**, the commentator attributes understanding of prophetic mysteries to the Teacher. **V 9–12** describes a dispute involving the Teacher, the Man of the Lie, and the house of Absalom. **XI 4–8** depicts the Wicked Priest pursuing the Teacher and confronting his community on the Day of Atonement. These are specific textual claims, not an identification of their protagonists with named New Testament figures. The Hebrew passages are reproduced in Gary A. Rendsburg, “The Nature of Qumran Hebrew as Revealed through Pesher Habakkuk,” especially p. 140, in *Hebrew of the Late Second Temple Period* (2015), pp. 132–159. [Text and analysis](https://jewishstudies.rutgers.edu/images/documents/faculty/Rendsburg/pesher-habakkuk-std-114.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004299313_010).

Another relevant passage, **1QpHab VIII 1–3**, interprets Habakkuk 2:4 in relation to Torah-observant people and their fidelity to the Teacher. The resemblance to Christian uses of Habakkuk makes comparison worthwhile, but the interpretive setting must remain visible. Pong Dae Im’s study specifically examines the relationship between fidelity, suffering, and deliverance in this passage rather than treating it as an interchangeable statement of Pauline theology. [“An Understanding of the Salvation of the Righteous in 1QpHab vii.5–viii.3,” *Korean Journal of Old Testament Studies* 22/2 (2016), pp. 66–95](https://journal.kci.go.kr/ksots/archive/articleView?artiId=ART002118184).

## Evidence: what the 2025 dating study found

Mladen Popović and colleagues’ **“Dating ancient manuscripts using radiocarbon and AI-based writing style analysis,” *PLOS ONE* 20/6 (2025), e0323185**, describes Enoch, a handwriting-based dating model trained with radiocarbon evidence. [Research article](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0323185).

The study distinguishes two often-confused results. **4Q114 (4QDanielᶜ)** has an accepted calibrated radiocarbon range of **230–160 BCE**, overlapping the authors’ proposed historical setting for Daniel’s final chapters. **4Q109 (4QQoheletᵃ)** receives a **third-century BCE Enoch prediction**. The former is a radiocarbon result; the latter is a handwriting-model prediction. Neither identifies an autograph. [§2.1, §5; supporting references: Appendix S4 §4.1.2 and Appendix S7](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0323185).

## Interpretation: three dates, three questions

A disciplined reconstruction separates the date of the physical writing material, the date when a surviving manuscript was written, and the composition or revision of its text. The historical activity of a person mentioned in that text is a further question. Treating these as interchangeable silently inserts assumptions about copying, memory, and authorship.

Accordingly, an earlier date for a Daniel or Ecclesiastes manuscript does not independently date the Teacher. A manuscript preserving a narrative about the Teacher is more directly relevant, but even then its copying date does not automatically identify the time of every narrated event. Dating evidence constrains an interpretation; it does not supply missing names.

## Hypothesis: was the Teacher Jesus?

Identifying the Teacher with Jesus requires more than thematic resemblance. A defensible proposal must account for the relevant manuscript chronology, the Teacher’s interpretive and legal role, the opponents described in the sources, and the proposed connection to independently identifiable events.

Lawrence H. Schiffman cautions against assigning texts to the Teacher without explicit evidence. His discussion also notes that **Damascus Document XIX 35–XX 1** places the Teacher’s death before the anticipated arrival of a messianic figure. Schiffman interprets this as distinguishing the Teacher from that expected figure. This is an argument from a particular passage, not permission to flatten every text’s messianic expectations into one system. [*Reclaiming the Dead Sea Scrolls* (1994), “Teacher of Righteousness”](https://cojs.org/teacher_of_righteousness-_lawrence_h-_schiffman-_reclaiming_the_dead_sea_scrolls-_jewish_publication_society-_philadelphia_1994/).

Timothy H. Lim offers another historically grounded interpretation: the Teacher’s defining role is authoritative scriptural exposition, rather than being a deposed Hasmonean high priest. His reconstruction demonstrates why an argument should identify its scholarly advocate instead of presenting an unnamed “consensus.” [*The Earliest Commentary on the Prophecy of Habakkuk* (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198714118.001.0001).

## Counterarguments and limits

The authors call for further validation with more data and improved images. [§4](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0323185). Reviewers questioned the model’s contribution beyond radiocarbon and possible assessment bias, then accepted revisions. These are not an unresolved rejection. [Review history, January 23 and April 4, 2025](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/peerReview?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0323185).

The warranted conclusion is therefore limited: new dating evidence can revise manuscript chronology, while identifying the Teacher remains a separate historical argument. Neither model output nor an archived AI conversation establishes that the Teacher was Jesus.
