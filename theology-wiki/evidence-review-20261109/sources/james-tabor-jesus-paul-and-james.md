# James Tabor on Jesus, Paul, and the Letter of James

## The argument and its attribution

James D. Tabor argues that Paul's proclamation transformed the Jesus movement: the center of gravity shifted from Jesus's Jewish teaching about repentance, righteous conduct and God's coming kingdom toward salvation through the crucified and risen Christ. In that reconstruction, the Jerusalem movement associated with Jesus's brother James preserves an inheritance that cannot simply be equated with Paul's. The Letter of James matters because its practical, Jewish ethical instruction provides a surviving comparison within the New Testament itself.

This is an attributed account of Tabor's historical proposal, not a declaration that all historians accept it, and not a text written or approved by him. His book's subtitle is *How the Apostle Transformed Christianity*, not a claim that Paul invented every element of Christianity from nothing. Tabor explicitly makes that distinction in his [response to James D. G. Dunn's review](https://jamestabor.com/james-dunns-review-of-my-book-paul-and-jesus/).

Micah Blumberg supplied a long interview transcript for this study on November 14, 2026. Tabor's [May 28, 2025 introduction to an interview with Jacob Giron](https://jamestabor.com/a-young-christian-believer-asks-me-four-questions-on-paul/) is a strong provenance match: the speakers, questions and books correspond. Its linked [recording](https://youtu.be/BEHIsCRBE_M) has not been checked word for word against the supplied transcript. This page paraphrases the discussion rather than publishing the transcript or treating its speech-recognition errors as quotations. The interviewer's objections are not silently attributed to Tabor.

## Reading the New Testament in historical order

In the interview, Tabor urges readers not to treat the printed Gospel-first order as the order in which surviving Christian texts were written. Paul's undisputed letters precede the completed canonical Gospels. Consequently, an interpretation of Paul's statements cannot simply assume he was quoting a Gospel already familiar to us.

Tabor gives 1 Corinthians 11:23 as an example: Paul says that he received the meal tradition from the Lord. Tabor reads this in light of Paul's revelations. That is an interpretive argument, not proof that no oral tradition intervened. Receiving a tradition with the Lord as its ultimate authority and receiving it through a personal revelation are different proposed mechanisms. The wording alone does not settle between them.

This principle also cuts both ways. A Gospel written later may preserve earlier material. A Pauline text written earlier may already contain inherited tradition. Historical ordering is indispensable, but earliest surviving document, earliest underlying tradition and earliest event are not the same thing. Compare the [source-dependence discussion](dan-mcclellan-texts-history-and-method.html) and the [early creeds study](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/early-creeds-angels-and-resurrection.html).

## Why James is central to Tabor's reconstruction

James’s leadership is a separate part of Tabor’s case, not merely an inference from the letter’s ethics. Galatians 1:19 identifies James as the Lord’s brother, and 2:9 names him among the recognized pillars. Acts 15:13–21 portrays James delivering a judgment at the Jerusalem deliberations. These witnesses give a substantial basis for discussing his authority, while their different dates, genres and agendas prevent treating them as a single unmediated institutional record. Leadership by James does not by itself establish every proposed doctrinal difference from Paul.

The Letter of James offers a particularly direct way to test the proposed contrast. Its opening identifies the writer as a servant of God and Jesus Christ (1:1). Its teaching attacks favoritism toward the rich, demands practical help for people without food or clothing, warns against destructive speech and condemns withheld wages. These are not peripheral concerns: they organize its account of faithful life (James 2–3; 5:1–6).

James 2:14–26 places faith and action in deliberately sharp relation. Mere assertion of belief is inadequate when a neighbor remains hungry. Abraham and Rahab illustrate faith enacted. The passage explicitly rejects justification by faith alone, making comparison with Romans 3–4 a substantive question rather than an artificial controversy. The [existing Paul and James study](paul-james-and-contested-authority.html) examines disagreement with Paul, correction of a Pauline slogan, and different uses of the key terms as competing explanations.

The letter does not develop Paul's extended account of participation in Christ's death and resurrection, or John's Logos prologue and extended discourses about Jesus's identity. That difference of emphasis is real. But silence in a short ethical letter is not the same as a denial. James 1:1 and 2:1 still identify Jesus as Lord and Christ, and 2:1 associates him with glory. “James has no Christology” would therefore be an overstatement. Nor can the absence of an atonement exposition alone show that its author rejected every interpretation of Jesus's death.

In the supplied interview, Tabor identifies James and the sayings material commonly reconstructed as Q as especially useful mirrors of Jesus's ethical teaching. Q is a hypothetical source, not a recovered manuscript, and its reconstruction is contested. Agreements between James and sayings attributed to Jesus support comparison of traditions; they do not automatically demonstrate direct borrowing from a particular written Gospel or establish the letter's author and date. James's relation to Jesus's brother must be investigated independently rather than built into the conclusion.

## Difference does not mean moral opposites

An important qualification in the interview is easy to lose: Tabor says Paul's ethics are close to Jesus's. Love, generosity and moral conduct are not absent from Paul. Tabor's central contrast concerns the means and theological framing of salvation, not “Jesus taught goodness while Paul taught wrongdoing.” Galatians 5:6 and 5:13–14 belong in the comparison, not outside it.

Tabor also treats Paul as sincere. His argument is not that Paul knowingly fabricated every experience, or was a Roman agent sent to destroy Jesus's movement. A historical interpretation may dispute a person's authority without claiming to know that person's dishonest intent. This distinction matters for the Wiki's wider inquiry into [authority and inherited narratives](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/eusebius-bauer-orthodoxy-and-heresy.html).

The interview discusses Paul's expectation of imminent transformation as a way of understanding tensions in his social instructions. That proposal should not erase passages about women praying and prophesying (1 Corinthians 11:5), nor collapse disputed Pauline letters into the seven generally treated as undisputed. The attribution, textual history and setting of each instruction require their own analysis. Tabor's qualified treatment is more informative than a slogan that makes either Jesus or Paul an undifferentiated modern political position.

## What the strongest historical disagreement concerns

Tabor's published [overview](https://jamestabor.com/conflicting-theology-a-review-of-paul-and-jesus-parts-1-2/) presents the Jerusalem followers as Torah-observant Jews awaiting God's kingdom and honoring Jesus as teacher and Messiah, without the later saving-death and sacramental framework he associates especially with Paul. This is the strong form of his thesis. It must remain attributed rather than presented as a consensus description of every early Jerusalem believer.

A serious challenge comes from 1 Corinthians 15:3–11. Paul introduces material as received, names Cephas and James among the witnesses, and says that he and the others preach the message. The familiar scholarly reading identifies inherited material in verses 3–7, including death for sins. If that reading is right, it makes an absolute separation between an original movement without saving-death belief and Paul's invention of it difficult. It does not, however, establish agreement on every implication for Torah, Gentile inclusion, authority or the nature of resurrection.

Galatians 2 likewise records recognition and disagreement: shared mission and care for the poor, followed by a serious dispute at Antioch. The evidence can support a fractured, negotiated movement without requiring either perfect harmony or permanent total separation. Dunn's criticism and Tabor's response illustrate the dispute over how much weight to give each witness; neither should disappear from an attributed presentation.

Paul and John must also remain distinct. John's theology is not simply Paul's letters rewritten, and differences between James and John do not establish direct dependence of John on Paul. The [John study](john-qumran-baptist-and-signs.html) separates the Gospel's Jewish setting, proposed earlier sources and disputed polemical targets.

## Connections for the book and Wiki

Tabor's work strengthens the Wiki's central question about competing Christian inheritances: whose teaching is foregrounded when a later tradition describes its beginnings? The useful method is passage-level comparison, attentive to genre, chronology and reception. It can preserve the force of James's ethical challenge without requiring unsupported accusations against Paul.

Read this alongside [Paul, James and contested authority](paul-james-and-contested-authority.html), [why early Christianity spread](why-christianity-spread.html), [Dead Sea Scrolls and Jewish diversity](../jesus-resurrection-empire-20261109/dead-sea-scrolls-jewish-diversity-and-empire.html), and the [preserved discussion of James's succession](../san-reader.html?page=james-and-contested-succession).
