Revelation: symbolic resistance and the problem of violence

Revelation’s violent imagery deserves close examination, but its composition should not be confused with its later acceptance as scripture. Calling it something “the canonical church created” collapses two different historical questions: who composed the work, and how Christian communities subsequently received it. Eusebius’s Church History records disagreement over Revelation’s standing, placing it conditionally among accepted writings while acknowledging that others rejected it. This is evidence of contested reception, not a report that a unified ecclesiastical institution commissioned the book. Eusebius, Church History 3.25

What the sources establish

The writer identifies himself as John, but identifying that John with the apostle is another claim. Ancient Christians themselves disagreed. Dionysius of Alexandria, quoted by Eusebius, accepted Revelation as the work of an inspired John while questioning whether its author was the son of Zebedee who, in his understanding, wrote the Gospel and epistle. His comparison concerned language, style, and self-identification. That testimony demonstrates an ancient dispute; it does not settle modern authorship questions. Eusebius, Church History 7.25

Dating also requires qualification. The USCCB introduction addresses the book as an exhortation to first-century Christians. In a specifically dated scholarly intervention, Albert A. Bell Jr.’s 1978 article describes the last decade of that century as the majority position at the time and argues instead for the period following Nero’s death in 68. Its abstract establishes this minority proposal, not the validity of every step in its argument or the distribution of scholarly opinion today. USCCB introduction; Bell, “The Date of John’s Apocalypse,” abstract

The notorious bird banquet appears in Revelation 19:17–21. An angel summons birds to consume the flesh of kings, commanders, powerful people, horses and riders, and people across social ranks. The beast and false prophet are captured; the remaining opponents are killed by the rider’s mouth-sword. Birds then consume their flesh. These are not birds consuming Jesus’s followers as their promised heavenly reward. The scene depicts the defeat of the forces assembled against the rider. Revelation 19:17–21

Its scriptural background is especially important. Ezekiel 39:17–20 likewise summons birds and animals to a sacrificial feast on defeated warriors, princes, horses, and riders. Revelation reworks an existing prophetic image of enemy defeat rather than introducing an isolated fantasy. Recognizing that relationship explains its literary vocabulary without making its violence ethically harmless. Ezekiel 39:17–20

Interpretation and continuing disagreement

Apocalyptic symbolism is not merely decorative. It makes claims about power, allegiance, judgment, and hope. The USCCB introduction places Revelation within Jewish and Christian apocalyptic literature and interprets Babylon as Rome. On that reading, monstrous and extravagant images expose imperial power as something other than its own flattering self-description. This is a historical-literary interpretation, not evidence that every symbol has one uncontested equivalent. USCCB, introduction to Revelation

Symbolic interpretation also leaves a genuine ethical problem. Barbara Rossing argues that the self-giving Lamb should govern interpretation of the book’s violent images and that its followers are called to nonviolent resistance. She explicitly acknowledges competing readings that retain a tension between the Lamb and the warrior imagery. Her account therefore supports neither treating Revelation as a straightforward military instruction nor pretending scholars have dissolved its violence into an agreed benign meaning. Rossing, “Apocalyptic Violence and Politics”

The editorial judgment here is that context and criticism belong together. A symbolic sword is not an ordinary weapon, but a narrative celebrating enemies’ destruction can still shape moral imagination. Readers can recognize the text’s resistance to oppressive power while asking whether its vision of victory reproduces features of the domination it condemns.
