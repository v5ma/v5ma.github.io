The most productive question is not whether the earliest church was perfectly unified or completely divided. It is what people agreed about, what they fought about, and whose account of that struggle survives. Paul's own letters provide unusually early evidence of both cooperation and serious conflict. Reading them critically makes the wiki's inquiry into competing inheritances more precise, not less challenging.

Start with the witnesses, not the finished story

Distinguish four different objects of study:

  • Paul's account of his relationships: Galatians 1–2, a participant's argument for his own authority, not a neutral transcript.
  • Acts' narrative of apostolic agreement: Acts 15, a separate literary account, not an additional letter by Paul.
  • The teaching of the Letter of James: especially James 2, whose relationship to James the brother of Jesus and to Paul's writings must be argued, not assumed.
  • Later descriptions of Christians who rejected Paul: evidence for later disputes and remembered inheritances, not automatic proof of the positions held by Jesus or James.

These witnesses ask related questions, but they do not all speak from the same moment or with the same access. Use this distinction alongside the wiki's editorial method.

Galatians preserves agreement and conflict together

In Galatians 2:1–10, Paul says Titus was not compelled to undergo circumcision. He names James, Cephas and John as recognizing his mission and extending fellowship, with concern for the poor remaining a shared obligation. Then, in 2:11–14, he reports confronting Cephas at Antioch: after people arrived from James, Cephas withdrew from eating with Gentiles, and Barnabas joined that withdrawal.

This is substantial evidence against a picture of effortless apostolic consensus. It also resists a picture in which James and Paul were simply enemies from the outset. Both episodes occur in the same argumentative account. An interpretation that trusts Paul's report of conflict but discards his report of recognition needs to explain the different treatment.

The words “from James” matter, but do not supply James's own instructions. Paul gives neither James's reply nor Cephas's response to the rebuke. The extent of James's responsibility and the outcome of Antioch therefore remain questions, not recovered facts.

Eric Stewart's study of the passage emphasizes its rhetoric and warns that several familiar reconstructions import details from Acts. The kind of meal and the dispute's outcome are not securely known. His argument is useful precisely because it keeps Paul's judgment of his opponents separate from the historian's reconstruction.[1]

Compare Acts without automatically harmonizing it

Acts 15 depicts debate over Gentile circumcision, speeches by Peter and James, and a collective resolution imposing selected requirements rather than full conversion to Judaism. The chapter's letter also distances the Jerusalem leadership from unauthorized troubling of Gentile believers (15:24).

Read that account next to Galatians rather than substituting it for Galatians. They differ in narrative setting, emphasis and presentation of the agreement. Whether particular visits refer to the same meeting is itself an interpretive question. A later narrator's interest in agreement can shape a story without proving that every agreement was invented. Conversely, a narrative of reconciliation cannot erase the conflict a participant reports.

For the wiki's larger argument, this offers a concrete alternative to calling every difference “imperial editing”: compare how authority and unity are narrated before deciding what produced the differences. Literary reconciliation, community negotiation and political accommodation are distinct possible explanations. Imperial sponsorship is not established simply by discovering a disagreement.

Faith and works: a real tension, not an automatic transcript of a feud

James 2:14–26 attacks faith without action, illustrated by failing to provide food or clothing. Its statement that justification is not by faith alone deserves direct comparison with Paul's language about faith and works of law. But James does not name Paul in this passage.

There are at least three live interpretations: disagreement with Paul himself; resistance to a version of Pauline teaching; or different uses of faith, works and justification addressing different problems. Matthew J. Thomas's scholarly survey lays out several reconciliation proposals as well as the possibility of contradiction. Canonical coexistence is a reason some theological readers seek harmony, not historical proof that the authors meant the same thing.[2]

Paul's own Galatians 5:6, 13–14 makes love and service integral to his account of faith. That does not settle the dispute, but it rules out the shortcut “James taught ethical action; Paul thought conduct did not matter.” The sharper question is how each text relates conduct, belonging, Torah and God's judgment.

Authorship is another independent question. James 1:1 names James without identifying him as Jesus's brother. Eusebius himself reports that the letter was disputed, while noting its public use in many churches (Church History 2.23.25). This is valuable evidence for contested reception, not an effortless, universally fixed canon.[3] Nicholas List's recent study further challenges the common claim that Origen securely identified the writer as Jesus's brother; his argument turns on the distinction between surviving Greek and later Latin translation.[4] Neither observation alone proves pseudonymous authorship. Together they warn against treating the letter as an unquestionable direct response from the historical James to Paul.

Anti-Pauline Christians: later evidence needs its own date

Irenaeus reports in Against Heresies 1.26.2 that people he calls Ebionites rejected Paul as an apostate from the law. This is important evidence that acceptance of Paul was not universal. It is also a late-second-century opponent's description, not an Ebionite self-description or a document by James.[5]

A historical link between such groups and earlier Torah-observant Jesus followers is worth investigating. But a direct chain of succession, a uniform “Ebionite” theology, and the claim that their position preserves Jesus unchanged require additional evidence. Modern churches that criticize Paul do not acquire an ancient institutional lineage merely by sharing that criticism.

This distinction connects the Paul-versus-James research record and churches rejecting Paul to the broader Eusebius and Bauer study: diversity is demonstrable without assuming every later dissenting voice is the original movement.

What would change the assessment?

A claim of complete early harmony must account for Antioch without making Paul's public accusation meaningless. A claim of total, permanent separation must account for his report of recognition and explain why it should be discounted. A claim that James 2 directly refutes Paul needs an argument about authorship, date, verbal relationship and the target of the passage. A claim that later anti-Pauline groups preserve James unchanged needs evidence of transmission across the intervening generations.

These are historical tests of the explanation, not mathematical proofs. Missing sources may leave some questions unresolved. The aim is to let the evidence constrain both the inherited orthodox interpretation and the proposed alternative.
