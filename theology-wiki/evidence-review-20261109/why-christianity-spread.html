Why early Christianity spread: practices, networks, hope, and power

A chronological explanation that examines community before empire and coercion after imperial support.

Christianity’s expansion cannot be explained adequately as either the inevitable triumph of truth or the simple suppression of every rival by eventual winners. Those interpretations begin with the outcome. Historical inquiry instead asks what particular sources reveal about communities, commitments, and institutions at different times. The same explanation need not account equally well for first-century congregations, second-century conversion, and fourth-century imperial religious policy.

Evidence for communities before imperial dominance

Paul’s greetings in Romans 16 offer a concrete starting point. He commends Phoebe as a benefactor, names Prisca and Aquila as coworkers, and greets the congregation meeting in their house. Numerous other named associates appear. These details document relationships, hospitality, labor, and meeting places. They support an interpretation of Christianity spreading through interpersonal connections, but they do not provide conversion statistics or prove that every community operated identically. Romans 16:1–16

A different kind of evidence comes from Pliny’s correspondence with Trajan. Pliny reports Christians in towns, villages, and rural districts and describes interrogations, punishments, and recantations. Trajan tells him not to search for Christians proactively, while permitting punishment when accusations are substantiated and providing pardon for those who renounce the affiliation. This is evidence for Christian presence under an often hostile government, long before Christian emperors could favor the movement. Pliny’s alarmed account is not a neutral census, and its claims about numbers should not be treated as one. Pliny, Letters 10.96–97

Practices could also sustain communities. Justin Martyr describes Sunday readings, instruction, prayer, shared eucharistic participation, and voluntary contributions supporting orphans, widows, prisoners, strangers, and others in need. These activities joined religious teaching to material assistance. But Justin was defending Christians before an imperial audience. His account provides evidence of practices he presented as characteristic, not proof that every Christian consistently met the ideal. Justin, First Apology 67

Interpretation: belonging and hope

Such evidence makes community formation a serious explanation rather than an apologetic afterthought. In a published interview, historian Helmut Koester stresses the creation of new community as a reason for the movement’s success. That interpretation helps connect household networks and repeated gatherings with durable belonging. It remains an explanation to test against particular settings, not a universal formula for conversion. Koester, PBS FRONTLINE, “The Great Appeal”

Belief mattered alongside social organization. In 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18, Paul addresses grief by promising resurrection and lasting union with the Lord, then instructs believers to comfort one another. Whatever a reader’s judgment about that promise’s truth, the passage shows hope doing practical work within a community. It does not establish that fear of death or expectation of resurrection was every convert’s principal motive. 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18

Authority was present too. Justin’s presiding figure teaches and administers contributions; Paul’s letters instruct as well as encourage. These sources do not describe organization-free spirituality. A reasonable inference is that recognized leadership could help sustain teaching and cooperation, while also concentrating influence. The evidence supports investigating both functions rather than assuming authority was either wholly benevolent or merely coercive.

Later power changes the explanation

Fourth-century legislation preserved in the Theodosian Code documents a changed political environment: religious privileges, penalties against dissident groups, restrictions on sacrifice, and disabilities imposed on people leaving Christianity. Such measures belong in any account of Christian dominance. Legal prescriptions establish authorized discrimination and coercion; by themselves, they do not show consistent enforcement everywhere. Codex Theodosianus, selected religious laws

The stronger explanation is therefore plural and chronological. Networks, worship, assistance, hope, and leadership help explain how communities existed and persisted before imperial dominance. Patronage and coercion help explain later advantages and exclusions. Acknowledging the former does not excuse the latter; acknowledging the latter does not explain away the former.
