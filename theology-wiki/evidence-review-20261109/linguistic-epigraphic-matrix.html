What this matrix tests

A late compilation can incorporate early material. An early inscription can attest a name, formula or practice without establishing the date of an entire book. This matrix keeps those propositions separate. It is a selected evidence register, not a complete linguistic corpus or a new statistical dating model.

Each entry records an identifiable witness, the limited inference it supports, and an inference it cannot establish alone. Proposed literary dependence must also explain significant differences and plausible routes of transmission. Similar themes are a starting point for comparison, not a date stamp.

Israel in an Egyptian inscription

Witness: the Merneptah victory stela. UCL's Digital Egypt record reproduces the reference to Israel and links to the inscription and an early edition. Observation: Israel appears in an Egyptian text long before the proposed Hellenistic composition of biblical literature. Limit: the occurrence does not date Genesis, establish the complete Torah, or verify every biblical narrative. Political or ethnic identity and the compilation of a literary corpus are distinct questions. UCL, Israel stela.

Ketef Hinnom: a written blessing, not a whole Torah

Witnesses: the silver amulets KH1 and KH2. Barkay, Lundberg, Vaughn and Zuckerman's 2004 re-edition argues for a late preexilic context. The inscriptions attest written language also found in biblical texts. The Posen Library notes parallels with Numbers 6:24–26 and, in KH1's opening, Deuteronomy 7:9. Barkay et al., abstract, BASOR 334, pp. 41–71; Posen Library, entry commentary.

Supported inference: some wording later incorporated into the Pentateuch circulated in writing before the Hellenistic period. Not established: the makers copied a completed Pentateuch. Calling the evidence merely oral is also mistaken: the surviving objects are inscriptions. A shared traditional blessing is a possible relationship between texts, not an independently proven reconstruction of their transmission.

Elephantine: practice, authority and correspondence

Witness: TAD A4.7, also Cowley 30, Berlin P13495, the petition of 407 BCE concerning the YHW temple. The document describes the temple's history in lines 13–17, earlier unanswered correspondence with Jerusalem in lines 18–19, and the rebuilding request in lines 23–29. These locators allow the reader to test the account against an identified Aramaic document. K. C. Hanson, transliteration and translation.

Supported inference: this community practiced a form of Judaean religion that cannot simply be read off a later centralized norm. It was not wholly cut off from Jerusalem. Not established: that every Pentateuchal text existed, or that none existed anywhere. A text's existence, circulation, authority and observance are four different questions. The petition reports silence from Jerusalem's high priest, not his authorization of the rebuilding.

Hebrew language: competing chronological models

Hendel and Joosten argue for combining historical linguistics, textual criticism and cultural history. Their model distinguishes archaic, classical, transitional and late Hebrew while allowing the transmitted text to contain multiple layers. Authors' account, Yale University Press, 2019.

Young, Rezetko and Ehrensvärd challenge the inference from linguistic variation to a reliable date of original composition. They emphasize overlapping styles, the accumulation criterion used to identify late features, and changes during scribal transmission. They do not deny that Hebrew changed historically. Authors' account, Bible and Interpretation, 2010, §§1–5.

Required record for a lexical argument: the exact Hebrew or Aramaic form, passage, manuscript/edition, proposed earlier and later comparanda, genre, distribution, and whether a variant reading changes the conclusion. A generic statement that a text “sounds early” or contains “Aramaisms” is insufficient. Script, language, dialect, register and composition date must not be collapsed into a single category.

Greek influence: what an objection actually shows

Stéphanie Anthonioz values Gmirkin's legal comparisons while questioning the proposed Alexandrian production setting and the absence of Greek linguistic influence in Hebrew. That is a concrete challenge to investigate, not a demonstrated rule that borrowing ideas necessarily imports a fixed quantity of loanwords. Anthonioz's 2018 review.

Johann Cook distinguishes potential influence on Hebrew authors from interpretive freedom in Greek translation and finds the Alexandrian scenario insufficiently supported. Cook, “Platonism and the Bible(s),” 2022, criticism and conclusion.

A translation's Hebraisms can indicate a Hebrew source without telling us how many centuries that source preceded its translation. Conversely, thematic similarities do not establish direct copying, a specific workshop or a single composition date. Each inference needs its own evidence. The matrix therefore does not supply a blanket verdict for or against every Hellenistic editorial hypothesis.

What remains to be done

The next useful increment is a reproducible passage-level lexical dataset with named editions and independent review. No such comprehensive dataset is claimed here. The manuscript-dating study illustrates the same discipline for radiocarbon, handwriting and historical identity. The editorial standard specifies how claims and corrections are recorded.
