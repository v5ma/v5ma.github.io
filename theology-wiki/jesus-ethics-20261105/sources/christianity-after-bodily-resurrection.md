# Christianity After Bodily Resurrection? Faith, Formation, and Possible Futures

## The question that remains after the premise is examined

What could Christianity become if many Christians no longer believed that Jesus was bodily raised from death? What would remain essential: an account of God's action, a way of life, an inward relationship to Christ, a community, or some combination of these?

My opening question joined this inquiry to two further suggestions: that resurrection belief was added after Jesus's original followers, and that a vast majority of Christians would eventually recognize this within the next hundred years. Those suggestions require separate examination. A worthwhile question about Christianity's future should not depend on presenting a disputed historical reconstruction or an unmeasured future majority as an established fact.

The [[early-resurrection-accounts-and-source-criticism|companion chapter on early resurrection accounts]] examines the textual evidence. Here the narrower historical starting point is that Paul already presents resurrection as a received proclamation and associates appearances with Cephas, the Twelve, James, and others. This is evidence of early resurrection belief, not independent verification of every reported appearance or the miracle itself. It also prevents us from treating resurrection as simply absent until later Gospel storytelling. [1 Corinthians 15:3–11](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15)

The constructive question survives that correction. A person can question a miracle without claiming that its first believers never believed it. A community can reinterpret its inheritance while acknowledging that its interpretation differs from earlier ones.

The further possibility is larger than a Christianity with one belief removed. Historical criticism can open Christianity toward a wider religious inheritance: the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Nag Hammadi library, writings commonly grouped as Gnostic, Mesopotamian traditions, Judaism, Kabbalah, and the other traditions already studied in this wiki. I want to examine that unification as a positive constructive project, including the historical relationships that could support it and the theological choices it would require.

## Four changes that should not be collapsed into one

Rejecting **resuscitation** means rejecting a return to ordinary mortal life. That is not necessarily a rejection of traditional resurrection. The Catholic Catechism distinguishes Jesus's glorified bodily life from the temporary restorations associated with Lazarus and others. The Orthodox Church in America's exposition likewise rejects the picture of a corpse simply restarting its previous biological existence while affirming Christ's actual resurrection. [Catholic Catechism, §§645–647](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_two/article_5/paragraph_2_on_the_third_day_he_rose_from_the_dead.html); [OCA, “Resurrection”](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/the-symbol-of-faith/resurrection)

Rejecting **bodily resurrection** goes further: it denies, or declines to affirm, that the dead Jesus himself entered a transformed embodied life. Someone taking that position might still believe that Jesus lives with God or is encountered through the Spirit. That is different from maintaining only that his influence continues.

Interpreting resurrection **symbolically** can also mean different things. The symbol might express a real divine vindication, a transformative encounter, liberation from fear, or the renewal of a community. Calling these symbolic does not tell us whether their interpreter believes in God, an afterlife, or the continuing personal reality of Jesus.

Finally, **removing resurrection altogether** would abandon its language and theological function, not merely change its explanation. These are several possible revisions, with different consequences. A question about one should not quietly become an answer about all of them.

Paul's phrase “spiritual body” belongs within this discussion, but it is not a convenient synonym for a surviving idea. His argument concerns the dead, transformation, and a kind of body. Disagreement about how to understand that body should remain visible rather than being settled by modern associations with the word spiritual. [1 Corinthians 15:35–54](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15)

## Why traditional Christianity cannot simply subtract Easter

For the traditions represented by the Nicene Creed, Jesus's resurrection and the future resurrection of the dead belong to the account of salvation itself. The Church of England's Articles of Religion make bodily continuity explicit in Article IV. These documents describe a doctrinal commitment, not the measured beliefs of every person belonging to their churches. [Nicene Creed](https://www.churchofengland.org/faith/what-we-believe/nicene-creed); [Articles of Religion, IV](https://www.churchofengland.org/liturgy/book-of-common-prayer/articles-religion)

The objection to a purely symbolic replacement is therefore substantial. If God has not overcome death for Jesus and will not overcome it for the dead, a promise has changed. An account of moral influence does not by itself restore a murdered person or establish that death is not final. A traditional Christian can value ethical transformation and still regard that difference as decisive.

This concern should not be reduced to attachment to a spectacular anomaly. The OCA's treatment of eternal life connects resurrection with the goodness of bodies and material creation. Its hope concerns persons and a renewed world, rather than escape into a disembodied existence. [OCA, “Eternal Life”](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/the-symbol-of-faith/eternal-life)

Nor does recognizing symbolic meaning require abandoning the event. Romans 6 connects baptism, participation in Christ's death and resurrection, and a changed life in the present. The ethical and participatory meaning is already there within a resurrection-affirming theology. The modern choice is not automatically between believing that something happened and allowing it to change how one lives. [Romans 6:1–14](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/6)

## Four precedents, with different projects

### Jefferson: a selected life and moral teaching

Thomas Jefferson's *The Life and Morals of Jesus of Nazareth*, assembled around 1820, extracts and arranges Gospel passages while excluding miraculous material. It ends with Jesus's burial rather than resurrection appearances. His 1803 letter to Benjamin Rush also claims a Christian identity grounded in attachment to Jesus's teaching. [Library of Congress, “Jefferson's Opinion of Jesus” and “The Jefferson Bible”](https://loc.gov/exhibits/religion/rel06-2.html#obj160)

This is a clear precedent for retaining Jesus as a moral teacher while setting aside the resurrection narrative. It is not evidence that the earliest Christians had already followed Jefferson's method. Nor should Jefferson be made into a straightforward atheist: Monticello's account identifies his belief in a benevolent creator and notes uncertainty and development in some of his other views. [Monticello, “Jefferson's Religious Beliefs”](https://www.monticello.org/encyclopedia/jeffersons-religious-beliefs)

His example demonstrates the possibility of a selective reading. It does not, by itself, supply a model for sustaining a church, its worship, or its obligations across generations.

### Tolstoy: a demanding religious way of life

In his own preface to *The Gospel in Brief*, Leo Tolstoy expressly lists Christ's resurrection among the passages omitted from his exposition. He locates the teaching's center in divine life, the present, love, and relationship to the Father. His argument is not simply that Christianity becomes whatever ethical preferences a modern reader already holds. [Tolstoy, *The Gospel in Brief*, preface](https://ccel.org/ccel/tolstoy/gospel/gospel.ii.html)

In *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*, nonresistance to evil by force becomes central to his criticism of established Christianity and organized coercion. Tolstoy offers a radical and contestable account of Christian discipleship, not an easy removal of inconvenient beliefs while ordinary life continues unchanged. [Tolstoy, *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*, author's preface and chapter contents](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/43302/43302-h/43302-h.htm)

His example raises a difficult question for any ethical reconstruction: what demands would remain when the miracle is no longer its organizing claim? Would the revision change conduct, or only make inherited language easier to accept?

### Crossan: resurrection retained as vindication and transformation

John Dominic Crossan explicitly told Terry Gross in 2004 that he did not believe a physical transformation had happened to Jesus's body. Yet he also insisted that crucifixion and resurrection belong together: Roman execution is opposed by God's vindication of the one executed. He understands this through metaphor, justice, and participation in changing the world, rather than treating metaphor as a synonym for something inconsequential. [Crossan, *Fresh Air* interview, April 1, 2004](https://freshairarchive.org/segments/historical-look-crucifixion)

Crossan is therefore a poor example of someone who simply removes resurrection. He relocates its meaning. His historical reconstruction of the first followers' experiences and intentions remains an argument to assess, not something established merely because he is a scholar. The distinction matters: evidence that Crossan holds a view is not proof that ancient Christians held precisely that view.

### Borg: a living sacred presence, not only remembered advice

Marcus Borg's 2011 discussion resists both a narrowly physical account and the dismissal of spiritual experience as unreal. He centers Easter on Jesus being experienced as present and as Lord. In 2012 he explicitly affirms resurrection's importance while arguing that its meaning does not depend on a transformation of Jesus's corpse. [Borg, “The Resurrection of Jesus”](https://marcusjborg.org/posts-by-marcus/the-resurrection-of-jesus/); [Borg, “Reflections on Easter”](https://marcusjborg.org/posts-by-marcus/reflections-on-easter/)

That position differs from reducing Jesus to an admired dead teacher. It also differs from the creedal accounts described above. The category liberal Christianity is too broad to erase these distinctions. It should not stand for a single doctrine, a demographic forecast, or the assumption that every politically progressive Christian rejects bodily resurrection.

These four figures provide precedents and competing proposals. They are not successive stages through which all Christians must pass.

## What could remain essential?

There is no neutral definition of Christian essence that all these positions accept. Three questions help prevent an answer from claiming more than it has established.

**What remains historically identifiable as Christian?** A community might retain its relation to Jesus, its scriptures, prayers, stories, calendar, rituals, and inherited institutions. That establishes continuity of some kinds. It does not automatically establish doctrinal continuity on every question.

**What counts as Christian according to a particular church?** Its answer may include commitments that another community reinterprets or rejects. A new synthesis cannot settle that boundary by declaring that only its preferred elements were ever essential. Conversely, describing a position as outside a creed does not make it historically unrelated to Christianity or render its adherents' lives meaningless.

**What is worth carrying forward in a constructive theology?** This is where the wiki's inquiry can make a proposal rather than impersonate a unanimous tradition. One possible center is a continuing discipline of attention, mercy, truthfulness, repentance, and repair, oriented by Jesus's life and teaching. Luke 6 joins love of enemies and mercy to the demand that hearing become action. Matthew 25 links the treatment of vulnerable people to the judgment of the Son of Man. Those passages supply resources for the proposal, although their own theological settings exceed a modern ethical summary. [Luke 6:27–49](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/6); [Matthew 25:31–46](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/25)

The proposal becomes more specifically Christian when Jesus and the received tradition remain capable of correcting the community, rather than serving only as decoration for conclusions reached elsewhere. That does not make mercy the property of Christians. Other traditions and nonreligious people can cultivate it. Christian identity would concern a particular history, language, relationship, and practice of formation, not a monopoly on goodness.

The [[christ-as-an-inner-model|Christic Self-Formation Theory]] developed elsewhere in this wiki provides one possible connection. An inward model of Christ can help someone notice that a preferred action contradicts the example they claim to follow. The present extension is that this formative relationship could continue for a person who no longer affirms bodily resurrection. Whether such practice actually changes conduct is an empirical and practical question. Its effectiveness would neither prove an ancient miracle nor disprove one.

## Historical criticism can enlarge the inheritance

The important shift is from asking which traditions must be excluded to asking how particular texts, practices, and ideas became related. Canon remains a fact about a community's authority, but it need not become a prohibition on comparison. A Christian reader can study a text excluded from a Christian canon without first declaring it either worthless or a suppressed account of what every early Christian really believed.

My constructive proposal is to bring these inheritances into a shared inquiry about divine reality, knowledge, authority, suffering, and transformation. This is more ambitious than collecting similar sayings. It asks whether traditions can illuminate one another's unresolved problems and contribute to a more adequate theology. Historical criticism supplies evidence and corrections; it does not, by itself, prescribe a unified religion or require every reader to reject resurrection.

### Shared settings, transmission, and surviving collections

The **Dead Sea Scrolls** reconnect Christian inquiry with a varied Jewish world rather than a single background doctrine. The Qumran manuscripts generally date from the third century BCE to the first century CE and include scriptural copies, interpretation, hymns, regulations, and other religious writings. They help locate questions about covenant, holiness, revelation, and expectation within Second Temple Judaism. That historical setting is a substantial relationship even where a direct Qumran-to-Christian institutional line remains unproved. [Israel Antiquities Authority, introduction to the Scrolls](https://www.deadseascrolls.org.il/learn-about-the-scrolls/introduction?locale=en_US)

The wiki's strongest reconstruction goes further. The [[jesus-teacher-of-righteousness-hypothesis|Earlier-Founder Jesus-Teacher Identity Hypothesis]] proposes that the Teacher of Righteousness and the figure remembered as Jesus originate in one earlier historical individual. His teaching and community survive across generations, while a later biography relocates the founder into a first-century setting. The [[tor-thomas-and-gnostic-transmission|Teacher–Thomas transmission inquiry]] develops proposed carriers through succession, displacement, and Egyptian connections. This personal-identity and relocated-biography hypothesis differs from the weaker alternatives of influence on a distinct later Jesus or participation in a shared Jewish setting. It is a testable conjecture with substantial chronological and evidential demands, not an established history. Findings favoring transmission would not automatically establish the stronger identity claim.

Some connections are more specific than a shared setting. The **Book of Giants** provides a concrete example of literary transmission and adaptation: Jewish Aramaic fragments at Qumran contain figures associated with Mesopotamian Gilgamesh traditions, and the Jewish work supplied a major source for Mani's later *Book of Giants*. Werner Sundermann identifies correspondences of names and narrative material, alongside subsequent transformations. This is evidence of a crossing between traditions, not a demonstration that their communities, cosmologies, or entire scriptures were identical. The surviving records are fragmentary. [Sundermann, “Giants, the Book of,” *Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/giants-the-book-of/)

The **Nag Hammadi collection** offers another kind of connection. Its surviving Coptic codices are fourth-century manuscripts, often preserving translations of earlier works. They require attention both to composition and to later collection and readership. [Paul Linjamaa, *The Nag Hammadi Codices and their Ancient Readers*, introduction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/nag-hammadi-codices-and-their-ancient-readers/introduction/333084781B98EFDB225C0C6EC60D1F84)

Codex VI actually preserves three Hermetic treatises, including the *Discourse on the Eighth and the Ninth* and the *Prayer of Thanksgiving*. Their presence gives a material basis for investigating overlapping spiritual and literary worlds. It is stronger evidence of contact within a collection than a resemblance noticed by a modern reader, though it does not establish a single doctrine shared by every work. [Lautaro Roig Lanzillotta, study of the Hermetic texts in Codex VI](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-discourse-on-the-eighth-and-the-ninth-nhc-vi6-the-prayer-of-t/)

The wiki's [Qumran overview](/theology-wiki/comparative-religion/abrahamic-inheritance/groups/qumran.html) and [Hermetic-texts entry](/theology-wiki/comparative-religion/abrahamic-inheritance/groups/hermetic.html) provide routes into those comparisons. A library, a religious movement, and a modern category such as Gnosticism should not be treated as interchangeable names. The developed [[melchizedek-priesthood-and-transmission|Melchizedek comparison]] follows one figure through Genesis, Psalm 110, 11QMelchizedek, Hebrews, and Nag Hammadi, asking how priestly authority changes across those settings.

### Older myths and later mystical interpretations

The Sumerian comparison reaches much further back. The Met describes the long preservation of Mesopotamian literary traditions, including myths from the late third and second millennia BCE, by later temple scribes. [The Met, tablet and literary-history description](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/321910?locale=en)

In the Oxford translation of *Inana's Descent*, Inana is killed, restored to life, and required to supply a substitute when leaving the underworld. The [[inanna-death-and-resurrection|earlier Inanna inquiry]] can therefore become a detailed comparison of death, restoration, divine power, and cost. The differences are informative: Inana's circumstances and the consequences of her return are not the Gospel narratives. A proposed line of borrowing requires evidence beyond the shared pattern of death and renewed life. The developed [[flood-inheritance-and-deep-time|flood-inheritance study]] supplies a further route through Mesopotamian and biblical narrative transformations, separating dates of works, surviving manuscripts, narrated events, and physical history. [*Inana's Descent*, especially lines 273–289, ETCSL](https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section1/tr141.htm)

**Kabbalah** enters at another chronological point. Eitan Fishbane describes its formation in late-twelfth-century Provence and its further development in thirteenth-century France and Spain, drawing on earlier Jewish inheritances. Medieval systems cannot simply be placed in the mouths of Jesus or the Qumran writers. They can nevertheless become later interpretive partners in questions of divine manifestation, relation, and transformation. The developing language of the sefirot articulates differentiation within one divine reality; it should not be treated as a direct equivalent of the Christian Trinity. The [wiki's Kabbalah entry](/theology-wiki/comparative-religion/abrahamic-inheritance/groups/kabbalah.html) keeps that inquiry within Jewish tradition rather than treating Kabbalah as Christianity's missing component. [Fishbane, “The Origins of Kabbalah in Medieval Europe”](https://www.jtsa.edu/torah/exploring-kabbalah-episode-3/)

These relationships have different shapes: a shared historical setting, demonstrable literary adaptation, physical co-preservation, a proposed genealogy, and a later constructive comparison. Keeping their shapes visible allows more connections to be investigated, rather than requiring every connection to carry the same claim.

### What the proposed unification would do

The resulting theology could understand Christianity as one historically situated participation in a larger religious inquiry. Christ could remain a central model of formation while the inherited understanding of Christ is expanded and corrected through encounters with other texts and communities. The [[cognitive-gnosticism|Cognitive Gnosticism]] inquiry could compare how understanding transforms the knower; the [[sacred-inheritance-and-rival-continuations|sacred-inheritance study]] could examine how authority is received and contested; the repair framework could ask what these interpretations enable people to do together.

This is a proposal for reciprocal interpretation. A Jewish reader need not accept a Christian account of fulfillment to enter the conversation. A Kabbalistic account of divine life need not be reduced to psychology. A modern psychological reading of an ancient myth can be useful while remaining distinguishable from the myth's earlier ritual and theological meanings. Participants must retain the ability to say that a proposed translation loses something essential.

The enlarged archive also complicates the word resurrectionless. The Nag Hammadi *Treatise on Resurrection* offers a spiritual and intellectual account of resurrection, with transformation potentially experienced in this life; it does not simply discard resurrection as an embarrassing addition. Its alternative can challenge a narrowly bodily reading without becoming a modern secular theory. [Melissa Harl Sellew, introduction to *Treatise on Resurrection*, NHC I,4](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-edition-of-early-christian-writings/treatise-on-resurrection/C245F9F9E8FB60E3FD863203FC7EC5AA)

Unification could therefore transform the meaning of resurrection as well as question it. Its achievement would be a more connected account of human religious life, with room for shared understanding and genuine disagreement. Whether that account should remain Christ-centered, become interreligious, or develop another identity is part of the constructive choice. Historical connections make the project richer; they do not make that choice on everyone's behalf.

## A changed doctrine changes worship and grief

Consider an imagined congregation whose members disagree about Easter. One expects bodily resurrection and reunion with the dead. Another believes in divine presence but is uncertain about personal survival. A third understands resurrection as the recovery of hope and ethical purpose. They may share a meal, visit someone who is ill, and work to repair an injustice. Their common practice can be real even when their explanations differ.

The disagreement becomes harder at a funeral. Saying that someone's influence continues is not equivalent to promising that this person will live again. If the congregation uses the same words for both claims without explaining the difference, apparent unity can depend on people hearing incompatible promises. A responsible symbolic theology should state what it affirms, what it hopes, and what it does not know.

Ritual would also need positive content. Baptism could mark a transformed way of life; communion could cultivate remembrance, gratitude, fellowship, and obligation to others. These are proposed reinterpretations, not complete descriptions of what sacramental traditions already mean. Familiar forms alone do not explain why a community should continue gathering or what it expects its practices to accomplish.

For a nonliteral reconstruction, this is a test of substance. Does its language sustain care when repair is incomplete, when someone dies, or when the wrong cannot be undone? Hope need not become a false guarantee, but it must be more than a demand that the wounded feel optimistic.

## The next hundred years are not already known

The demographic evidence does not establish the future majority imagined in the opening question. Pew Research Center's 2025 report estimates that the Christian population grew in number between 2010 and 2020 while declining as a share of the world population. Its Christian-population chapter reports that sub-Saharan Africa overtook Europe as the region with the largest Christian population. These are historical estimates of affiliation and population change, not measurements of a worldwide rejection of bodily resurrection. [Pew, global religious change](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/how-the-global-religious-landscape-changed-from-2010-to-2020/); [Pew, Christian population change](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/christian-population-change/)

We should not turn developments in one region into Christianity's universal destination. Nor should we assign a single theology to an entire continent from its population growth. A credible forecast of resurrection beliefs would need comparable questions across countries, languages, denominations, and generations. It would have to distinguish denial, uncertainty, transformed embodiment, and rejection of mere resuscitation. This chapter supplies no such century-long forecast.

Several conditional futures can nevertheless be considered:

- **Continuing plurality.** Creedal, symbolic, and uncertain interpretations could coexist, sometimes within the same churches. The central challenge would be truthful communication across the differences.
- **Institutional separation.** Some communities could make symbolic interpretation explicit while others strengthen bodily resurrection as a boundary of membership or teaching. The result could be several more clearly differentiated Christian movements.
- **Ethical or contemplative reconstruction.** Some communities could organize around Jesus-oriented formation, shared practice, and service while revising supernatural claims. Their durability would depend on what they build and transmit, not simply what they stop affirming.
- **Cross-tradition synthesis.** Communities could bring Christian, Jewish, Gnostic, Hermetic, and other inheritances into a shared framework. Some might retain a Christian identity; others might become explicitly interreligious. Their test would include how truthfully and reciprocally they receive the traditions they use.
- **Disaffiliation.** Some people could conclude that removing resurrection changes the religion too fundamentally and leave Christian identification altogether. Others might remain culturally attached without participating in a church.

These are scenarios, not ranked predictions or exhaustive alternatives. They could occur together in different places. No percentage or timetable is assigned to them.

## A future without erasing the past

The deepest question is not how little Christianity can retain and still keep its name. It is what kind of relationship to truth, persons, and inherited meaning a community can sustain when a central belief is reconsidered.

The [[apocalyptic-repair-theology|repair framework]] suggests a practical direction: preserve the capacity to admit error, protect vulnerable people, reconsider authority, and act where harm can be reduced. A symbolic account can be judged by those commitments without pretending that it reproduces every claim of the first Christians. A traditional account can share those commitments without surrendering its hope that God actually raises the dead.

Christianity could continue in forms that do not affirm bodily resurrection. Historical precedents already make that possibility intelligible. Whether those forms preserve Christianity's essential truth is a theological disagreement; whether they become dominant is an unanswered empirical question. The constructive task is to make the proposed life clear enough to examine, including the hope it offers and the promises it can no longer honestly make.

## Provenance and interpretive boundaries

This chapter develops Micah Blumberg's newly supplied question of November 5, 2026, and his subsequent proposal that historical criticism can lead toward unification with the Dead Sea Scrolls, Nag Hammadi, Sumerian traditions, Judaism, Kabbalah, and other religions studied in the wiki. The question and proposal are treated as invitations to historical and theological inquiry, not as evidence that their historical premises or predicted outcomes are already established. Prior AI answers supplied with the question are not independent authorities.
