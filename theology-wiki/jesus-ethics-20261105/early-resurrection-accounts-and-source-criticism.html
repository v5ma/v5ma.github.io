The question behind the sequence

My starting question is whether the resurrection stories changed as Christianity developed. Paul speaks about a spiritual body. Mark, in its earliest recoverable ending, does not narrate an encounter with the risen Jesus. Matthew, Luke and John tell more extensive appearance stories, including bodily contact and, in Luke, eating. What happened between these accounts? Did later Christians give an increasingly tangible form to an earlier experience of revelation?

The original proposal in Paul and the Resurrection was stronger: an early spiritual resurrection was subsequently replaced by a resurrection of the flesh. Biblical Resurrection Interpretations presses the objection that simply calling both accounts “embodied” can hide an important difference. That objection deserves a serious comparison. “Body” is not a sufficient explanation if the texts imagine different kinds of body.

The comparison must also allow the evidence to complicate the proposal. A later surviving story does not necessarily describe a later-invented belief. A spiritual body does not necessarily mean an immaterial soul. An account of what Paul believed does not establish what happened to Jesus. These distinctions make it possible to investigate development without assuming either perfect agreement or a predetermined sequence of fabrication.

This chapter studies the historical and textual question. Christianity After Bodily Resurrection? considers the separate theological question of what faith and Christian formation might become when people cannot affirm bodily resurrection literally.

Begin with documents, not a reconstructed film

First Corinthians belongs to the 50s CE. It is earlier than the surviving canonical Gospels and is a letter addressing particular communities and disputes. The USCCB introduction places it around 56 and identifies its Pauline authorship as secure. Its age makes it indispensable, but a letter is not an exhaustive account of everything its author or audience knew. Introduction to 1 Corinthians.

A conventional critical chronology places Mark around 70, Matthew and Luke commonly in the 80s or 90s, and John toward the end of the first century. The exact dates, especially the dating of Luke–Acts and stages of John, remain debated. These are approximate composition dates, not dates printed on autographs. Nor are they the dates of the oldest surviving manuscript copies. The introductions to Matthew, Luke and John explain this conventional framework; Dale Martin's discussion of Mark illustrates disagreement over its precise relation to the war and the destruction of the Temple.

Markan priority is the widely accepted judgment that Matthew and Luke used Mark, not a claim that Mark is the earliest Christian document or that every tradition it contains originated with its author. Arguments concern patterns of wording, sequence, omissions and editorial changes. The relationship between Matthew and Luke remains disputed: the Two-Source hypothesis uses Mark and a reconstructed sayings source, Q; the Farrer hypothesis has Luke use Matthew as well as Mark. Accepting Markan priority does not require accepting Q. Mark Goodacre on Markan priority; Stephen C. Carlson's survey of the Synoptic Problem.

This chronology supports comparing earlier and later presentations. It does not establish that nobody described a bodily resurrection for fifty years, that all possible witnesses were dead when Matthew was written, or that Mark simply turned Paul's letters into a biography. Those are additional claims requiring additional evidence.

What Paul actually supplies

In 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, Paul passes on a sequence: Christ died, was buried, was raised and appeared to named individuals and groups, ending with Paul himself. He presents material he had received, so the letter's date is later than at least some of its tradition. Scholars debate the precise boundaries, provenance and age of that inherited material. The passage does not identify the occasion on which Paul received every element. 1 Corinthians 15:3–11 and its textual notes.

Paul does not supply the Gospel scene of women finding Joseph of Arimathea's tomb empty. He does not describe wounds being inspected, a meal with the risen Jesus, or the sensory details of the appearances in his list. The five hundred are reported by Paul; we do not possess five hundred independent accounts. An early report and independently corroborated testimony are different kinds of evidence.

Yet Paul gives resurrection more than the meaning that a teacher remains influential. His argument is that if Christ has not been raised, his proclamation fails and the dead are lost. Christ's resurrection begins the future defeat of death. The second half of the chapter asks what kind of body the dead will have, contrasting perishability with imperishability and ordinary life with spiritual life. The seed analogy gives interpreters material for both continuity and radical difference. 1 Corinthians 15.

Paul calls his gospel a revelation in Galatians 1 and asks whether he has seen Jesus in 1 Corinthians 9:1. These are his own statements, distinct from the later narratives of his encounter in Acts. “Visionary” can describe a revelatory experience, but should not quietly become either a medical diagnosis or a complete explanation of what every earlier disciple experienced. Putting the appearances in one list does not establish identical circumstances. Galatians 1; 1 Corinthians 9.

A spiritual body is a disputed concept, not a shortcut

The central expression is sōma pneumatikon, conventionally “spiritual body,” contrasted with sōma psychikon, often translated “natural body.” The difficulty is not solved by importing the modern opposition between solid matter and an invisible, immaterial mind. The question is what Paul meant by these descriptions in his own intellectual and religious setting.

Tabor: radical difference from ordinary flesh

James Tabor emphasizes that resurrection need not mean the revival of a corpse into its previous condition. His discussion challenges the assumption that the later Gospel scenes of wounds and touch define every early Jewish or Christian use of resurrection language. He reads Paul's future body as a different, heavenly mode of existence. Crucially, he explicitly rejects the inference that this means a disembodied surviving soul. Tabor, “Resurrection of the Dead: Old Body or New Body?”.

Tabor therefore supports taking the contrast between earthly and heavenly embodiment seriously. Citing him as though he simply says “Paul believed in a ghost” would misrepresent his argument. His broader judgments about how dominant particular resurrection models were remain scholarly interpretations, not conclusions established merely by the English adjective “spiritual.”

Ehrman: the same body transformed

Bart Ehrman makes a different emphasis explicit. In the publicly accessible opening of “A Gnostic View of Jesus' Resurrection,” he reads Paul as believing that Jesus' corpse itself was transformed into an immortal spiritual body. He distinguishes this from later views in which the body remains dead while the spirit survives. In a follow-up comment, he clarifies that his interpretation of Paul does not imply that Paul knew the Gospel story about women discovering a particular tomb. Ehrman's post and clarification.

That is an interpretation of Paul's belief, not Ehrman's affirmation that the miracle occurred. It is also evidence that a skeptical modern historian need not read Paul's “spiritual body” as the abandonment of bodily resurrection. The two debates cannot be mapped onto a simple division between believing and unbelieving scholars.

Martin and Wright: a real disagreement about the language

Dale Martin places Paul's language against ancient ideas in which pneuma could be a kind of substance. In his Yale lecture, the resurrection body is radically different from ordinary flesh and blood, but still substantial. This challenges the assumption that ancient spiritual existence must mean modern immateriality. Martin, “Paul as Pastor”.

N. T. Wright instead emphasizes what animates the body: psychikon and pneumatikon distinguish ordinary mortal life from life empowered by God's Spirit. He resists translating the contrast as physical versus nonphysical. His argument also draws on Romans 8:11, where divine Spirit gives life to mortal bodies. Wright, “Mind, Spirit, Soul and Body”; Romans 8.

These readings should not be collapsed into one consensus about the mechanics of transformation. What they jointly expose is the inadequacy of a two-box choice between an unchanged human corpse walking again and an entirely disembodied mind. My original distinction remains worth investigating, but the alternatives must be described more carefully than “spiritual versus literal.” A writer can believe literally in a transformed spiritual body.

The contrast between “Greek Paul” and “Hebrew bodily resurrection” is also too sharp. Daniel 12 combines awakening from the dust with radiance like the stars. Jewish resurrection imagery already allowed transformation; Greek vocabulary did not make Paul cease to be a Jewish interpreter of scripture. A genealogy of ideas needs specific texts rather than two uniform civilizations. Daniel 12:1–3.

Paul's conflicts do not establish total rejection

The archived proposal also connects Paul's revelation with rejection by the Jerusalem apostles. Galatians gives a more complicated picture. Paul claims independence, describes visiting Cephas and James, reports recognition of his mission by James, Cephas and John, and recounts a confrontation with Cephas over table fellowship. His account is interested and argumentative, but it is evidence for both cooperation and conflict. Galatians 1–2.

The claim that these apostles nicknamed Paul “Simon Magus” is not supplied by these letters. Identifying Paul behind a later literary opponent requires its own argument from the dates, sources and aims of those texts. It cannot be inserted into Paul's biography as an established event. Likewise, an exact interval of seven years between Jesus' death and Paul's revelation is not established by the passages discussed here.

Mark's endings: three questions that must stay separate

What does Mark 16:1–8 say?

The women visit the tomb, encounter a young man, hear that Jesus has been raised and are directed toward a future meeting in Galilee. They flee in fear. Jesus does not appear as a character in this surviving scene, but resurrection is explicitly announced. “Mark contains no resurrection” is therefore inaccurate. “The earliest recoverable ending contains no narrated appearance of the risen Jesus” identifies the actual difference. Mark 16:1–8.

What was added during transmission?

The fourth-century Greek codices Vaticanus and Sinaiticus end at 16:8. Other witnesses transmit further material. The familiar Longer Ending is 16:9–20. The technical term “Shorter Ending” usually names a separate brief addition after 16:8, not merely the Gospel ending at verse 8. There is also an expansion within the Longer Ending known as the Freer Logion. The USCCB text and notes display the different endings.

H. A. G. Houghton's recent study describes the editorial consensus that the earliest attainable text ends at 16:8. It reassesses two medieval catena manuscripts, GA 304, often cited in earlier discussions, and GA 239, not previously prominent in them. Neither should count as evidence for the ending represented by Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. Their physical and commentary histories matter, not just where the surviving text stops. This is a useful example of criticism revising its own evidence rather than merely counting manuscripts. Houghton, “Unfinished Business,” New Testament Studies 69 (2023), 35–42; accessible article PDF.

The Longer Ending is nevertheless ancient. Irenaeus cites its ascension statement as Mark's conclusion around the late second century. Its presence in most later Greek copies is real evidence of reception, though numerical predominance among surviving copies does not by itself determine the earliest wording. Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.10.5; James Kelhoffer's study of the early evidence.

Did the author intend the abrupt ending?

Recovering an early text and recovering an author's intention are different tasks. Some interpreters see 16:8 as a deliberate literary ending that confronts the reader with fear, failed response and an unfulfilled promise. Others propose that an original conclusion was lost. A lost ending remains a hypothesis; we do not possess it. Establishing that 16:9–20 is secondary does not automatically decide between those possibilities. Martin's discussion of Mark's ending.

Canonical authority is another question. A church may receive the Longer Ending as scripture while acknowledging its distinct composition history. The Catholic notes linked above do exactly that. Historical authorship, earliest recoverable wording and a community's judgment about its canon should not be treated as interchangeable verdicts.

What becomes more tangible in the later Gospels?

The differences are visible without constructing a single harmonized account. In Matthew 28, women grasp Jesus' feet, and the disciples encounter him in Galilee. Matthew also includes a dispute about the removal of the body. These details are absent from Paul's appearance list and Mark 16:1–8. Matthew 28.

Luke 24 gives the contrast unusual force: the disciples fear that they see a spirit; Jesus points to his flesh and bones, invites touch and eats fish. Yet the same chapter has him vanish after recognition at Emmaus. Luke's story combines bodily continuity with extraordinary capacities. Calling it an ordinary resuscitation would overlook part of what the text narrates. Luke 24:13–43.

Paul's “flesh and blood can't inherit God's Kingdom” in 1 Corinthians 15:50 and Luke's “flesh and bones” in 24:39 deserve direct comparison. Paul is discussing the transition from perishable existence to imperishable resurrection life. Luke is narrating Jesus' response to disciples who mistake him for a spirit: the bodily language establishes his tangible identity. A continuity reading interprets Paul's phrase as mortal humanity in its present condition and Luke's as embodied identity after transformation. A development reading emphasizes how much more explicitly Luke insists on palpable flesh. The different contexts help explain the language, but they do not make the difference disappear or establish that the writers imagined precisely the same thing. Nor does the change from “blood” to “bones” supply an anatomical account of the risen body. 1 Corinthians 15:35–54; Luke 24:36–43.

John 20 likewise identifies the risen Jesus through wounds while describing his presence among disciples behind locked doors. Thomas is invited to examine the wounds; the narrative does not explicitly say that he actually touches them. The episode culminates in belief, including the position of later readers who have not seen. John 20.

The doubt and failures of recognition are themselves part of the question. Matthew 28:17 places worship beside doubt. Luke 24:16 says the travelers' eyes are kept from recognizing Jesus, while 24:37–43 moves from fear of a spirit through bodily demonstration to astonished disbelief. John 20:14–16 has Mary mistake Jesus for the gardener until he addresses her by name; 20:24–29 gives Thomas an explicit demand for evidence. These are distinct literary depictions of recognition, uncertainty and persuasion, not a uniform report of effortless identification. They may invite historical questions about the experiences behind the stories, but they do not independently establish those experiences' causes. Doubt is neither automatic proof that the encounters were invented nor automatic proof that they happened because an author included an awkward detail. Matthew 28; Luke 24; John 20.

These scenes support the observation that later surviving narratives make tangible identity much more explicit. It is reasonable to investigate apologetic purposes, remembered traditions and literary development. It is a further inference to say that their authors invented all such material specifically to overturn Paul's doctrine. The texts do not announce that project. Luke's opposition to mistaking Jesus for a spirit is direct evidence; an exact reconstruction of the opponents or sources behind it is less secure.

Nor does the date of a Gospel settle the age of each tradition within it. A later author can preserve older material, elaborate it, combine it with scripture, or introduce new scenes. Source criticism must make the case passage by passage. “Later” is an important observation, but it cannot do all that explanatory work alone.

The virgin birth: a parallel question, with different evidence

Matthew 1 and Luke 1 explicitly attribute Jesus' conception to divine action without sexual relations. Their infancy narratives differ in presentation: Matthew's announcement is to Joseph in a dream, while Luke's is to Mary through Gabriel. The shared claim deserves attention, but the two narratives should first be read separately rather than combined into a familiar Christmas sequence. Matthew 1:18–25; Luke 1:26–38.

Paul does not explicitly teach a virginal conception. Galatians 4:4 says that God's Son was born of a woman and under the law. That wording supplies neither a virgin-birth story nor an explicit rejection of one. Mark begins with Jesus' adult ministry; John's prologue speaks of preexistence and incarnation rather than narrating Mary's conception. Absence from these different kinds of text cannot be converted into a precise date when the belief was invented. Galatians 4; Mark's structure; John's prologue and composition.

Matthew's use of Isaiah 7:14 is especially important. In its earlier setting the oracle addresses Ahaz and a contemporary political crisis. Hebrew ‘almah denotes a young woman without itself specifying virginity; the Greek rendering with parthenos provides the wording Matthew applies to Jesus. Distinguishing the earlier context from Matthew's reading makes the interpretive development visible. It does not, by itself, establish whether scripture generated the conception story, was used to interpret an existing tradition, or participated in a more complicated process. Isaiah 7 and the notes on 7:14.

The strongest defensible comparison is therefore between different levels of attestation and different literary aims. Matthew and Luke give explicit conception accounts where the earlier surviving sources do not. The further claim that the accounts were consciously fabricated requires an argument beyond their lateness. Equally, their presence in scripture is not independent historical verification of the event they affirm.

People noticed problems before modern criticism

The manuscript problem at Mark's ending was not discovered only after the Enlightenment. The ad Marinum, transmitted under Eusebius' name, considers both setting aside the disputed ending and harmonizing the apparent difference with Matthew. Kelhoffer stresses its apologetic purpose and the complexity of its testimony. It demonstrates ancient awareness of variants and discrepancies; it should not be turned into a modern census of every manuscript then in existence. Kelhoffer, “The Witness of Eusebius' ad Marinum,” ZNW 92 (2001), 78–112.

Modern historical criticism expanded the inquiry into language, authorship, sources and social setting. Spinoza's Theological-Political Treatise (1670) argued for interpreting scripture through its language and historical circumstances rather than treating later doctrinal assumptions as the key to every passage. He was part of a longer interpretive history, not the first human being to notice textual difficulty. Steven Nadler's account of Spinoza and scripture.

Reimarus's posthumously published arguments in the eighteenth century distinguished Jesus' aims from those attributed to his followers. Strauss's Life of Jesus (1835–36) made mythical formation central to Gospel interpretation. In this usage, myth did not have to mean a deliberate lie: a community could express religious conviction through stories shaped by inherited scripture. Their particular reconstructions are not binding results of using critical methods. Fernando Bermejo-Rubio's historical survey.

This history matters because “critical” is often mistaken for a predetermined rejection of religion, just as “believing” is mistaken for an inability to recognize variation. The actual arguments require examination. An interpreter's religious position does not replace evidence for a reading.

A method that does not assume its conclusion

For this inquiry, the tasks can be kept distinct:

  • Textual criticism: What wording is best supported by manuscripts, translations and early quotations? Mark 16 is the clearest example here.
  • Source criticism: What earlier written or oral material did an author use? Matthew's use of Mark is a different question from Paul's understanding of resurrection.
  • Form and tradition criticism: How might individual sayings and stories have circulated before their surviving written settings? Reconstructing an earlier stage requires an argument; it does not recover a missing document by assertion.
  • Redaction criticism: How does an author select, arrange and change material? Added emphasis can be identified more confidently than every motive behind it.
  • Historical reconstruction: What probably happened, given the nature, date, dependence and limitations of the evidence?
  • Reception history and theology: How did communities read these texts, and what authority or meaning do they assign to them now?

Multiple documents do not automatically equal multiple independent witnesses. A theological purpose does not automatically make every detail false. An earlier source is not automatically exhaustive. Silence matters most when there is good reason to expect the author to mention the missing information. A reconstruction should also identify what evidence would count against it, rather than explaining every possible result as confirmation.

Historical investigation can establish that resurrection was proclaimed early and can compare what its advocates meant. Whether a supernatural act is the best explanation requires further judgments about historical inference and the possibility of miracles. Simply recovering an early creed does not settle that issue. Conversely, showing literary development does not amount to a demonstration that no extraordinary event occurred.

The same discipline can open a wider comparison involving the Dead Sea Scrolls, Nag Hammadi writings, Sumerian texts, Judaism and Kabbalah. Each comparison still needs its own dates, contexts and evidence of transmission; a resemblance alone does not establish shared origins. A contemporary cross-tradition synthesis can be explored as a constructive project without presenting it as a recovered ancient consensus. The companion chapter takes up that broader possibility.

What remains of the original proposal?

The evidence preserves the question's force while limiting its strongest formulation.

  1. Early resurrection proclamation is securely attested in Paul. Its wording cannot be reduced to a later Gospel scene, but neither does it merely say that Jesus' teachings survived.
  2. Paul's resurrection body differs radically from ordinary mortal existence. The precise relation between old and new embodiment remains debated. “Spiritual” alone does not establish disembodiment.
  3. Mark's earliest recoverable ending announces resurrection without narrating an appearance. Its textual history is real, ancient and independently worth studying.
  4. Later Gospel accounts elaborate tangible encounters. The development of literary presentation is observable; a single proven transition from universally nonbodily belief to invented bodily belief is not.
  5. The virgin-conception narratives have a different pattern of attestation. Their absence from earlier surviving texts is relevant, but does not locate their origin with certainty.

The resulting inquiry is still substantial: how did communities transmit, interpret and give narrative form to claims about Jesus after his death? Keeping that question open is more productive than requiring every text to mean the same thing, or requiring every difference to prove the whole reconstruction in advance.
