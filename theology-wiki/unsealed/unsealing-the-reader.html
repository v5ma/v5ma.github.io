INWARD FORMATION / OPEN EVIDENCE

Unsealing the reader

A recovered text can remain unread in the very mind that possesses it.

Micah Blumberg / Authorial working study / September 9, 2026

Possession is not reception

A manuscript can be photographed, translated and available on a screen while a reader encounters only confirmation of what they expected. The words are accessible, but their capacity to change an interpretation remains closed. This is the second unsealing I propose investigating: not an event in the history of a container, but a change in what a person can notice, understand and do.

The image of consuming a scroll in the prophetic texts offers a comparison between receiving a message and becoming responsible for it. My cognitive interpretation is a later use of that image. It does not identify reading with a specific neural signature, prove the divine source of an experience, or make unfamiliar information automatically wise.

Within Christic Self-Formation, an inward exemplar matters when it participates in a developing choice. A received representation can interrupt an impulse, make another response available, and change what the person prepares to do next time. Recovering a neglected witness adds a possibility at another level: the representation of the exemplar may itself need revision.

The second correction

Consider a reader who interprets fidelity as defending every claim made by a trusted teacher. An unfamiliar text reveals that an opponent's position was more thoughtful than the teacher's summary allowed. The first temptation is to collect enough quotations to restore the old confidence. A different response is to investigate the whole argument, correct the summary, and distinguish loyalty to truth from protection of a reputation.

That response changes more than one historical opinion. It changes what fidelity authorizes. The reader might still belong to the same community, cherish the same scripture, and regard the new text as noncanonical. Yet the inherited model can no longer use belonging as a reason to falsify another person's position.

The reciprocal test is essential. A recovered text may contain an appealing criticism of authority and an exclusion the reader had overlooked. Discovery should not excuse the new favorite from the scrutiny applied to the old one. Otherwise, an apparently radical change simply installs another infallible interpreter.

These examples express a proposed formative mechanism, not results from a completed experiment. A study could examine how readers handle unfamiliar evidence, distinguish recognition from endorsement, and revise inaccurate descriptions. Behavioral changes would not by themselves establish the truth of a theological claim about the discovery.

Humility can come from within commitment

First Corinthians 13 associates love with an acknowledgment of partial knowledge. First Thessalonians 5 holds openness to prophetic speech beside the requirement to test and retain what is good. These passages do not require a believer to regard every claim as equally true. They do challenge the assumption that religious commitment must eliminate examination.

There is a distinction between uncertainty about a complete account of God and paralysis about immediate obligations. A person can refuse to claim exhaustive knowledge of divine intention while acting firmly to protect someone from harm. Humility need not mean abandoning judgment; it can mean making judgment answerable to evidence and consequence.

I would therefore ask communities to distinguish the authority they attribute to a text from the certainty they attribute to their own reading. Even a believer who regards the text as perfect has not thereby shown that their translation, historical reconstruction or present application is perfect. The same distinction applies to this wiki and to the person writing it.

When a checklist looks like evidence

The prophetic checklist supplied with the discussion is useful as a demonstration of this problem. A checked box is an interpretation already made, not an independent observation. The relevant question is what the cited passage actually says, which figure it describes, and what documented event connects it to the proposed application.

Several of its labels draw on the same unit in 2 Thessalonians 2. Repeating that unit under different headings does not create several independent textual witnesses. Genesis 10 introduces Nimrod and places associated with his kingdom; Genesis 11 describes the builders of Babel's city and tower. They do not directly label a modern builder of towers as Antichrist. That application would need its own argument.

Revelation 9:11 identifies the ruler of the abyss as Abaddon or Apollyon. Identifying that figure with the first beast of Revelation 13 is another interpretive step, not something proved merely by attaching the label Destroyer to a person. Different passages can illuminate one another without becoming interchangeable descriptions by default.

The point is not to prohibit prophetic comparison or remove the wiki's figure rankings. It is to keep the passage, the event, the proposed relation and the remaining gap visible. If fourteen checks depend on a handful of passages and repeated descriptions of one event, the presentation should not pretend it has fourteen independent confirmations. This appendix audits selected textual connections, not every claim in the image.

An invitation rather than a concealed conclusion

The purpose of placing traditions beside one another is to let readers encounter the evidence that a slogan would conceal. Subtle writing can create room for recognition without requiring a humiliating surrender. It should not hide a predetermined genealogy inside a picture and invite people to mistake the picture for a demonstrated history.

My declared aim is peace through more truthful comparison. A participant should remain free to reject the synthesis while recognizing a passage, a practice or a question that deserves attention. The credibility of the project depends on allowing that freedom, not merely on making its conclusions attractive.

A library can make a neighbor's inheritance available without demanding that the neighbor cease to be themselves. A scripture can orient a life without giving its present interpreter ownership of every truth. A recovered text can become a gift without becoming a replacement sovereign.

The proposal that God may intend human beings to learn from recovered history remains a theological possibility, not an archaeological measurement. Its constructive force is this: no community should assume that divine truth is obligated to stop at the boundary of what that community has already understood. I take the same obligation upon my own account.
