SHARED INHERITANCE / DIFFERENT AUTHORITIES

A library larger than any one canon

What becomes visible when communities read beside one another?

Micah Blumberg / Authorial working study / September 9, 2026

Begin with a room, not a family-tree verdict

Imagine a reading room in which people bring the writings through which they learned to recognize divine authority. Some bring works familiar to others. Some bring a differently ordered collection, a commentary, a received teaching, or a text whose authority the other readers dispute. The first task is not to decide which visitor secretly belongs to another visitor's religion. It is to learn what each book is being asked to do.

That is the picture I want this inquiry to make available. People can share memories, names and questions while disagreeing over how an inheritance should continue. What one calls preservation, another may call correction or expansion. A comparison becomes revealing when it follows those operations through evidence rather than announcing that all the participants were always the same.

A family tree can be useful where descent is documented. It becomes misleading where it makes one living community the established parent of every other tradition without evidence for the intervening history. Shared inheritance, direct descent, literary reuse and a modern ethical analogy are different relationships. Leaving them distinguishable makes room for a more interesting history.

One inheritance, different kinds of books

The Israelite Samaritan Information Institute describes an identity centered on one God, Moses, the Torah and Mount Gerizim. Encountering that self-description beside a Jewish library makes shared Mosaic material and disputed authority visible together. The self-description is a voice in the comparison, not a neutral judgment resolving all rival histories.

In Chabad's account, the Talmud records the Mishnah and the further discussions associated with its interpretation. It belongs to the tradition of Oral Torah, not to a replacement covenant called a Jewish New Testament. The distinction is important even for a critic: written compilation and a community's account of the antiquity of a teaching are not the same claim.

The same institution presents Kabbalah as a received inner dimension of Torah and distinguishes it from the Talmud's characteristic concerns. Kabbalah is not one additional testament with precisely the authority that another religion assigns to its scripture. These descriptions show ways an inheritance is extended and interpreted without requiring all later writings to occupy one identical category.

From outside, a reader can investigate when a particular text was composed and how its claims developed. From within, a believer may understand the same teaching as received rather than invented. The historical inquiry should identify the witnesses supporting a proposed date; the presentation of belief should preserve what the community actually claims. Neither task benefits from making the other disappear.

Confirmation, renewed revelation and distinct witnesses

Quran 5:48 describes the Quran in relation to earlier scriptures, asserts its own authority, and places differences in laws and ways of life beside a summons to compete in good works. This is not simply a claim that another volume has been attached to an unchanged Bible. The text's openness to a comparative ethical question coexists with a distinctive claim about revelation and judgment.

The Latter-day Saint Articles of Faith 8 and 9 state commitments concerning the Bible, the Book of Mormon and continuing revelation. Second Nephi 29 directly challenges the claim that possession of a Bible exhausts what God may say to the nations. That chapter also honors the Jewish contribution to the Bible and rebukes hostility toward Jews. A selective quotation about additional books would miss that accompanying obligation.

These passages provide a striking comparison with recovered writings without independently authenticating any new manuscript as revelation. They show that openness to further teaching can itself be articulated from within a canon. That is different from an outsider announcing that a tradition must abandon its identity to become open-minded.

The Mandaean Book of John must receive its own treatment rather than being assigned the status of an appendix to the canonical Gospel of John. Charles G. Haberl and James F. McGrath's edition and its institutional catalogue identify a distinct Mandaean work. This limited bibliographic comparison does not establish a direct Mandaean descent from Samaritanism or any particular early Christian school.

The Nag Hammadi collection likewise is not a membership register for a single church called Gnosticism. Its surviving books are witnesses to be compared work by work. The collection even includes a Coptic adaptation of a passage from Plato's Republic. Finding different writings together does not give them one author, one argument or one level of sacred authority.

A canon is not the complete archive of an inheritance

A canon identifies writings accorded a particular normative standing by a community. A historical archive can be much larger. A noncanonical text may change what is known about a community's language, arguments, practices or opponents without becoming scripture for that community. The choice is not limited to rejecting its evidence or canonizing every sentence.

The Dead Sea Scrolls make this distinction unusually concrete. The Israel Antiquities Authority's catalogue distinguishes biblical compositions, interpretations, community rules, calendrical material and other types. Its labels explicitly concern modern classification; the sacred standing of a work among particular ancient readers remains a question to investigate. The presence of a manuscript is not an automatically reconstructed ancient canon.

Nor does exclusion prove that a text was false, or inclusion prove that every later interpretation is correct. A judgment about authority requires reasons. A judgment about what was historically believed requires witnesses. A judgment about what an interpretation does to people requires attention to its practice. These inquiries can enrich one another without becoming substitutes for one another.

Selection is therefore real, but it need not be described as a casual preference or a cynical invention. Communities can experience their obligations as inherited, reasoned and sacred. A serious comparison asks which decisions were made, what reasons were given, and what alternatives were known. Humility begins when the reasons become available for examination.

A common table need not become one compulsory creed

The shared table has room for disagreement. A participant may learn from a rival's interpretation and still decline its account of God. Another may revise a historical belief without changing a practice of prayer. A third may find that a passage once used to exclude a neighbor contains a demand the community has not yet applied to itself.

I understand these encounters as possibilities for Apocalyptic Repair Theology. The aim is not to make every tradition confess an identity it rejects. It is to make mutual recognition, accurate memory and care possible where a claim to exclusive possession of truth had made listening seem disloyal.

That aim requires the same restraint from this synthesis. I cannot demand that other readers admit the limits of their interpretations while presenting mine as the final view above every tradition. The proposed quilt must show its seams: where a historical connection is documented, where a theological bridge is offered, and where the participants still disagree.

The room becomes more peaceful not because all differences have vanished, but because a difference no longer has to be mistaken for permission to stop learning about the person across the table.
