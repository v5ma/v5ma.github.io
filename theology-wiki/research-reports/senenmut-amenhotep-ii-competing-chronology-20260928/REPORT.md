# Senenmut, Hatshepsut and Amenhotep II: competing early-Exodus chronology audit

Updated September 28, 2026.

## Purpose

This report adds the Hatshepsut–Senenmut–Thutmose III–Amenhotep II reconstruction as a competing chronology beside the Theology Wiki's existing end-Hyksos/Avaris model. It does not replace the archived Hyksos report or convert structural parallels into historical identifications.

The comparison is necessary because a literal reading of 1 Kings 6:1's 480 years, combined with a conventional date near 966 BCE for Solomon's fourth year, produces an Exodus near 1446 BCE. The existing end-Hyksos model instead explores a departure/conflict horizon around the middle of the sixteenth century BCE. These cannot be treated as the same chronology.

## What is historically secure about Senenmut

Senenmut was a major nonroyal official under Hatshepsut. The Metropolitan Museum's excavation of the family burial below TT71 independently identifies Ramose and Hatnefer as his father and mother. Ramose's relatively modest original burial and Hatnefer's much richer later burial document the family's change in circumstances after Senenmut's rise. The burial also contained other probable family members. This is important counterevidence to any presentation of Senenmut as an otherwise parentless outsider whose Egyptian family background is unknown.

Met source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/545147

The same burial contains material connected directly with the royal household. Linen used with Ramose bears the cartouche of Hatshepsut's daughter Neferure, and other linen came from royal stores. This supports Senenmut's unusually close connection to Hatshepsut and Neferure; it does not demonstrate that Hatshepsut adopted Senenmut.

Met source: https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/549192

Senenmut's rise, royal access, stewardship and building responsibilities make him a useful structural comparison with the literary Moses profile under an eighteenth-dynasty chronology. His role in relation to Neferure is especially important, but it is a reverse parallel: Senenmut is documented as an adult guardian/tutor associated with a royal child, whereas Exodus portrays Moses as the child raised by Pharaoh's daughter.

Claims that the name Senenmut, commonly interpreted approximately as "mother's brother," proves adoption into Hatshepsut's family should remain classified as modern interpretation unless an Egyptian source explicitly attesting such a renaming is produced.

Likewise, claims that Senenmut commanded a Kush campaign should not enter the factual layer without object- or inscription-level evidence. Later traditions that make Moses an Egyptian commander in Ethiopia belong to the reception-history layer, not contemporary Egyptian evidence.

## The disappearance and funerary evidence

Senenmut prepared exceptional funerary monuments, including TT71 and TT353. His funerary program was not completed in the ordinary way and no secure burial of Senenmut has been recovered. This makes his final years a legitimate historical question.

It does not by itself establish flight from Egypt. Abandoned or unfinished funerary work has multiple possible explanations. The Wiki should therefore distinguish the observed facts — unfinished work, uncertain burial and an incomplete late-career record — from the proposed equation with Moses's flight.

The chronological coincidence sometimes proposed between Senenmut's late attestations and a Moses flight near 1486 BCE is conditional. It depends on a particular absolute chronology for Hatshepsut, a literal c.1446 BCE Exodus, and Acts 7's forty-year life-stage scheme. It is worth recording as a model-dependent synchronism, not as an independently dated event.

## Hatshepsut as "Pharaoh's daughter"

Hatshepsut was a daughter of Thutmose I, royal wife of Thutmose II, stepmother of Thutmose III, regent and ultimately pharaoh. She is therefore a chronologically testable candidate for Pharaoh's daughter only under particular fifteenth-century Exodus reconstructions.

The identification remains unproven. Royal self-descriptions concerning compassion, love or treatment of foreigners are ideological inscriptions and cannot independently establish an adoption. Depending on the absolute chronology chosen, Hatshepsut may also have been quite young at the proposed date of Moses's infancy.

The Wiki should not repeat the claim that Thutmose III was Hatshepsut's only child. Thutmose III was Thutmose II's son by Iset; Hatshepsut and Thutmose II also had the royal daughter Neferure.

## Amenhotep II and the Exodus-pharaoh proposal

Amenhotep II is a genuine candidate within some early-Exodus reconstructions, but several popular arguments need qualification.

Amenhotep II was not Thutmose III's original eldest heir; an older royal son, Amenemhat, is attested and apparently predeceased his father. That circumstance is compatible with, but does not demonstrate, a firstborn-death scenario.

Amenhotep II's early reign contains aggressive military activity and royal rhetoric of extreme violence. His known campaign record should not be reduced to "only two campaigns" without resolving the different regnal-year records and co-regency proposals. The existing Theology Wiki artifact audit already notes evidence for campaigns associated with years 3, 7 and 9.

A reduction in major campaigning later in the reign does not independently demonstrate destruction of an Egyptian army in the Sea of Reeds. Diplomatic and geopolitical stabilization is an alternative explanation. Likewise, later captive-taking cannot be labeled a slave-replacement program for escaped Israelites unless an independent source connects the two events.

The reported warning involving foreigners and magical practitioners in material associated with Usersatet is a useful object-level research target. The exact Egyptian text, translation, date and context should be collated before it is compared with Exodus's magician narratives.

## Manetho, Josephus and "Amenophis"

Josephus preserves, while attacking, a Manethonian story involving Avaris, a king called Amenophis, an expelled or polluted population, Jerusalem, and a Heliopolitan priest called Osarsiph who supposedly changed his name to Moses.

Josephus text: https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/apion-1.html

This is important reception evidence. It shows that a late Egyptian/Jewish polemical tradition connected Avaris, Moses and an Amenophis figure.

It does not identify that king securely as Amenhotep II. Amenophis is a Greek form used for Egyptian kings named Amenhotep, and the Manethonian tradition is chronologically late and internally problematic. The correct Wiki claim is therefore "Manetho/Josephus preserves an Amenophis–Avaris–Osarsiph/Moses tradition," not "Manetho identifies Amenhotep II as the Exodus pharaoh."

## Comparison with the end-Hyksos model

The two models gain and lose different kinds of evidence.

The fifteenth-century model gains the straightforward literal 1 Kings 6:1 calculation and produces a court sequence in which Hatshepsut, Senenmut, Thutmose III and Amenhotep II can be tested against the biblical profile. It also places the Rekhmire brickmaking scene in approximately the broader social world being proposed.

Its major weakness is identification. No contemporary Egyptian inscription identifies Senenmut as Hebrew or Moses, Hatshepsut as his adoptive mother, or Amenhotep II as the ruler of Exodus. Senenmut's excavated Egyptian family is a substantial constraint that any identity hypothesis must explain.

The end-Hyksos model gains an unusually strong eastern-Delta social setting: a large Levantine-derived population at Avaris, deep Egyptian-Levantine cultural integration, foreign-origin elites reaching royal status, direct links toward Canaan, and a real Egyptian-Hyksos war culminating in Avaris's fall. It also gains the later Manetho/Josephus Avaris departure tradition as possible reception evidence.

Its major weakness is biblical chronology. A c.1550 BCE Exodus conflicts with a straightforward literal 480-year reading of 1 Kings 6:1 unless the 480 is schematic, an anchor date changes, or another chronological/textual solution is adopted. The existing report already records this cost and it should remain visible.

Neither model currently identifies Moses in contemporary Egyptian evidence.

## Shared evidence should not be double-counted

Papyrus Brooklyn, Avaris archaeology, Rekhmire's brickmaking scene, Ipuwer, Soleb/Amara West yhw material, Manetho/Josephus, Egyptian campaign inscriptions and the Merneptah Israel inscription do not all date the same event. Each must retain its own archaeological or textual date.

A useful evidence ledger should therefore encode at least: artifact/text; physical date; proposed composition date where different; event described; geographic setting; direct wording; historical inference; biblical comparison; chronology favored; chronology challenged; and confidence/limitations.

The question is not how many artifacts can be placed beside Exodus. The question is which chronology can accommodate independently dated evidence without moving artifacts, people or texts out of their actual historical contexts.

## Research targets

The highest-value next work for the eighteenth-dynasty model is object-level rather than personality-first: collate Senenmut's last dated attestations; catalogue TT71/TT353 evidence and the chronology of damage; verify every claimed military title or Kush connection; reconstruct Hatshepsut's age and status under alternative absolute chronologies; collate Amenhotep II's year 3/7/9 campaign texts; obtain the full Usersatet warning and Egyptian terminology; reconstruct Amenhotep II's succession and firstborn evidence; and separate Manetho's Amenophis traditions by manuscript witness.

For the end-Hyksos model, retain the existing priorities: early Hyksos royal-family prosopography, Yanassi's monument, royal women and King's Daughter attestations, Tell el-Dab'a chronology, elite kinship/mobility, and the exact political geography connecting Avaris with the Theban war.

## Current synthesis

The Theology Wiki should carry both reconstructions as competing hypotheses.

The Hatshepsut–Senenmut–Thutmose III–Amenhotep II reconstruction is chronologically attractive under a literal fifteenth-century Exodus calculation and contains several genuine structural parallels, but no contemporary evidence currently identifies Senenmut as Moses.

The end-Hyksos/Avaris reconstruction has a particularly strong archaeological social and geopolitical setting for a Levantine-associated population in Egypt and a later mass-departure memory, but it pays a substantial cost against a literal 1 Kings 6:1 chronology and likewise lacks a contemporary identification of Moses.

Future updates should prefer discriminating evidence — evidence that one model predicts and the other does not — over accumulating additional superficial parallels.
