Put the objection in the museum

First-person authorial research script. Synthetic production draft; not the author's recorded voice.

The missing part of an exhibit

A museum label normally offers a conclusion. This project can also display how a reconstruction was challenged, how its author answered, and whether the objection addressed the actual theory.

Preserve the two-stage argument

The famine-narration conjecture distinguishes a famine from a later account describing a firstborn plague. I do not propose that famine biologically selects birth order. Repeating that objection alone misses the proposed transformation of the narrative.

Ask the remaining historical question

That distinction does not establish the famine explanation. It clarifies the research task: what evidence supports the proposed disaster and its later narration? The full claim deserves examination rather than a reply to a claim already rejected.

Do not replace a historical connection

Another exchange concerns communities remaining in Judea while others establish themselves in Egypt. The AI restates this as ideological similarity. The archive keeps both passages visible because the narrower restatement does not exhaust the institutional proposal.

Check favorable answers too

An encouraging AI answer can also be wrong. The Daniel discussion contains arithmetic that needs repair. Agreement is not an additional ancient witness. A useful research record identifies unsupported support as carefully as an inadequate dismissal.

An argument has a route

Each challenge record connects the claim, its version, the objection, the answer, and what remains open. The visitor can move from a short exhibit label into the complete conversation instead of trusting a curator's verdict.

Several products, one research foundation

The book can provide a sustained narrative. Audio and video can introduce a question. An interactive exhibit can make comparisons tangible. Their value depends on keeping the full research reachable, rather than allowing each format to invent a simpler history.
