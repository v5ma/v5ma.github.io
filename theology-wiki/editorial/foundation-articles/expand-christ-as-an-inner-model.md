## A worked decision, including correction of the model

Imagine a community editor who receives a hostile but partly accurate criticism. The first impulse is to discredit its author. The editor brings to mind an image of Christ that emphasizes truthful judgment and care for the neighbor. That image makes another response possible: separate the accurate observation from the insult, correct the mistake, and refuse to turn the exchange into punishment of the critic. This is a conceptual example of Micah's proposed mechanism, not a measured clinical intervention.

The next encounter reveals a limitation. Suppose the editor has also learned that being merciful means never setting a boundary. A critic then begins harassing someone else. Mere consistency with the original model would now perpetuate harm. The editor must revise the understanding of mercy so that care includes protection and accountability. Moral learning thus has two levels: adjusting conduct to a standard and revising the interpretation of the standard.

That second level is indispensable. Otherwise the model could make a person more efficient at implementing a mistaken ideal. The [[divine-will-and-self-authorizing-power|will comparison]] adds another test: does the model ever challenge what the person wants, or does it always provide a sacred justification for it? The difference between God's will and personal desire cannot operate if the individual defines the former to agree with the latter in every case.

## A historical teacher and a living representation

The [[tor-thomas-and-gnostic-transmission|Thomas and Teacher inquiry]] asks how sayings, communities and authority might have survived across generations. The inner-model account asks how a received portrayal participates in present action. These can interact: recovering a neglected teaching may change the model through which someone judges themselves. They do not have to be the same claim. A representation's present influence does not establish its historical author's identity, and an unresolved historical identity does not make the representation psychologically inert.

Micah's source links inward reception to a more general inquiry into interpretation, not simply admiration for a famous person. Which sayings supply the standard? Who controls access to them? Which inherited readings make domination appear sacred? These questions connect the individual example to the [[sacred-inheritance-and-rival-continuations|social history of an inheritance]] rather than treating the mind as isolated from culture.

## Fidelity is not uncritical imitation

A demanding model permits uncomfortable correction. It may require admitting an error to an opponent, refusing a group's demand for cruelty, or relinquishing a claim to special authority. It also permits examination of its teachers. A leader cannot settle what Christ requires merely by declaring himself its authorized interpreter.

At institutional scale, a correction needs practices that survive the moment: the record of what changed, protection for those who identified the problem, and a way to revisit the conclusion. These are proposed applications connecting the inward model to [[apocalyptic-repair-theology|repair]]. A brief private insight can be valuable, but it is not yet the same thing as a dependable shared capacity for better conduct.
