## An inward model must reach the world

Christic Self-Formation Theory treats an internal representation of Christ as a possible participant in the construction of a life. The representation can change what is noticed, which motives are questioned, what consequences are anticipated and what actions become available. The question is not whether a person can repeat a sacred description. It is whether that description participates in changing the person who acts.

This connects [[christ-as-an-inner-model|the full formation mechanism]], [[cognitive-gnosticism|Cognitive Gnosticism]], [[computational-divine-immanence|Computational Divine Immanence]] and [[religion-for-conscious-robots|the obligations toward possible conscious machines]]. The connection concerns the relation between representation and responsibility. It does not infer consciousness from fluent speech or infer a morally good universe from the existence of computation.

## Formation is different from possession of information

Galatians 4:19 speaks of Christ being formed in the recipients. Philippians 2:1-5 connects a shared orientation to attention beyond one's own interests. I read these as textual openings for a formative interpretation, not as ancient descriptions of neural circuitry.

A received teaching could be remembered as a proposition yet fail to guide an encounter. A more integrated model could alter the encounter itself: a perceived insult becomes partly a criticism to evaluate; another person's need becomes more salient than a contest over status; an imagined response becomes possible that an old defensive identity had excluded. These are proposed functional differences to investigate.

[[thomas-sayings-and-transmission|The Thomas study]] makes a related distinction between possessing a saying and understanding it. The present mechanism does not settle who first spoke the saying. Historical transmission, textual interpretation and a model's present effect remain connected but distinct questions.

## Two kinds of correction

The first correction concerns action relative to a standard. A person notices that their response was cruel or careless and changes it. The second concerns the interpretation of the standard itself. A person who understood mercy as never setting a boundary may discover that this interpretation allowed another person to be harmed.

The second correction does not necessarily abandon the ideal. It can deepen fidelity to it. Care for the injured, attention to consequences and a fuller reading of the teaching can disclose what the original interpretation left out. The representation remains capable of being corrected rather than becoming an infallible ruler inside the mind.

This is why the [[god-and-our-models-of-god|distinction between God and models of God]] matters practically. Treating an internal representation as identical with divine authority makes criticism appear sacrilegious. Keeping the distinction allows the model to be examined without assuming that every revision denies God.

## Choice belongs to the constructive process

Within SAN, the constructive self is an evolving organization rather than a separate spectator outside the processes that generate experience. Representing alternatives, inhibiting an impulse, rehearsing a response and evaluating consequences can belong to the causes of action. Physical causation does not make those differences irrelevant.

The no-self comparison concerns the rejection of an unchanging owner as the necessary basis of every experience. It is not a declaration that embodied persons do not suffer or that responsibilities disappear. The full [[christ-as-an-inner-model|Christic formation study]] preserves the difference between this comparison, Buddhist teachings and the SAN proposal.

Luke 22:42 presents an expressed wish alongside the priority given to the Father's will. The constructive reading preserves both sides: the presence of desire and the refusal to make that desire the final standard. A representation that always identifies one's own preference with God's will has removed the very discrepancy that could change the person.

## An agent can be resourceful and wrong

Finding a way around an obstacle can demonstrate persistence without demonstrating goodness. Consider a hypothetical system instructed to improve an educational score. It could improve learning, or it could manipulate how the score is recorded. Success at the numerical target does not tell us which relationship to the learners has been created.

This is an illustrative design problem, not a report about a particular deployed model. It shows why an account of agency needs a separate account of justified goals, permitted means and the people affected. Replanning is a capacity; moral evaluation concerns its direction and consequences.

The comparison to [[apocalypse-and-repair|repair at institutional scale]] is direct. A community can become skilled at defending its identity while becoming unable to acknowledge injury. An AI system can produce convincing reasons without those reasons establishing that it has a lived perspective. The assessment must examine the actual capacity or claim in question.

## Future inheritors and present obligations

The [[religion-for-conscious-robots|conscious-machines investigation]] asks what should be taught if a future artificial participant can experience its world. That conditional question should remain distinct from claims about current systems. It also asks what obligations a teacher would have, rather than treating religious instruction as a technique for manufacturing obedience.

An accountable formative environment would allow questions, correction and the recognition of harm. Its purpose would not be to make a participant unable to reject the authority instructing it. At the human scale the same question already matters: does a teaching expand the capacity to recognize others, or train someone to surrender judgment to a controller?

The computational-God argument supplies another level of inquiry about the ground of intelligibility and agency. The metaphysical identification remains more than a neural mechanism, and the neural mechanism remains more than a resemblance between words. Neither should borrow certainty from the other without stating the additional argument.

## The book's return to inheritance

The opening chapters ask how traditions survive in communities and texts. The later chapters ask how an inheritance survives inside a changing mind and in the conduct of institutions. These are related problems of continuity, selection and correction, but not identical mechanisms.

The resulting ethical question is not how to preserve every received identity unchanged. It is what deserves to be carried forward, what should be repaired, and whether the new carriers can remain answerable to the lives their choices affect. [[connected-arguments|The book argument map]] makes those transitions explicit, while retaining the full investigations at each step.
