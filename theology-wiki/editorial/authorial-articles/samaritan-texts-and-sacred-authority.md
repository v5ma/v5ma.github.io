## The question beneath the place name

Mount Gerizim opens a question about inheritance. Where do Samaritan and Jewish texts divide? Why does a community accept shared Mosaic material, and what changes when later books do not belong to its scriptural collection? These questions concern the constitution of sacred authority, not merely two versions of a place-name.

Several historical explanations are proposed or repeated as questions in the exchange. Those questions should not be converted into established facts merely because they appear in a source chat. The durable research problem is the relationship between shared text and competing claims to continuity.

## Three comparisons that must not be collapsed

A difference between readings in a manuscript is not the same thing as a difference in the books accepted as scripture. Neither is identical to a disagreement over the proper sacred center. I examine all three: wording associated with Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, the boundaries of a Samaritan canon, and their implications for the authority of Samuel, David and Solomon.

Separating the questions makes the comparison more powerful. A community could share a textual feature without sharing every institutional allegiance. A canon could omit a book without eliminating every ethical difficulty in the books it retains. An argument about sacred geography needs its own textual and archaeological evidence.

## The Dead Sea Scrolls question

Do pre-Samaritan textual affinities in the Dead Sea Scrolls support the Gerizim claim? A shared reading or harmonizing style would not by itself establish a community's allegiance or a particular manuscript's position on the sanctuary. The move from textual affinity to sacred geography is the inference to test.

A developed historical article should identify the manuscript and disputed passage, show the relevant readings and explain why each reading matters. This edition records the research question rather than supplying a verdict from an unverified AI summary.

## The ethical question is not incidental

Changes in a canon can also change the materials through which moral authority is interpreted. The Samuel comparison asks whether sacred authority is used to authorize objectionable conduct, and whether other retained texts challenge it. This connects the inquiry to [[antichrist-as-a-pattern-of-conduct|the judgment of conduct]] and [[christ-as-an-inner-model|the content of an inward moral model]].

A shorter canon is not necessarily an ethically simpler canon. Each retained passage and each rejected claim to authority still needs examination. Selective readings cannot establish the moral character of an entire tradition by choosing only the passages that make the desired contrast easy.

## Shared inheritance, different continuations

The most useful framework is therefore not a contest over which tradition is merely a copy. It is an inquiry into what was shared, what diverged, how those differences were justified and what consequences followed.
