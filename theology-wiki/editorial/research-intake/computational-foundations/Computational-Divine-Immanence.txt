Computational Divine Immanence is a working name for an interpretation in which physical reality is not a second, independent realm standing outside God. The proposal identifies the world's intelligible, generative ground as divine and asks how finite minds, choices, and histories belong within it. It is a philosophical extension of a computational account of nature, not the claim that a neuroscience experiment has detected a universal person.

I use computational to describe organized transformations of states and relations, not necessarily a digital machine with a programmer outside it. There are two different pictures here. In one, a powerful operator constructs a universe on some further substrate. In the other, the generative order is intrinsic to reality rather than a device installed in a larger room. Divine immanence develops the second picture. It does not require calling the world unreal. Pain, affection, bodies, and consequences remain real within the account.

An intelligent ground is a stronger proposal than lawful structure. Merely renaming the universe God would not explain memory, understanding, evaluation, or responsiveness. The argument must therefore say what intelligence contributes. The computational-tomographic account supplies a candidate organization: partial information is received and transformed, related to a model, evaluated against alternatives, used in selection, and updated through consequences. This organization makes the proposed intellect intelligible without assuming a human-shaped ruler beyond the sky.

The local investigation begins with Self Aware Networks and Tomographic Coherence-Based Neural Rendering. Distributed activity can be modeled as contributing partial information to a recurrent, action-guiding estimate. The present TCNR protocol asks a restricted empirical question about spatial phase features and prediction in a motor task. The wider account concerns the connection among difference, representation, prediction, action, and feedback. These are related projects, but an affirmative result for one task would not settle every other level of the account.

A rendered experience and an externally created world must remain distinguishable. A mind can construct a compelling scene without physically producing the objects represented in that scene. Altering a representation can also affect the world through ordinary action: a revised expectation changes a decision, the decision changes conduct, and conduct changes the conditions encountered afterward. The claim that a cosmic ground actualizes a world is stronger than either mental imagery or embodied action. It requires its own argument.

The computational-tomographic God argument supplies that separate route. It begins with contingent reality and a restricted Principle of Sufficient Reason: a contingent totality calls for a sufficient explanation of why it obtains rather than a relevant alternative. If that principle, the coherence of the totality, and the prohibition on circular explanation are accepted, the argument reaches a necessary ground. The conclusion at this stage concerns explanatory necessity, not yet intellect or moral perfection.

The next question is what kind of ground could preserve contingency. A necessary deterministic law together with necessary complete conditions would fix its output. A stochastic law provides probabilities, but under the argument's stronger explanatory demand, a distribution does not by itself explain the particular realized outcome. Reasons-responsive agency is advanced as an alternative: a selector can represent more than one admissible possibility and act for reasons without those reasons deductively necessitating only one result.

This is the affirmative agency argument, not a demonstration that every impersonal explanation has already been eliminated. Its central burden is precise: explain how reasons-guided selection differs from unexplained selection while retaining genuine alternatives. A critic who rejects that bridge has challenged the agency argument, not thereby refuted the earlier conditional necessary-ground result or the independent neuroscience program.

The identification of that ground with an ideal renderer adds another bridge. Integrated modeling, alternatives, selection, and world actualization are not interchangeable properties. Neither knowledge nor power entails goodness by definition. The God paper makes these property mappings explicit. Its modal argument then asks what follows if the joined necessary intellective ground is genuinely metaphysically possible and the S5 modal system is appropriate. Formal consequence must be distinguished from the defense of those premises.

The paper's three-world model makes one connection particularly useful for the theory of choice. A ground may exist throughout the model while its relation to the selected world differs. Necessary existence and the necessity of every particular outcome are not the same proposition. The model is a formal consistency witness; it is not an observation of additional universes or proof that a finite person can select the physical world's history.

Divine immanence introduces a further question that the necessary-ground proof does not automatically settle. Is the world identical with that ground, dependent on it, or an expression within it? These relations need different formulations. I develop the expression-within account as a candidate connection: contingent forms can change while remaining dependent on a ground that is not simply one more contingent object. This formulation preserves the possibility of an evolving world without making every revision of a human representation a revision of the fundamental laws.

Coherence is important to this research, but it cannot perform every explanatory job merely because one word can travel between disciplines. TCNR distinguishes statistical dependence, neural oscillatory coherence, computational integration, and quantum coherence. SIT likewise requires a specified measurement and a calibrated connection to a model field. The constructive question concerns which relations allow a system to preserve distinctions while organizing them into a usable whole. Perfect uniformity is not the same as richer understanding.

The earlier picture of opposing tendencies toward coherence and decoherence therefore needs operational definitions rather than an automatic identification with two thermodynamic arrows. The supplied SIT successor narrows its claim to specified processes, measures, disturbances, and physical bookkeeping. Its examples distinguish changing purity from quantum coherence and contraction of a mismatch from physical entropy production. This repair preserves the investigation into organization and dissipation while removing a universal statement that the later argument does not support.

At the personal scale, the relevant connection is to Christic Self-Construction Theory. An inward model of Christ can be proposed as part of the organization through which situations, motives, and possible responses are interpreted. The exemplar matters not because its name is repeated, but because its content can challenge a current impulse. A model that always sanctifies an existing desire does not perform the same function as one that makes an uncomfortable correction possible.

This approach to transformation does not need an unchanging inner spectator. A constructive self can preserve bodily, historical, and practical continuity while its representations change. Choice then belongs to the processes that make alternatives available, compare their consequences, select an action, and learn afterward. A physically caused deliberation need not be an irrelevant deliberation. The account should not silently identify this bounded, organism-level agency with the stronger non-necessitating agency proposed in the metaphysical argument.

Concern for future selves creates an ethical connection without requiring a claim to special personal status. If tomorrow's person matters despite changes in memory, feeling, and circumstance, permanence is not the only possible foundation for care. Recognizing the future vulnerability of other people extends that concern beyond a privileged personal history. The ethical proposal is to organize action around the lives affected, not to equate greater intelligence or greater computational capacity with greater moral worth.

A physical balance is not automatically a moral judgment. Work, heat, information, and entropy require specified physical definitions; responsibility and justice require additional ethical argument. Calling consequences a form of accounting can illuminate persistence and obligation, but it cannot turn thermodynamics into evidence that every wrongdoing receives a corresponding punishment. The repair project must address actual harm, protection, and changed conduct rather than promise that a physical law will perform those tasks on its behalf.

Historical religion belongs within this account as a changing human encounter with questions of existence, suffering, order, and restoration. The date of a community's ancestor does not set the age of the reality about which it speaks. Tracing a flood narrative across traditions can deepen its cultural ancestry without dating creation itself. A lineage can preserve recognizable structures while revising divine agency, human responsibility, and the meaning of survival.

The comparison with the Monad, the Pleroma, or the Ruliad should identify a relationship rather than announce that different terms have always named exactly the same entity. The Apocryphon of John and Wolfram's computational construction ask different questions in different conceptual languages. A modern interpretation can compare an ultimate ground, a structured realm of possibilities, and a finite perspective within a larger order while retaining the differences that make the comparison informative.

Experiences of unity or transformation can motivate these questions without acquiring automatic authority over the physical world. A report of a powerful experience establishes what was experienced and how it was interpreted; its external cause requires additional evidence. The explanatory distinction protects both sides of the inquiry. Neuroscience need not declare a theological question meaningless, and theology need not turn a vivid perception into a measured physical event.

The resulting project has a joined structure. Scientific models investigate candidate mechanisms. Philosophical arguments examine explanatory ground, intelligence, possibility, and identity. Historical inquiry traces texts and communities. Constructive theology asks how an interpretation changes the way a life is lived. Their value lies in the connections, but those connections remain visible arguments rather than concealed changes in the meaning of evidence.
