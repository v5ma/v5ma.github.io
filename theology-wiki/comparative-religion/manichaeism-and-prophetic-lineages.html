Manichaeism and universal prophetic lineages

A synthesis can invoke common teachers while retaining a distinctive account of creation, self and liberation.

Mani's synthesis was not a neutral combination of equal doctrines

Manichaean traditions situate Mani in relation to earlier revelations associated with figures including Jesus, Buddha and Zoroaster. The claim is a particular theology of revelation and completion, not evidence that the earlier traditions understood their teachings as interchangeable. The surviving languages and settings of Manichaean literature require attention to how the message was translated and presented.

The light-and-darkness opposition is more radical than merely assigning wrongdoing to a creature under one sovereign creator. Yet the familiar summary that dark powers simply made a useless material prison misses the role of the light powers in constructing and operating the cosmos as part of the extraction and rescue of light. The cosmological machinery is part of the proposed solution to mixture, not just a meaningless accident.

Sources: Manicheism i. General Survey, Encyclopaedia Iranica / Werner Sundermann, Cosmogony and Cosmology iii. In Manicheism, Encyclopaedia Iranica.

Body, work and community require specific descriptions

The distinction between Elect and Hearers describes different religious obligations and forms of support. Calling them rigid hereditary castes adds a claim that the classification itself does not establish. Their relationship needs examination through practice, admission, teaching and the circulation of resources.

Likewise, celibacy and rules intended to avoid injury to living light should not be reduced to the claim that the Elect simply did nothing. A restriction on one kind of work can coexist with teaching and ritual duties. The historical explanation of Mani's death must also include its sources and political-religious setting rather than attribute a complex outcome to one disliked lifestyle practice.

Sources: Manicheism i. General Survey, Encyclopaedia Iranica / Werner Sundermann, Cosmogony and Cosmology iii. In Manicheism, Encyclopaedia Iranica.

The Buddhist comparison has a substantive difference

Samyutta Nikaya 22.59 examines the five aggregates through nonself. That gives a precise comparison with an account in which liberation releases an enduring light substance. Shared ascetic or meditative vocabulary does not make those accounts of a person equivalent.

Nor does a Buddhist rejection of a permanent self mean that every Buddhist teaching regards ethical agency or conventional persons as nonexistent. The comparison should distinguish permanent metaphysical identity from practical responsibility and from moment-to-moment experience. A claim that Mani borrowed a specific institution or identified himself as Maitreya requires its own dated textual witness; it is not inferred merely from Asian vocabulary.

Sources: Samyutta Nikaya 22.59, translation by Bhikkhu Bodhi / Manicheism i. General Survey, Encyclopaedia Iranica.

Later traditions also recognize Buddha and Zoroaster

The Baha'i community explicitly includes Buddha and Zoroaster among the named figures in its account of progressive revelation. An Ahmadiyya community publication likewise recognizes both within its understanding of prophetic history. Those are sufficient counterexamples to the claim that only Manichaeans included the two figures.

They do not establish one shared cosmology across the three traditions. Recognizing a predecessor can involve reinterpretation through a later theological framework. The historical Buddha's own teaching, a later community's attribution of prophecy to him and a scholar's comparison between the two must therefore remain separate records.

The supplied Druze claim about a particular list of Buddha, Zoroaster and immortal sages has not been authenticated in a specific authoritative passage. The directory records a scholarly point of entry for Druze history without constructing a list of secret doctrines from analogy.

Sources: Baha'i international community, introductory teachings / Ahmadiyya Muslim Community Greece, community and founder account / Druze doctrine and law, Institute of Ismaili Studies lecture description.

Ahmadiyya and Sufism are not equivalent kinds of category

The Ahmadiyya community traces its foundation to Mirza Ghulam Ahmad at Qadian in 1889, in British India. Its claims about messiahship and prophecy distinguish it from other Muslim communities. Its self-identification as Muslim and the fact that other authorities contest aspects of that identification should both be represented without an encyclopedia deciding the reader's faith for them.

Sufism is not simply another mutually exclusive denomination beside Sunni and Shia. It refers to devotional and interpretive traditions, including orders and practices, that can exist within different Muslim affiliations. A comparison must therefore keep doctrine, jurisprudence, spiritual practice and institutional belonging on separate lines of inquiry rather than draw a misleading one-level family tree.

Sources: Ahmadiyya Muslim Community, official introduction / The Pluralism Project, Sufism / The Pluralism Project, One Ummah with Many Views.
