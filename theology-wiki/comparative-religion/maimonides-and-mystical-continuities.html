Maimonides, mystical traditions and the scope of an objection

Replace a caricature of medieval rationalism with a distinction between normative judgment and historical evidence.

Do not attribute an objection without a passage

The proposition that Jesus imported a Persian Satan may be an argument made by a later reader citing Maimonides. Unless its actual Maimonidean passage is supplied, it should not be presented as his own documented wording. His works should be read for what they say rather than reconstructed from a modern polemical summary.

Guide for the Perplexed III.22 discusses Job and invokes a rabbinic identification of Satan, the evil inclination and the angel of death. It develops a philosophical interpretation of familiar religious language; it does not merely report that Judaism has never known an adversary. The chapter also emphasizes permitted action. A disagreement over how to interpret an agent differs from a denial that the tradition ever used the agent's name.

Sources: Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed III.22.

Two different kinds of anachronism

It would be historically mistaken to use a medieval author's preferred explanation as a complete inventory of what earlier Jews believed. It is not inherently anachronistic for a later religious authority to assess an earlier claim according to that authority's own standard. The first move misdescribes a historical field; the second is an identifiable normative judgment. The response is strongest when it makes that distinction instead of declaring Maimonides disconnected from Judaism.

In Laws of Kings and Wars 11, the messianic argument concerns concrete restoration, Torah and fulfillment; miraculous performance is not the decisive test. The criticism cannot be reduced to an inability to imagine supernatural conflict. Demonstrating pre-Christian Jewish demonology would not, by itself, satisfy those messianic criteria.

The phrase that Maimonides tried to sanitize Judaism of all myth and apocalypse was supplied as a purported Scholem quotation without an edition or page. It has not been authenticated and is not adopted as a quotation or a complete description of the philosophical project.

Sources: Maimonides, Laws of Kings and Wars 11, translated by Eliyahu Touger / Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed III.22.

Later mystical language demonstrates plurality, not one proven chain

Tanya's Iggeret HaTeshuvah 6 connects sin, displaced divine vitality and the other side with the work of repentance. It supplies a particular later Jewish account of opposition and restoration that cannot be summarized as a second equal god. It also makes transformation through conduct important rather than treating cosmic vocabulary as an excuse for passivity.

That witness establishes neither direct borrowing from Christianity nor an uninterrupted institutional line from Qumran through Jesus to Hasidism. To establish a transmission history requires intermediaries, texts, dates and distinguishable mechanisms of reception. A later parallel can demonstrate that a type of theological problem remained meaningful without supplying those missing links.

Apocalyptic Repair Theology can engage these traditions constructively while naming its own further proposal. It can ask how descriptions of alienation become practices of repair. The historical question of who borrowed from whom remains a different inquiry.

Sources: Tanya, Iggeret HaTeshuvah, chapter 6.

Cooperation without compulsory agreement

The 2015 Orthodox rabbinic declaration To Do the Will of Our Father in Heaven advocates cooperation with Christians while retaining theological differences. This is evidence of what its signatories endorsed, not an announcement that Orthodox or Hasidic Judaism collectively accepts Jesus as divine or Messiah.

The strongest bridge does not require either community to abandon its self-understanding. It distinguishes historical recognition, ethical cooperation, appreciation of a teacher and doctrinal assent. Those are possible relationships between traditions, not successive grades on a scale of enlightenment.

Sources: To Do the Will of Our Father in Heaven, Orthodox rabbinic statement (2015).
