Nag Hammadi is a collection, not one doctrine of escape

Jesus, the creator, resurrection and ritual must be compared tractate by tractate.

Avoid making a library into one speaker

Nag Hammadi designates a collection of manuscripts containing different works, not a surviving catechism agreed by one homogeneous Gnostic church. Sethian and Valentinian classifications are useful scholarly groupings, but they do not make every tractate say the same thing about Christ, creation or salvation. Nor does the category exhaust the philosophical and religious material transmitted in the codices.

Many Christian works in the collection present Jesus or the Savior as a revealer. That is not a reason to write that every Nag Hammadi text reveres Jesus or never entertains a criticism. Some works do not center him at all. The Gospel of Judas belongs to Codex Tchacos, not the Nag Hammadi discovery; it can be compared with the collection without changing its manuscript provenance.

Sources: Gnosticism, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy.

The lower creator is not always the same kind of enemy

A stark contrast between the highest divine realm and a deficient lower ruler occurs in important writings grouped as Sethian. That does not automatically entail two independent uncreated principles. A lower being derived through a larger divine history differs metaphysically from an evil principle existing on its own from the beginning.

Ptolemy's Letter to Flora provides a particularly important Valentinian comparison: the maker and lawgiver is distinguished from the highest good Father and from the devil, and is characterized through justice rather than simple evil. The letter survives through an opponent's preservation, not as a Nag Hammadi manuscript. It demonstrates why the blanket formula that all Gnostics hate one identical evil creator fails.

A comparison should therefore ask about origin, ignorance, moral quality, function and eventual fate separately. Even two texts using the word creator may assign it different meanings. Their disagreement is evidence about the history of the ideas, not noise to remove before analysis.

Sources: Gnosticism, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy / Ptolemy's Letter to Flora, G. R. S. Mead translation.

Philip does not abolish ritual

The Gospel of Philip names baptism, chrism, eucharist, redemption and bridal chamber as mysteries. Whatever the precise reconstruction of their practice, a text that makes such rites central cannot be used as evidence that all esoteric Christians rejected physical ritual. The formulation that gnosis always replaces scripture, reason and ritual with a solitary private experience is not adequate to this witness.

The comparison with Mandaean and Qumran purification becomes more interesting once it asks how bodily actions convey an otherwise hidden identity or transformation. Their rites need not be identical for the relationship between visible performance and invisible meaning to be a substantive comparative subject.

Sources: Gospel of Philip, translated by Willis Barnstone / Mandaean Associations Union, Mandaean beliefs.

Resurrection is transformed, not simply denied

The Treatise on the Resurrection explicitly insists on resurrection's reality and contrasts it with the world's instability. Its treatment of inward life, imperishability and present participation differs from a straightforward reassembly of the visible corpse. But describing it as saying that resurrection is merely false or illusory reverses its stated claim.

This is a useful primary-text comparison for Apocalyptic Repair Theology: liberation can be described as transformation and as a changed mode of participation, not only as an event deferred until the end of history. That constructive use does not settle what its author believed happened to Jesus's body, nor does it turn every Christian resurrection doctrine into the same proposition.

The next level of research is text-specific: compare translations against the Coptic, identify the narrative and ritual setting, and distinguish statements about Christ from statements about the believer. The indexed translation passages used here support the limited corrections; a complete critical-edition study remains additional work.

Sources: Treatise on the Resurrection, translated by Willis Barnstone.
