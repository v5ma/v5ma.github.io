Zoroastrian traditions are not one frozen formula

The description of Zoroastrianism as invariably teaching two equal, uncreated gods is too blunt. Iranian sources and later interpretations differ over the destructive spirit, the beneficent spirit, Ahura Mazda, creation and the course of the final struggle. Gathic, later Avestan, Pahlavi and Zurvanite formulations should not be collapsed into a single sentence.

A more useful comparison asks whether an adversary is independent, derivative or permitted; whether the material creation is good, mixed or intrinsically defective; and what resolution the text anticipates. Those questions allow Jewish and Iranian formulations to be compared without defining either by a caricature of the other.

Sources: Gherardo Gnoli, Dualism, Encyclopaedia Iranica / Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin, Ahriman, Encyclopaedia Iranica.

Isaiah 45 is a strong monotheistic text, not a named opponent's manifesto

Isaiah 45 places the declaration about light, darkness, peace and calamity in a passage naming Cyrus and denying other gods. It firmly assigns the scope of those paired realities to one divine speaker. The verse does not name Zarathustra or explain an Iranian school. Describing its precise target as a proven refutation of Zoroastrian dualism exceeds what the wording alone establishes.

The contrast also matters for translation. In the selected World English Bible rendering, calamity stands opposite peace. A statement about disaster cannot automatically be converted into the claim that God morally approves every human evil. The interpretation should specify which sense of evil is being discussed.

Likewise, Ezekiel's exilic setting is not itself evidence of a fully developed Greek-Iranian synthesis. A book's narrative date, the date of its component passages and the dates of surviving manuscripts are distinct. There is no secure chronology made simply by arranging Job, Ezekiel and later mystical works in canonical order.

Sources: Isaiah 45, World English Bible / Job 1-2, World English Bible.

Plato supplies a decisive control case

In Timaeus, the maker's goodness is the reason for ordering the cosmos as well as possible. That is incompatible with the blanket claim that Platonism introduced the doctrine of an evil material universe made by an evil deity. Particular later writers could criticize or reverse elements of a Platonic cosmology; such a reversal needs explanation rather than being attributed to Plato himself.

Distinguishing intelligible reality from changing visible things is not identical to morally condemning every body. A spiritual ascent, an intellectual account of forms and an adversarial account of a lower creator can share vocabulary while making different judgments about creation.

Sources: Plato, Timaeus, translated by Benjamin Jowett.

A researchable contact hypothesis

Collins and Nati's 2024 commentary description treats the two-spirits teaching in relation to Iranian dualism, while also attending to Hellenistic associations and the plurality of manuscripts. The description makes these legitimate research subjects; it does not demonstrate every proposed borrowing. Only the publication description was available for this pass, not the full commentary.

The constructive hypothesis is a network of reception: Jewish scriptural interpretation, Iranian religious contact and Greek philosophical debate supplied resources that different groups selected and altered. The stronger genealogical claim would require a dated route, a distinctive shared feature and evidence that the proposed recipient could encounter it.

The author's Temple-trauma hypothesis belongs here as a proposed mechanism for reinterpretation after catastrophe. It should be tested against texts or motifs that may predate the catastrophe, against groups that pursued restoration rather than escape, and against competing explanations. An intellectually interesting mechanism becomes stronger when its limits are specified.

Sources: John J. Collins and James Nati, The Rule of the Association and Related Texts (2024) / Jacques Duchesne-Guillemin, Dead Sea Scrolls, Encyclopaedia Iranica / Israel Museum, Digital Dead Sea Scrolls project.
