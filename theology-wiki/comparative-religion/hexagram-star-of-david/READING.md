# Before the Star of David: The Hexagram’s Shared History

Research edition • 1 October 2026 • Theology Wiki / Comparative Religion

The familiar name “Star of David” can make a six-pointed design sound like an emblem inherited directly from the biblical king. Its documented history tells a different story: an old geometric form acquired different names and meanings before becoming a widely recognized Jewish national symbol. Art historian Alec Mishory explicitly describes the hexagram as a form that was never exclusive to Jewish tradition.[^mishory]

This wider history deserves to be visible. Armenian archaeology, Hindu sacred symbolism, Buddhist ritual imagery and Islamic objects each preserve parts of it. The central question is not simply who uses the star today, but how much of its earlier and parallel history its familiar modern name leaves out.[^simonyan2012][^himalayan][^dharmodaya][^earring]

## David’s name and the history of the shape

No biblical passage identifies the familiar hexagram as King David’s personal emblem. The association belongs to later tradition, rather than an established identification of a surviving Davidic shield. In his 1949 study, Gershom Scholem made the essential distinction: “the name and the symbol were not originally linked together.”[^scholem1949]

Magen David means Shield of David, but the history of that name and the history of two interlocking triangles are different subjects. Scholem traces the design through decoration and protective magic, not through a demonstrated royal insignia passed down from David. The name is therefore not, by itself, an origin story.[^scholem1949]

## Armenia: a Bronze Age witness and a medieval legacy

A concrete Armenian example comes from Verin Naver. Excavator Hakob Y. Simonyan’s 2012 report describes a bronze dagger-handle pommel with a six-pointed design in Grave 1A. Associated pottery is assigned to approximately 1600–1400 BCE. This is a published archaeological object, not merely a modern comparison between similar drawings.[^simonyan2012]

Simonyan’s 2025 paper adds an illustrated account: Figure 15 shows the bronze ornament, and the text reports a radiocarbon-based date of 1610–1550 BCE for the Upper Naver I:A royal tomb. That is a date reported for the burial context, not a direct carbon date of the metal. Figure 16 illustrates the motif on medieval Armenian monuments.[^simonyan2025]

Together, these publications establish a Bronze Age occurrence in present-day Armenia and a later place in Armenian architectural and funerary art. These are substantial parts of the hexagram’s history that a purely modern national reading can overlook.[^simonyan2012][^simonyan2025]

## Hindu and Buddhist sacred geometry

In Hindu tradition, the Shatkona has its own religious vocabulary. Himalayan Academy describes the opposed triangles through Shiva and Shakti, also associating them with fire and water. The significance comes from their union. Here, the form belongs to a theological interpretation quite different from a national flag.[^himalayan]

The University of Illinois’s Spurlock Museum similarly explains the hexagram in Hindu geometric symbolism as a joining of polarities. Its discussion places the design among forms used in mandalas and yantras: diagrams whose arrangements carry religious meaning. Calling every such design a Star of David would obscure that context.[^spurlock]

Related interlocking triangles also appear in Tantric Buddhist imagery. Scholar Lyudmila Klasanova describes their role in representations of Vajrayogini and the dharmodaya, understood as a source of reality. This is a particular Buddhist ritual setting, with meanings supplied by that tradition.[^dharmodaya]

## The Islamic Seal of Solomon

The Metropolitan Museum of Art preserves an especially useful material witness: an Iraqi earring, probably dating to the eleventh through thirteenth centuries, with a central hexagram. Its catalogue interprets the design as probably referring to the protective Seal of Solomon, associated with legends of Solomon’s authority over supernatural beings.[^earring]

This was not simply an incidental resemblance to a modern flag. It belonged to an established vocabulary of protection and sacred power. Scholem’s Encyclopaedia Judaica entry describes the Seal of Solomon name as widespread in Arab sources and adopted by Jewish groups. That provides historical support for Arabic influence on medieval Jewish talismanic terminology, alongside the earlier ornamental occurrences of the shape.[^judaica]

A nineteenth-century Balkan patch box in the Met offers a later example. The museum identifies its central hexagram as most likely the Seal of Solomon and explains the motif’s popularity in Islamic protective imagery. Islamic use therefore has its own long history, extending from medieval objects into the period when modern Jewish national symbolism was developing.[^patch]

The useful chronological point is clear: Muslim use of the hexagram as the Seal of Solomon substantially predates its modern Zionist and Israeli national role. This comparison concerns national adoption, rather than the first appearance of a geometric ornament in any Jewish setting.[^earring][^mishory]

## From Jewish ornament to communal and national emblem

Jewish encounters with the shape also predate modern nationalism. Scholem records ancient synagogue ornament, including Capernaum, while distinguishing such occurrences from an emblem identifying the Jewish people as a whole.[^judaica]

A particularly clear medieval witness is the Leningrad Codex. Completed in 1008–1009 CE, it includes an elaborate star-shaped carpet page, folio 474a, documented by USC’s West Semitic Research Project. A design decorating a medieval Bible manuscript is evidence of the manuscript’s art, not a biblical statement that David used it.[^tyndale][^usc]

The Jewish Museum in New York describes the decisive later development: distinctive communal use in seventeenth-century Prague, followed by broad identification with the Jewish people in the nineteenth century. Its exhibition text explicitly notes that the familiar impression of an ancient Jewish identity symbol does not match the much later development of that particular meaning.[^museum]

Mishory traces the national flag through the First Zionist Congress in 1897 and the Israeli state’s adoption decision of 28 October 1948. These are the relevant milestones for its modern national role. Roughly two centuries is thus a useful scale for its broad modern identification, not for every previous Jewish use.[^mishory][^museum]

## When modern politics changes an older symbol’s reception

There are concrete examples of the hexagram becoming objectionable because of its modern association. A 1959 Jewish Telegraphic Agency report describes Arab League boycott pressure on the Swiss watchmaker Ardath over its six-pointed trademark.[^jta]

A later case was reported by ABC News in December 2010: a star visible on an Iran Air building in Tehran through Google Earth prompted demands for removal. The report describes Iranian coverage treating it as a Zionist symbol. This documents a particular episode of rejection; it does not establish that removal was completed.[^abc]

Read alongside the Islamic museum objects, these reports support an important interpretation: modern political identification can overshadow an older, shared visual heritage. That is evidence for changed reception in particular settings, rather than a claim that Muslims everywhere ceased using the hexagram at one moment.[^jta][^abc][^patch]

## A familiar emblem can acquire an ancient biography

ANU, the Museum of the Jewish People, describes how stories about David’s shield and other ancient associations entered discussion of the national symbol. Its account also presents Scholem’s historical challenge to those stories. The gap between a compelling ancestral narrative and documented provenance is therefore itself part of the symbol’s history.[^anu]

The comparison suggests a useful way to frame the public-knowledge problem: recognizing a symbol is not the same as knowing its history. A familiar modern name can encourage readers to project a present meaning backward, even when the surviving evidence points to several different traditions.

Scholem’s study was published in 1949. This research did not have to wait for the internet or AI; what online access offers now is an easier way to place an excavation report, a museum object and an older historical essay beside one another.[^scholem1949]

## Dating notes for further research

The third-millennium-BCE claim circulating online concerns Nerkin Naver; the excavation evidence discussed above concerns Verin Naver. The older claim remains a lead to trace to an identifiable object and original report. The sources checked here establish ancient and medieval Armenian examples, not a documented, uninterrupted 5,000-year sequence.[^peopleofar][^simonyan2012]

The Hindu and Buddhist sources explain religious meanings rather than a shared adoption date of 1000 BCE. Buddhism developed later in the first millennium BCE; the Met places the historical Buddha in the sixth to fifth centuries under traditional dating, while noting later scholarly chronologies. Dating particular hexagram-bearing Indian objects remains a separate research task.[^buddha]

## Conclusion

The hexagram’s modern Jewish and Israeli significance is one chapter of a much wider history. Its name does not establish personal use by King David, and its modern visibility should not erase its Armenian, Hindu, Buddhist or Islamic contexts. The historical picture is richer than a single ancient national emblem: an enduring geometric form became meaningful in several cultures, and its most familiar modern identity developed over time.[^scholem1949][^simonyan2012][^himalayan][^dharmodaya][^earring][^museum]
