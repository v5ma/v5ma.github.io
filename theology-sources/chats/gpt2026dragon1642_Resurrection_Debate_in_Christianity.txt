In practical terms it means cancelling unpayable debts, eating with the stigmatised, blessing rather than cursing enemies, and telling the truth even when truth shames one’s own faction.  These acts compose a social echocardiogram: they reveal in real time whether a community is beating in phase with the life-giving rhythm he names Abba or whether it has slipped back into the fibrillation of rivalry.

Because the pattern is articulated in stories rather than statutory programmes, it remains portable across centuries.  Whenever a culture recognises the same cluster of symptoms—scapegoating, extractive wealth, manufactured fear—it can lay the gospel template like tracing paper over its own moment and see the lines match.  The prescription remains the same: repent, which in Semitic idiom means pivot the whole orientation of life, and then embody the reign by repairing the fractures one transaction, one boundary crossing, one relinquished privilege at a time.  

That is how the tradition “saves” across millennia without relying on timetable prophecies.  It hands down a diagnostic grammar and a therapeutic regimen sturdy enough to be re-applied whenever the old illness mutates into new cultural forms.  The survival of those stories, sayings, and enacted parables is itself part of the remedy, because they keep alive a counter-imaginary in which power is measured by how much burden it lifts rather than how much status it extracts.  To the extent a society still allows that imagination to interrogate its habits, the healing work continues in the present tense.
