Self Aware Networks GPT:
Yes. Mainstream Pauline scholarship supports the core of that description, with important nuances about what “mystical union” and “liberation” mean for Paul. The clearest textual anchor is Romans 6:3–6, where Paul says believers are “baptized into Christ Jesus,” “baptized into his death,” “buried with him through baptism,” and raised to “walk in newness of life.” Galatians 3:27 adds that those “baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ,” and 1 Corinthians 12:13 ties baptism to incorporation into the one body through the Spirit. On the back of these texts, a long scholarly line—from Albert Schweitzer’s “participationist” reading of Paul (the “mysticism of Paul the Apostle”) through E. P. Sanders, J. D. G. Dunn, J. Louis Martyn, Michael Gorman, Beverly Gaventa, N. T. Wright, and others—treats baptism as the rite that marks or mediates believers’ participation in Christ’s death-and-resurrection and their transfer from the dominion of Sin and Death to the lordship of the risen Christ. In this framework “Sin” is not merely bad behavior but a supra-personal enslaving Power; baptism signifies and, for many interpreters, effects the believer’s break with that Power because of union with Christ’s crucifixion and resurrection.

Where scholars differ is on mechanism and emphasis. Some, especially in sacramental or “effective-sign” readings (e.g., Gorman’s language of “cruciform participation” and even theosis-like union), see baptism as the moment God actually unites a person to Christ’s death and raises them into a new mode of life, which grounds moral transformation. Others, often influenced by Dunn and Fee, stress the primacy of the Spirit and faith, with baptism functioning as the decisive enacted entry into that reality rather than a “magical” act; the rite is still the normative boundary-crossing into the Messiah’s people and thus into the new sphere where Sin no longer rules. Apocalyptic readings of Romans (Martyn, Gaventa) intensify this by describing baptism as liberation from hostile cosmic powers, not a mere metaphor.

Two cautions temper Ehrman’s sweeping contrast with “modern traditions.” First, Paul never spells out a baptismal formula or rubrics; “into Christ” is his key phrase, not “in the name of X,” and while the burial metaphor fits immersion, Paul’s argument is theological rather than prescriptive about mode. Second, several living traditions actually do preserve major parts of Paul’s outlook, even if they articulate it differently: Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic theologies explicitly link baptism to dying and rising with Christ and deliverance from sin’s dominion; many Baptist and Evangelical communities preach the same participation and liberation, even when they construe baptism as a sign that accompanies Spirit-given union by faith. Those intra-Christian differences reflect the scholarly debate over whether baptism effects union, seals it, or publicly enacts it. But across those debates, the core Pauline thesis you summarized—baptism as participation in Christ’s death and resurrection that frees from Sin’s power not by human achievement but by sharing in Christ’s victory—is very well supported in the research.
