- **Mainstream Second Temple Judaism:** anticipated a *single* Davidic Messiah.

- **Implication:** The Qumran community envisioned salvation as both cultic (atonement, purity) and political (deliverance, justice).

### 1. Scriptural Ground
Deuteronomy says God will raise up a Prophet “like you [Moses] … and I will put My words in his mouth.”  
Moses’ prototype role included:
- Signs and wonders in Egypt and the wilderness (Exodus 7-12; 14-17),
- Revelation of Torah (Sinai),
- Intercession for Israel.

Therefore later interpreters reasoned that the new Prophet would be **a public miracle-worker** confirming divine revelation just as Moses had.

### 2. Second-Temple Expectations
- **Qumran** and other groups sometimes call this the *Eschatological Prophet* or *Prophet of the Last Days*—a revelatory and miracle-capable figure distinct from the messiahs.  
- **1 Macc 14 : 41** (“until a faithful prophet should arise”) shows Jews still awaited such a person to resolve legal and cultic questions—presupposing someone with divine authentication.  
- **Philo of Alexandria** (*De Praemiis et Poenis 9–10*) explicitly says the Prophet like Moses will show “signs” proving his commission.  
- **Josephus** (e.g., *Antiquities* 20.97–99) records pseudo-prophets who promised miracles in the wilderness—evidence that Jews expected a wonder-working prophetic deliverer.

### 3. Rabbinic and Later Traditions
Rabbinic texts continue to distinguish *prophecy* (which may involve miracles) from *messiahship* (kingship or priesthood). Elijah’s return in Mal 4 : 5–6 is another example: he performs wonders but is not the messiah.

### 4. Contrast with the Christian synthesis
Christian theology later **merged** the roles—one figure (Jesus) as *Prophet, Priest, King, and Messiah*—but that conflation is unique to Christianity.  
In Jewish eschatology, the “Prophet like Moses” could indeed work miracles, yet remained **a distinct prophetic office**, not a royal or priestly messiah.
