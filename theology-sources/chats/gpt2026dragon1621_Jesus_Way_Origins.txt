## Historical and Theological Background of the Two Positions

### Peter vs. Simon Magus: Orthodoxy and Heresy from the Start  
The contentious dynamic between Huff and Abke echoes one of the earliest Christian legends: the clash between the Apostle **Peter** and **Simon Magus**. In the Book of Acts, Simon the Magician is a Samaritan sorcerer who tried to buy the power of the Holy Spirit (giving rise to the term “simony”), and he was rebuked by Peter (Acts 8:9–24). Later Christian writings greatly expanded Simon’s role, casting him as the archetypal heretic who opposed the apostles. For example, **4th-century texts** like the *Pseudo-Clementine Recognitions* imagine a lengthy public debate between Peter and Simon in Rome – portraying Simon **“less as a sorcerer than as a founder of heresies,”** able to disturb even Peter with his deceptive teachings【33†L67-L72】. Early church fathers such as St. **Justin Martyr** and **Cyril of Jerusalem** record that Simon Magus claimed divine titles and taught a **“wicked and deceitful doctrine,”** even allegedly **pretending to be Christ** through magic illusions【33†L39-L47】【33†L51-L59】. In these legends, Peter (often joined by Paul) ultimately confronts and miraculously defeats Simon, reaffirming true doctrine over falsehood【33†L61-L65】.

**Applying the analogy:** In the eyes of the orthodox camp, Abke’s *Jesus Way* theology resembles Simon Magus’s role – a **charismatic teacher mixing Christian language with unorthodox ideas**, needing rebuttal by those holding apostolic truth (Huff’s side). Indeed, Huff and colleagues frequently allude to *“false teachers”* arising even in apostolic times. Conversely, Abke’s supporters might invert the analogy: they see **Huff as akin to the intransigent apostles** defending a rigid dogma, while Abke is introducing higher spiritual insights (though Abke himself would reject the Simon Magus comparison and argue he is actually restoring what Peter and James originally taught). Either way, the **emotional intensity** of the debate – with one side viewing the other as dangerously misleading, and the other claiming to represent *authentic* revelation – is hardly new. It traces to the earliest days when the church struggled to define orthodoxy versus heresy.

This refers to a real tension in the 1st century: Paul of Tarsus, who wrote much of the New Testament, took the message of Jesus to the Gentile world and taught that *faith in Jesus’s sacrificial death and resurrection* (not keeping the Jewish Law) is the basis of salvation. Meanwhile, Jesus’s **original Jewish disciples** – sometimes called “Nazarenes” or later “Ebionites” – remained centered in Jerusalem under the leadership of **James the Just** (Jesus’s brother) and, according to historical sources, maintained a more **Jewishly observant form of faith**. These Nazarenes viewed Jesus as Messiah and teacher but **continued to follow the Torah**, and notably they **rejected Paul’s authority** and theology【9†L61-L69】. Church father Irenaeus wrote that *“those who are called Ebionites… use only the Gospel of Matthew and repudiate the apostle Paul, saying he was an apostate from the Law”*【9†L67-L75】. In response, the early orthodox Church labeled the Ebionites heretics. They were “**slandered, damned, and declared heretics by the mainstream Christian Orthodox Church Fathers**”【31†L20-L28】. However, modern scholars like James Tabor argue that this group may in fact represent the **remnant of the original followers** of Jesus – the very *Jerusalem church* led by James and Peter before gentile Christianity spread【31†L20-L28】【31†L31-L35】. This fundamental disagreement – was the **post-Jesus “gospel” preached by Paul a faithful fulfillment of Jesus’s mission, or a departure from it?** – underpins many of the specific theological clashes in the debate.

### The Dead Sea Scrolls Connection: Essenes, “the Poor,” and Jesus’s Context  
A unique feature of this controversy is the *Dead Sea Scrolls* being brought into a debate about Jesus. The Dead Sea Scrolls (discovered 1947 near Qumran) are Jewish sectarian writings from around the time of Jesus (likely produced by the **Essenes**, a strict sect contemporary with the Pharisees and Sadducees). They include community rules, biblical commentaries, apocalyptic visions, and more. Now, nowhere do the Dead Sea Scrolls mention Jesus or Christianity – they are a **pre-Christian Jewish library**. However, some scholars (notably Robert Eisenman) have hypothesized parallels between the Essene community and the early Jerusalem church. For instance, both refer to themselves as *“the Poor”* (`Ebionim` in Hebrew), both had a leader teaching righteousness, etc. Abke seizes on these parallels. In the debate, **he claimed that Jesus was actually associated with the Essene movement** – essentially that Jesus (and/or John the Baptist) was part of this “fringe Jewish sect that rejected temple sacrifice and the religious system of their day”【45†L59-L67】. This would align with his broader point that Jesus opposed the Temple sacrifices (like the Essenes did) and that the earliest Christians were an Essene-like community (anti-establishment, focused on purity and ethics, referred to as “the Poor/Ebionites”). Abke buttressed this argument by showing various **quotes** – from the Dead Sea Scrolls, early church writers, etc. – trying to link terms and ideas (for example, highlighting the use of *“Ebionim”* or “the Poor” in the Qumran texts)【9†L23-L32】【9†L41-L49】. His conclusion: *if* the Essenes and Ebionites shared this anti-sacrifice stance, and *if* Jesus was connected to them, then Jesus himself likely **“rejected animal sacrifice”** and taught a message similar to what the Essenes believed【45†L59-L67】. In essence, Abke paints Jesus as an Essene/Nazarene teacher who never intended to found a new sacrificial religion – that aspect was overlaid later by others.

The **orthodox debaters responded with skepticism and fact-checking** of these Essene/Ebionite claims. A detailed analysis by one reviewer found that some of Abke’s Dead Sea Scrolls references were misused or **quote-mined**. For example, Abke cited Eisenman’s *Dead Sea Scrolls Uncovered* to imply that the Qumran sect actually called themselves “Ebionites,” but in reality **Eisenman only speculatively used the term “Ebionites”** as an interpretative label – the scrolls themselves do *not* use that word【9†L37-L45】. The fact-check notes *“Eisenman doesn’t claim they used the term ‘Ebionim’ directly. He uses it as his interpretation,”* whereas Abke’s presentation made the connection seem definitive【9†L37-L45】. Another point of contention was Abke’s assertion that the **Essenes left Jerusalem because they rejected animal sacrifice**. In fact, the Dead Sea Scrolls scholar he cited (Eisenman) actually explained that the Essenes left due to disagreements over the **Temple’s purity and calendar**, *“not because they rejected animal sacrifices”*【8†L23-L30】. Additionally, one of Abke’s Dead Sea Scroll references turned out to be nonexistent: he purportedly quoted the Community Rule text about “the poor in spirit,” but **“no such quote exists in the Community Rule (1QS) or any other Dead Sea Scroll text”** – the phrase appears only in a different scroll (the War Scroll) with a different context【8†L39-L46】. The orthodox side used these corrections to argue that Abke’s historical foundation is shaky. In their view, **there is little solid evidence Jesus was an Essene**, and the attempt to link Jesus’s message to the Qumran sect is speculative at best, if not misleading【8†L25-L33】. They maintain that Jesus participated in Jewish worship (e.g. attending Temple festivals) and that his issue was with corruption in the Temple, not the sacrificial system as such – thus he wasn’t on a crusade to end sacrifices in the way the Essenes or Abke suggest.

Nonetheless, the **Dead Sea Scrolls have influenced the tone of the debate**, giving it a detective-story flavor: Abke and Waters present themselves as uncovering hidden connections and “forgotten teachings” from 1st-century texts, while Huff and Boyce counter that these purported connections dissolve under scrutiny. The scrolls also tie into James Tabor’s contributions – Tabor has discussed the possibility of **“connections between Jesus and the Dead Sea Scrolls group”** (the Essenes). In an interview with Abke and Waters, Tabor explored parallels such as shared vocabulary and apocalyptic expectations【11†L19-L27】. However, Tabor’s position is nuanced; he often concludes that direct linkage is hard to prove. For instance, the *Treatise on Resurrection* teaches that resurrection is not of the flesh but a spiritual experience, and the *Gospel of Thomas* emphasizes self-knowledge and the divine light within, rather than salvation through the cross.

While Nag Hammadi texts were not a primary focus of the Huff–Abke debate, they form part of the *intellectual backdrop*. For centuries, our only knowledge of these groups came from polemical Church Father writings that *“portrayed them as heretics and perverters of God’s word”*【42†L69-L77】. This perspective dovetails with what Abke/Waters say about the Ebionites as well: *history is written by the winners*, so the faction that “won” (Pauline gentile Christianity) defined groups like the Ebionites or Gnostics as heretical, though those groups believed they had the true teachings of Jesus.

In the current debate, **Huff’s side staunchly upholds the canon of the New Testament as the only reliable source on Jesus**, whereas Abke’s side is open to (and indeed enthusiastic about) non-canonical texts. For example, Huff has explained *“why the Gnostic gospels are left out”* – highlighting that these later texts contain obvious legendary embellishments and doctrinal agendas that conflict with earlier apostolic writings (thus the Church was right to exclude them)【2†L23-L25】. The orthodox camp sees the Nag Hammadi scriptures as *interesting historical artifacts* but ultimately the products of sects disconnecting from the apostolic truth in the mid-2nd century. Abke and supporters like Gnostic Informant, however, see value in these texts for *what they reveal about alternative early Christian beliefs*. In sum, the Nag Hammadi and other extra-biblical texts serve as a silent undercurrent in the debate. They represent the *“forgotten” or suppressed voices* of early Christianity that the *Jesus Way* side is keen to rehabilitate, while the orthodox side warns that those voices led people away from the apostolic gospel. As one scholar summarized, the Church’s traditional view (articulated by Eusebius in the 4th century) was that **true doctrine descended in an unbroken line from the apostles, and heretics were splinter groups deviating from this truth**【42†L81-L89】. That view held largely unchallenged until the 20th century, when discoveries like Nag Hammadi prompted a reassessment of whether early “heresy” might in fact preserve authentic teachings【42†L85-L91】. This very much mirrors the argument between Huff (echoing Eusebius’s continuity-of-truth narrative) and Abke/Waters (echoing Bauer/Pagels’s idea that *the first Christian beliefs were diverse* and what we call orthodoxy may not always reflect Jesus’s intentions).

## Main Theological Disagreements  
From that historical canvas emerge specific **theological flashpoints** in the Huff vs. Abke debate. The core disagreements can be summarized in a few key areas: **the basis of salvation (Jesus’s teachings vs. Pauline atonement), the authority of Scripture, the reality of Jesus’s resurrection and miracles,** and relatedly, the person of Jesus himself. Below we detail each of these, incorporating the claims and counter-claims made by each side.

### 1. **Salvation: Jesus’s Teachings vs. Pauline Doctrine**  
**The Jesus Way position:** Abke and Waters contend that *salvation (or spiritual liberation) comes through adhering to the **teachings and example of Jesus*** – what they call *“the Jesus way”* of living. In their view, Jesus showed humanity how to attain oneness with God through love, forgiveness, inner transformation, and discovery of the divine within. They emphasize sayings of Jesus about the **Kingdom of God being “within you”** (Luke 17:21) and the importance of love (e.g. the Great Commandment) and righteousness (the Sermon on the Mount). **Importantly, they do *not* see Jesus’s death as the mechanism of salvation.** Abke explicitly argues that *“Jesus didn’t die for our sins”* and that the whole idea of **substitutionary atonement is a tragic misunderstanding**【45†L69-L73】. In their theology, Jesus’s crucifixion was more of a martyrdom – a consequence of his confrontations with the authorities (such as overturning the money-changer tables in the Temple) – rather than a preordained sacrifice required for humanity’s redemption【21†L53-L60】. They maintain that Jesus was saving people *by his life and teachings*, by revealing truth, *not* by dying. Kam Waters has said that if Abke’s and his claims are true, *“then Jesus was simply a moral teacher with provocative ideas”*【45†L102-L107】 – and they indeed lean into that notion: Jesus saves in the way that a teacher saves students, by illuminating the path of truth and how to live in harmony with God. This is sometimes called a **“Jesus as Guru”** or **“mystical teacher”** model of salvation, and it resonates with both the Ebionite view (which saw Jesus as a prophet who taught the law) and various New Age views (Jesus as an enlightened master).

**The Orthodox position:** Huff and Boyce vehemently disagree, asserting that *salvation comes only through **Jesus’s sacrificial death and resurrection***, as articulated especially in Paul’s epistles and later Christian creeds. They argue that **Jesus’s teachings, important as they are, cannot save a person if taken alone** – because no one can live up to God’s standards perfectly, and the problem of sin and death needed a divine solution. According to orthodox doctrine, Jesus’s primary mission was to *die as an atoning sacrifice* (the Lamb of God) and to rise again, thereby defeating sin and death and reconciling humanity to God. This is the essence of the Gospel as taught by Paul: *“Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures… and He rose on the third day”* (1 Cor 15:3–4). In the debate, Huff emphasized scriptures and prophecy pointing to the necessity of the cross. He likely cited passages such as Isaiah 53 (the suffering servant “pierced for our transgressions”) to show that **the idea of atonement wasn’t Paul’s invention but God’s plan foretold in the Hebrew Bible**【21†L79-L85】. The orthodox side accuses Abke’s message of gutting Christianity of its central truth. Dallas Cowan (an observer on Huff’s side) wrote that Abke is offering *“a gentler, more palatable Jesus – one who never judged, never bled, and never died to save”*, which may sound nice but **“it’s not the Jesus of the Bible.”**【45†L83-L91】 From their perspective, Abke’s “salvation by teachings” approach reduces Jesus to merely a wise man and fails to deal with humanity’s need for redemption. Boyce and Huff have gone so far as to call the *Jesus Way* gospel a **“counterfeit”**: *“what’s being offered here isn’t Jesus. It’s a reinvention of Him,”* a different gospel altogether【45†L107-L115】. They would point to Paul’s warning in Galatians 1: *“if anyone preaches a different gospel… let him be accursed.”* In short, the debate has zeroed in on **the cross**: Abke’s side says *the focus on Jesus’s death was an error* – Jesus saves through what he taught, not what was done to him – whereas Huff’s side says *the cross and resurrection are the very heart of Jesus’s saving work*, and downplaying them is profoundly un-Christian.

### 2. **Biblical Authority and Inerrancy**  
**Abke/Waters’s view:** Given their claim that early Christianity was “corrupted” by others, it’s unsurprising that Abke and Waters question the **infallibility of the Bible**. They do use the New Testament, but very selectively. For example, they give primacy to the Gospel of Matthew (which the Ebionites favored) and sometimes the Gospel of Thomas, while casting doubt on parts of the Old Testament and the writings of Paul. Abke has openly stated that some biblical texts are **forgeries or alterations**. In particular, he and Kam assert that the **Old Testament Torah was tampered with by scribes** – especially laws about animal sacrifice. They cite verses like Jeremiah 7:22 (“I did not command your fathers concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices…”) and Jeremiah 8:8 (“How can you say ‘We are wise, and the law of the LORD is with us,’ when, behold, the false pen of the scribes has made it into a lie?”) to argue that the **sacrificial laws in the Pentateuch were later insertions** not originally commanded by God【21†L43-L51】【21†L45-L53】. In Abke’s narrative, **God never wanted animal sacrifices at all**【45†L64-L72】, and thus the entire sacrificial system (including the idea of Jesus’s sacrificial death) is built on a corruption of scripture. Additionally, Abke heavily leans on non-canonical texts (Dead Sea Scrolls, church father quotations, etc.) to reconstruct history, implying that the canonical Book of Acts or Paul’s letters aren’t telling the full story. This approach effectively rejects *biblical inerrancy*. It treats the Bible as containing truth but also error, requiring modern spiritual insight (theirs) to sift through. One reviewer summarized Abke’s movement as *“a mash-up of modern New Age philosophy and Islamic revisionism with a thin layer of biblical language”*【45†L83-L91】 – notably mentioning *Islamic revisionism* because the ideas that “the Bible was corrupted” and “Jesus didn’t actually die for sins” are reminiscent of Islamic claims. Indeed, Abke’s stance on the Torah and atonement is strikingly similar to certain Islamic teachings that previous scriptures were altered and that Jesus’s crucifixion was not God’s plan. Abke likely doesn’t derive it from Islam, but from a combination of Essene lore and his own spiritual philosophy, yet the overlap is there【45†L83-L91】.

**Huff/Boyce’s view:** The orthodox side upholds that the **Bible is the authoritative, reliable Word of God**, and that its canon (agreed by the early Church under the Spirit’s guidance) is sufficient for knowing Jesus’s truth. While they might not insist on a particular theory of inerrancy in the debate, they certainly defend the integrity of Scripture against Abke’s charges of forgery. For instance, when Abke implied that the Old Testament sacrificial laws were false additions, Boyce would have countered with evidence that the Torah’s composition is far older and that sacrifices were an integral part of Israel’s religion commanded in multiple books. Huff also reportedly responded to Abke’s claims by pointing out that Jesus and the apostles **themselves treated the Scriptures as authoritative**, frequently quoting the Torah and prophets. From Genesis through Revelation, the narrative of sacrifice and redemption is woven throughout – so accusing scribes of “falsifying” that would mean God allowed the entire biblical revelation to be distorted, which Huff’s side finds untenable. In one of his videos, Huff addressed Abke’s allegations head-on, stating *“Aaron Abke says the Bible we have is based on forgeries”* (as a summary of Abke’s position) and then refuting it point by point【43†L39-L45】. The orthodox team also tends to view Abke’s use of apocryphal sources as cherry-picking. For example, Abke will trust an obscure quote from a church father or a contested interpretation of the Dead Sea Scrolls but not trust the Gospel of Mark or Paul’s letters if they conflict with his theory. To Huff, this is backward – the New Testament, backed by historical manuscript evidence and early attestation, should be trusted over later or speculative materials. Essentially, **the debate here is over epistemology**: Abke’s approach is *“mystical-experiential”* (personal revelation and eclectic sources determine what parts of scripture to accept) while Huff’s is *“sola Scriptura”* (Scripture itself is the final court of appeal, and one cannot redefine Christianity by outside sources that the church rejected). Huff’s side warns that once you throw out biblical authority, you lose any firm basis for Christian truth – and they see Abke’s theology as proof, since (in their view) it has drifted into a kind of make-your-own-Jesus syncretism. One commentator summed up Abke’s method as presenting a **“thin layer of biblical language”** over essentially non-biblical ideas【45†L83-L91】, implying that he’s reinterpreting or discarding any scripture that doesn’t fit his New Age-leaning narrative.

### 3. **Jesus’s Resurrection and Miracles**  
**Alternative side perspective:** Abke and Waters give decidedly *less emphasis* to Jesus’s physical resurrection and miracles, viewing them through a spiritual or metaphorical lens. In their presentations, the **Resurrection** is not denied outright, but it’s not central. For example, Abke might affirm that *“Christ is risen”* in a spiritual sense – as in the **continuation of Jesus’s presence or the awakening of Christ-consciousness among his followers** – but he casts doubt on or downplays the necessity of an **empty tomb with a reanimated body**. In line with the Ebionite/Gnostic tendency, they likely consider the resurrection narratives as secondary to Jesus’s teachings. Indeed, if one believes Jesus did not need to die for sins, then one might also feel that the significance of Easter is symbolic: victory of truth over falsehood, or the *presence of Jesus in the community* overcoming his death. During the debate, Huff pressed Abke on this point, and Abke’s answers were reportedly evasive or unclear, which orthodox critics interpret as effectively **denying the Resurrection’s historicity** without wanting to say so outright (as that could alienate listeners). The tone of Abke’s theology makes room for skepticism about *miracles* in general. He approaches the Bible with a view that many stories serve spiritual teaching purposes. For instance, Jesus’s *miracles* (walking on water, feeding multitudes, healings) might be seen by Abke as **“parables in action”** or events that may or may not have happened exactly as written but whose true importance is the lesson they convey (e.g. Jesus demonstrating God’s provision or the power of faith). Abke’s New Age background also suggests he might accept some paranormal or miraculous concepts (like energy healing or mystical experiences), but he doesn’t anchor faith on historical miracles. In fact, he criticizes reliance on miracles as a basis for belief. A telling sign is that Abke characterizes the orthodox focus on Jesus’s bodily resurrection as misguided – he implies it wouldn’t matter if Jesus’s bones were still in a grave, because what’s important is the *eternal truth Jesus taught*. This is similar to liberal Christian and Gnostic views where the “spiritual resurrection” (Jesus rising in our hearts or ascending in a non-physical form) is what counts.

**Orthodox perspective:** For Huff’s team, **the bodily Resurrection of Jesus is absolutely non-negotiable**. It is the linchpin of Christian faith (“if Christ has not been raised, your faith is in vain,” 1 Cor 15:17). Huff highlights that all four Gospels and Paul unanimously testify to Jesus physically rising and appearing to witnesses, eating with them, showing his wounds. The orthodox argument is that **Christianity stands or falls on the historical reality** of these events. They likely pointed out that Abke’s reluctance to affirm the Resurrection plainly is a huge red flag – a sign that his theology is outside the Christian fold. In the debate, Huff cited scriptural proofs and perhaps historical arguments (like the empty tomb, the transformation of disciples, etc.) to assert the Resurrection’s truth. Similarly with **miracles**: Huff and Boyce uphold that Jesus’s miracles were real demonstrations of his divine authority and compassion. They would argue that the Gospel writers intended to report actual events – Jesus truly healed the blind, walked on water, raised Lazarus, etc. – and that these acts reveal who Jesus is (the Son of God). Dismissing or allegorizing them, in their view, undermines Jesus’s identity. They probably accused Abke of effectively creating a *naturalistic* or *de-supernaturalized* Jesus, one who is just a sage rather than the miracle-working Son of God. This connects to the deeper christological disagreement: orthodox Christianity declares Jesus to be **God incarnate**, whose miracles and resurrection are the ultimate vindication of his divine status. Abke’s stance, influenced by New Age thought, seems to regard Jesus as an extraordinary enlightened man – perhaps filled with the “Christ spirit” that all can access – but not uniquely God in the flesh. While the debate’s focus was not explicitly on the Incarnation or Trinity, this is an underlying divide: Huff’s side would affirm *Jesus is Lord and God*, worthy of worship, whereas Abke’s rhetoric about *“Jesus never wanted worship, only for us to follow his way”* suggests a **non-orthodox Christology** (either purely human or a kind of panentheistic view of Christ that all can share). To the orthodox, denying Jesus’s bodily resurrection and miracles goes hand-in-hand with denying his deity, and thus Abke’s theology rings as **modern rehashed **Arianism** or **Gnosticism**, removing the supernatural and sacrificial elements that define Christianity.

In summary, across salvation, scripture, resurrection, and miracles, the disagreements all point to one overarching issue: **Is Jesus Christ exclusively the divine Savior whose death and resurrection accomplished our salvation (orthodox view), or is Jesus primarily a spiritual teacher who taught a path to God and whose legacy was later distorted (Abke’s view)?** Each specific theological argument ties back into this fundamental divergence.

## Accusations and Criticisms: “New Age Jesus Plus” vs “Jesus Only” Christianity  
Throughout the debate, each side has levied strong **criticisms and warnings** about the other’s theology. A recurring accusation from the orthodox camp is that Aaron Abke is preaching a *“New Age Jesus Plus”* theology – meaning he takes **Jesus and adds a grab-bag of other spiritual ideas** (Eastern mysticism, New Age philosophy, even bits of Islam or Gnosticism). This phrase “Jesus Plus” suggests that Abke isn’t satisfied with *“Jesus only”* as presented in Scripture, but rather combines Jesus with concepts of chakras, law-of-one cosmology, reincarnation or karma-like beliefs, etc., effectively creating a **hybrid spirituality**. Dallas Cowan’s review described Abke’s message as *“a redefinition of God – a mash-up of modern New Age philosophy and Islamic revisionism with a thin layer of biblical language.”*【45†L83-L91】 In other words, from the orthodox perspective, Abke has constructed a *bespoke religion*: he uses Christian terms (Jesus, Christ, Kingdom) but pours very un-Christian meanings into them, drawn from New Age teachings. For example, Abke speaks of “Christ consciousness” (a New Age concept that the divine Christ spirit can awaken in anyone) which is foreign to biblical Christianity. He also echoes the **“Law of One”** (a channeled New Age text) by emphasizing oneness and suggesting Jesus taught esoteric wisdom secretly. Huff and others have not hesitated to label this as **outright heresy or deception**. They argue that by adding so much, Abke actually **subtracts the core** of who Jesus is. As Cowan bluntly put it, *“what’s being offered here isn’t Jesus. It’s a reinvention of Him.”*【45†L105-L113】 The term “counterfeit gospel” has been used to warn Christians that Abke’s attractive message is not the true Gospel of Christ, but a fake that might lead them away from salvation【45†L107-L115】.

On the flip side, Abke and Waters accuse the orthodox side of **clinging to man-made doctrines and fear-based religion**. They often frame Huff’s brand of Christianity as *“church Father orthodoxy”* that corrupted Jesus’s simple message. In their view, Huff represents a *dogmatic, closed-minded approach* that elevates a book (the Bible) and later theological formulas (Nicene creed, etc.) above the living teachings of Jesus. One could say Abke sees Huff’s faith as **“dead orthodoxy”** – all about a Jesus to worship rather than *a Jesus to emulate*. In their response video titled “Responding to False Accusations by Wes Huff,” Abke and Waters addressed claims Huff made about them. They felt Huff misrepresented their positions to make them sound worse (the classic strawman tactic). For instance, Huff might have insinuated that Abke doesn’t even believe in Jesus or that he thinks he’s channeling spirits (exaggerations that Abke would deny). Abke’s team complained that Huff **changed debate rules last-minute** and wasn’t fair in his approach【27†L39-L42】. They also counter that what Huff calls “New Age” is actually not new at all – it’s how the **earliest followers of Jesus (the Nazarenes)** understood their faith, with an emphasis on *mystical union with God* and *moral transformation*. Thus, Abke and Waters position themselves not as adding to Jesus, but as stripping away 2,000 years of additions (they’d say *orthodoxy* is actually “Jesus Plus Paul plus Augustine plus creeds”). In their eyes, *they* are the true “Jesus Only” movement – only Jesus’s original gospel, unencumbered by later dogma. Kam Waters said they aim to reveal the original faith *“before … Church Father orthodoxy came on the scene”*【45†L125-L131】. This naturally comes across as arrogant to the orthodox side, who see it as a nearly cult-like claim that “Christianity was wrong until we came along to fix it.” Huff and Boyce have pointed out that such a stance mirrors many **old heresies and modern cults**, from Gnostics to Jehovah’s Witnesses, who all said the church got it wrong and they have the real truth.

The **tone of these accusations** can get sharp. Huff’s allies sometimes mock Abke’s presentation as *“enlightened” fluff – a sweet poison.”* In an X (Twitter) post, Anton Wisbiski remarked: *“People in the new age prefer the sweet poison rather than the bitter truth. The comments on the Aaron Abke vs. Wes Huff debate make that painfully clear.”*【37†L38-L41】. This quote encapsulates how Huff’s side sees Abke’s appeal: *sweet, pleasant, affirming – but poisonous in that it leads souls astray*. The “bitter truth,” presumably, is the orthodox message that requires repentance, humility, and acceptance of hard doctrines like human sinfulness and the necessity of the cross. Abke’s side, conversely, accuses Huff of lacking love and spiritual insight. Many viewers noted the **contrast in demeanor**: Abke remained calm, smiling, and spoke of love and unity, whereas Huff (and especially Boyce) came off as intense, combative, even angry at times. Some commenters claimed that *“Aaron was embodying the true Jesus Way of love and compassion… glowing with light”* during the debate, whereas the apologists appeared hostile or fearful【38†L0-L8】【38†L10-L18】. One TikTok snippet even joked that *“Aaron definitely had great skin!”* – a lighthearted way of saying he looked visibly more “at peace” than his opponents【38†L9-L13】. Such observations fueled the Abke camp’s narrative that *“by their fruits you shall know them”* – implying that Abke’s loving tone vindicates his closeness to the real Jesus, while Huff’s aggressive tone betrays a spirit of religiosity rather than Christlikeness.

Of course, tone can be subjective. Huff’s supporters would argue that righteous anger against false teaching is appropriate (citing Jesus’s own harsh words against deceivers), and that kindness in demeanor does not equal correctness in doctrine. Indeed, Huff likely sees Abke’s genial front as a strategy: **“wolves in sheep’s clothing”** come smiling. Meanwhile, Abke’s side would say Huff’s anger comes from fear and dogmatic insecurity, not from spiritual strength. Thus, each side interprets the emotional and rhetorical manner of the other as evidence for their own cause.

In terms of **rhetorical strategies** observed:
- **Huff/Boyce’s strategy:** They adopted a direct, often rapid-fire approach. Huff bombarded Abke with scriptural references and yes-or-no questions (e.g., *“Do you believe Jesus is YHWH God? Did Jesus rise physically?”* etc.), trying to pin down where Abke stands. This “cross-examination” style is common in apologetics debates, aiming to expose any heterodoxy clearly. Huff also employed **appeals to authority** – quoting mainstream scholars, early Church Fathers, and of course Scripture itself to show that Abke’s position is outside the consensus of historical Christianity. At times, Huff’s side moved into **refutation mode**, dissecting Abke’s slides and sources to reveal misquotes or errors (as seen in the Medium *Fact-Checking* article【4†L42-L50】【4†L55-L63】). Their tone carried a mixture of frustration (at what they saw as Abke’s evasions) and fervor (to defend what they consider the true faith). They did not shy from strong language: calling Abke’s ideas *“heretical,” “New Age distortion,”* etc., effectively warning the audience that this is spiritual life-and-death, not an amicable academic chat.

- **Abke/Waters’s strategy:** They, in contrast, often took a **softer, narrative approach**. Instead of proof-texting everything, they painted a big-picture story – “Imagine Christianity before Paul… a community of the Poor, led by Jesus’s brother, very Jewish and spiritual… then along comes Paul…” – inviting the audience to *rethink the assumptions* of tradition. They used a lot of **historical sketching** (e.g., describing the Essenes, quoting fragments from scrolls or early writings) to give an impression of scholarly depth and uncovering hidden history. Abke tends to use inclusive language like “friends” and talks about *love, unity, and consciousness*, which can be disarming. When pressed by Huff on specifics, Abke sometimes answered the question with a question or reframed it (a tactic that frustrated Huff). For example, if Huff demanded yes/no on “Do you believe the Bible is corrupted?”, Abke might respond, *“Let’s define corruption – we know scribes have edited texts, right?”* thereby redirecting to his talking points rather than directly answering. This can come off as evasive, but it’s a common strategy when one doesn’t want to be cornered by the opponent’s framing. Abke also appealed to **moral/emotional arguments**: he spoke about church hypocrisy, people hurt by rigid religion, and why a reimagined Jesus is appealing to the disillusioned【45†L83-L91】. By doing so, he connected with viewers who feel traditional Christianity has let them down. In essence, Abke positioned his message as *the cure for the failings of the Church* – a more compassionate, open-minded spirituality that still centers on Jesus but without the dogmatic baggage.

Both sides also leveraged their **online communities** post-debate. Huff’s side (including figures like Melissa Dougherty and ministries on platforms like Facebook, Instagram) spread the word warning about Abke. Abke’s side (with supporters on YouTube, TikTok, etc.) shared clips that flattered their narrative – e.g., showing Huff “unable to answer” a question or appearing flustered, with titles like “Wes Huff Can’t Answer Aaron’s Simple Question on Blood Atonement”【43†L5-L12】. Gnostic Informant’s videos further critiqued Huff’s performance, at times ridiculing mistakes (for instance, Huff apparently claimed *“Jesus said ‘I AM YHWH’”* – something not actually quoted in Scripture, which was noted on a Reddit forum【27†L35-L38】 – and Gnostic Informant pounced on that to show Huff’s inaccuracy). This digital back-and-forth has extended the debate beyond the initial encounter, turning it into an *“ongoing” saga* across multiple channels.

## Conclusion and Key References  
The Wes Huff vs. *Jesus Way* debate is more than a one-off event; it encapsulates a broader clash between **traditional Christian orthodoxy and modern spiritual reinterpretations** of Jesus. On one hand, defenders of orthodoxy see themselves as fighting the same fight the apostles did – preserving the true Gospel against syncretism and false teachings (whether ancient Gnosticism or today’s New Age). On the other hand, Aaron Abke, Kam Waters, and their collaborators believe they are *championing a restoration* of Jesus’s authentic message – aligning themselves with the very first Jewish disciples and unveiling truths that institutional Christianity buried. The friction between these viewpoints has ignited passionate exchanges, rich in historical references and not without personal barbs.

As this report has detailed, the two sides differ on nearly every theological point: how we are saved, who Jesus is, what authority scripture has, and what role (if any) miracles and resurrection play. They even differ in style – one side coming with direct apologetics and firm boundaries of orthodoxy, the other with exploratory historical conjectures and an irenic spiritual tone. The debate’s **emotional dynamics** range from Huff’s urgency (some describe it as alarm or anger) to Abke’s calm confidence (some say too calm, as if impervious to correction). Viewers’ reactions likewise run the gamut: some feel Huff *“demolished falsehood”* with facts and scripture, others feel Abke *“embodied Christlike love”* and opened minds.

To truly appreciate the debate, one can consult the **primary sources and analyses** produced around it. Here are a few key references and where they fit in:

In closing, this YouTube-based theological debate has essentially revisited age-old controversies in a modern forum. It asks the same questions the early church faced: *Who is Jesus? What is the Gospel?* and *Which writings accurately convey God’s truth?* The participants may quote Dead Sea Scrolls or Instagram comments instead of papal bulls, but the heart of the matter remains timeless. As of 2025, the conversation is ongoing. Both sides continue to produce content, respond to each other’s points, and rally their followers. For observers, it offers a fascinating look at how **21st-century seekers and defenders are wrestling with 1st-century issues** — using all the tools of the digital age to debate what is, ultimately, the Jesus of history and faith.
