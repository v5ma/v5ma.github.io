Your statement reflects a historically supported position: the concept of the **Triune God**—that is, **one God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit**—does not explicitly appear in the **Hebrew Bible (Tanakh)** and was not a doctrine recognized in Second Temple Judaism (the context of Jesus and Paul).

Here’s a breakdown of the historical and theological development:

### 1. **Old Testament / Hebrew Bible**
The foundational creed of Judaism is the *Shema*:  
**“Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one.”** (Deuteronomy 6:4)  
This expresses **strict monotheism**, and the idea of God being plural in personhood is completely absent. The Hebrew Bible never introduces anything resembling the later Christian Trinity.

### 2. **Jesus’ Time (Early 1st Century CE)**
Jesus, as a Jewish teacher, operated within that same **monotheistic framework**. While the New Testament includes language of divine sonship and the Holy Spirit, these were not formulated in terms of a co-equal, co-eternal Trinity.

Jesus speaks of God as Father and mentions the Spirit, but the **doctrine of the Trinity**—with its philosophical precision about three co-equal persons—is **not present** in his teachings.

### 3. **Paul and the Early Church (Mid 1st Century CE)**
Paul refers to Jesus as the Son of God and to the Holy Spirit as a divine agent, but like Jesus, **Paul never articulates a doctrine of the Trinity**. His theology often blurs lines of divine status (e.g., calling Jesus *Kyrios*, Lord), but **he does not teach a formal Trinitarian doctrine**. 

Most scholars agree that early Christian beliefs about Jesus’ divinity were still in flux, and not yet Trinitarian in the later creedal sense.

### 4. **Later Development (2nd–4th Centuries CE)**
The **formal doctrine of the Trinity** began to crystallize only **after the New Testament was written**:
- **Tertullian** (early 3rd century) was the first to use the Latin word *Trinitas* (Trinity).
- The **Nicene Creed** (325 CE) and **Council of Constantinople** (381 CE) defined the Trinity as orthodoxy—centuries after Jesus and Paul.

These doctrines were forged through theological and political debates in the Romanized church, not derived directly from Jewish or apostolic teaching.
