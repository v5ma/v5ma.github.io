Totally fair question. “Kabbalah” is originally and essentially **Jewish**—but in the Renaissance some Christian humanists re-read Jewish kabbalistic texts through Christian theology. That project is what scholars call **Christian (or “Christian Cabala”) Kabbalah**.

What it was (in a nutshell)
- A **15th–16th-century Christian reinterpretation** of Jewish kabbalistic sources (e.g., Zohar, Sefer Yetzirah, Gikatilla, Recanati) to argue for Christian doctrines—especially the Trinity and Christology. Often missionary/apologetic in aim. citeturn0search16turn0search6  
- Methods included mapping **sefirot** onto the Trinity, playing with **divine names/letters** (gematria, Tetragrammaton), and treating Hebrew as a conduit of primordial wisdom—now “fulfilled” in Christianity. citeturn0search1turn0search6

Key figures & dates (yes, late 15th c.)
- **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola** (1463–1494): set the tone in 1486 with his *900 Theses*, including a block of explicitly kabbalistic claims used to support Christian theology. citeturn0search1turn0search5  
- **Johann Reuchlin** (1455–1522): developed a systematic “Christian Kabbalah” in *De verbo mirifico* (1494) and *De arte cabalistica* (1517); he mined Jewish kabbalists like Gikatilla/Recanati and even defended the study of Jewish books. citeturn0search2turn1search0  
- Others in the orbit include **Paolo Riccio/Paulus Ricius** and later Christian Hebraists who studied Hebrew and kabbalistic lore with Jewish teachers. citeturn1search3turn0search7

How this differs from Jewish Kabbalah
- **Jewish Kabbalah** (a medieval Jewish mystical tradition that crystallizes in 12th–13th-c. Provence/Castile and later Safed) has its own aims, practices, and theology.  
- **Christian Kabbalah** is a **Christian appropriation/translation** of those ideas into a Christian framework; it’s not part of the Jewish tradition and is often viewed by scholars as polemical or missionary in intent. citeturn0search18turn0search16

Bottom line: you’re right—Kabbalah is strictly Jewish in origin and practice. “Christian Kabbalah” was a Renaissance experiment by Christian humanists (late 1400s–1500s) who tried to harness Jewish mystical texts for Christian theology. citeturn0search6
