Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, usually shortened to ACT, is a modern cognitive-behavioral approach that trains psychological flexibility. It teaches people to observe thoughts and feelings without fusing with them, to accept the full range of inner experience, to stay in contact with the present moment, and to take actions that express personally chosen values. The material channelled by Esther Hicks under the name “Abraham” centres on what it calls the Law of Attraction. The teaching holds that every thought radiates a vibration which the universe mirrors back; therefore sustained attention to wanted experiences, coupled with an “emotional guidance scale” that favours relief and joy, is said to draw matching life events. Alignment with “Source energy” and deliberate cultivation of positive emotion are presented as the practical route to health, wealth, and fulfilling relationships. citeturn1search3turn1search4  

Several points overlap. Both systems invite mindful awareness of internal events, encourage noticing the felt quality of the present moment, and frame emotion as a directional signal. ACT asks whether a thought helps you live your values; Abraham asks whether a thought raises your vibration. In both cases you become an observer of mental content and you pivot attention toward a more workable or uplifting focus. That shared attentional shift can look similar in practice—breathing, grounding, naming the feeling, then choosing a course that feels expansive or value-consistent. citeturn0search3turn1search1  

The differences, however, are structural. ACT treats unpleasant emotion as a normal, unavoidable part of being human and emphasises “make room for it, then move your body toward what matters,” without promising any particular external result. The Abraham material treats sustained negative emotion as evidence of mis-alignment that, if left uncorrected, will magnetise further unwanted events; its primary recommendation is to reach for a better-feeling thought so that reality can improve. Where ACT is empirically grounded and outcome-agnostic, Abraham Hicks is metaphysical and outcome-oriented. citeturn0search2turn2search0  

Because of that divergence, mixing the two requires care. Someone who uses ACT-style acceptance to meet grief or fear may later choose an Abraham exercise—say, a focus-wheel—to cultivate optimism, but if they adopt the belief that every flicker of sadness will attract catastrophe they risk adding shame to the original pain. Conversely, rejecting the Law of Attraction while keeping its emphasis on values-congruent joy can fit comfortably inside ACT’s science-based model of behaviour change. The intersection is therefore pragmatic rather than theoretical: mindfulness, emotional literacy, and values clarification can serve either worldview, but the explanatory stories behind those practices remain distinct.  

In short, the two frameworks touch at the level of practical exercises that train attention and emotion regulation, yet they stand on different philosophical ground. ACT offers an evidence-backed method for behaving meaningfully amid life’s full spectrum, while Abraham Hicks proposes a cosmology in which steady alignment with positive emotion shapes external reality. Whether one blends them or keeps them separate, recognising that difference helps prevent confusion and preserves the strengths of each. citeturn2search4
