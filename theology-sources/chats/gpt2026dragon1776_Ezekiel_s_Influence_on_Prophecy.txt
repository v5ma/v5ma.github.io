---

### **Ezekiel → Daniel**

**Historical overlap**:  
While *Ezekiel* and *Daniel* are both set during the Babylonian exile, Ezekiel is presented as a prophet **in Babylon**, while Daniel is a Judean noble living in the **royal court**. Ezekiel may have been known to the writers of Daniel, but Daniel is likely a **later composition** (especially chapters 7–12), possibly from the 2nd century BCE.

**Shared themes and motifs**:
- **Visions of beasts and divine thrones**:  
  Ezekiel 1’s vision of the cherubim and the fiery throne-chariot strongly prefigures Daniel 7’s vision of the "Ancient of Days" and the beasts rising from the sea.
  
- **Apocalyptic structure**:  
  Both feature symbolic visions explained by angels, a structure Ezekiel initiates and Daniel refines.

- **Cosmic battle and restoration**:  
  Ezekiel's war imagery (Gog and Magog, Ezekiel 38–39) anticipates Daniel's apocalyptic wars and final judgment.

---

### **Ezekiel → Revelation**

**John of Patmos clearly draws on Ezekiel**:

- **Temple imagery**:  
  Revelation 11 and 21–22 echo Ezekiel’s **vision of a restored temple** (Ezekiel 40–48), especially in the **measuring of the temple** and the **river of life** flowing from it.

- **Apocalyptic creatures**:  
  The **four living creatures** around God's throne (Revelation 4) are directly taken from Ezekiel 1 and 10, with their multiple faces and wings.

- **The scroll**:  
  Ezekiel eats a scroll (Ezekiel 2:9–3:3), as does John in Revelation 10:9–10 — both symbolic acts of internalizing prophecy.

- **Gog and Magog**:  
  Revelation 20:8 explicitly **names Gog and Magog**, lifting directly from Ezekiel 38–39.

---

### **Conclusion**

Ezekiel was **highly influential** on both *Daniel* and *Revelation*, especially in the following ways:

- He **founded the genre** of symbolic, visionary prophecy with otherworldly creatures, cosmic judgment, and temple restoration.
- His **imagery and structure** formed the **template** for later Jewish apocalypticism.
- *Daniel* inherits his style and themes; *Revelation* amplifies and universalizes them.

So while not the only source, Ezekiel is a **central pillar** of the prophetic-apocalyptic lineage that culminates in Revelation.

In **Judaism**, *Ezekiel* is indeed considered a prophetic book with **end-times (acharit ha-yamim)** significance — especially in **traditional rabbinic** and **Kabbalistic** interpretations — but it holds a different place than *Daniel*, which is more strongly associated with explicit apocalyptic timelines in **Christian eschatology** than in Jewish thought.

### **Ezekiel in Jewish End Times Thought**

1. **Resurrection of the Dead (Ezekiel 37)**  
   - The vision of the **Valley of Dry Bones** is one of the clearest depictions of **techiyat ha-metim**, the resurrection of the dead.
   - Rabbinic Judaism sees this as a metaphor for the **national rebirth of Israel**, but also as a literal reference to resurrection at the end of days.

2. **Gog and Magog (Ezekiel 38–39)**  
   - These chapters describe a **cataclysmic war** involving “Gog of the land of Magog.”
   - Rabbinic texts (e.g. *Sanhedrin 97a–99a*, *Midrash Tehillim*) interpret this war as a precursor to the **coming of the Messiah**.
   - It's a **major part of Jewish eschatology**, although not always systematized in the way Christian prophecy is.

3. **The Third Temple (Ezekiel 40–48)**  
   - These final chapters describe a detailed vision of a **future temple**.
   - The **Mishnah, Talmud, and later rabbis** sometimes reference this as a **blueprint** for the **messianic age**, especially in mystical Judaism (Kabbalah and later Hasidism).

---

### **Daniel vs. Ezekiel in Jewish Prophetic Tradition**

- **Daniel** is *included* in the **Ketuvim** (Writings), not the Prophets (*Nevi’im*), and his apocalyptic visions are **not emphasized** in rabbinic Judaism.
  - Some rabbis even viewed Daniel as **not a prophet** in the classical sense because he did not deliver messages to the people.
  - His visions are viewed as **enigmatic** and often **avoided in Halachic discussions** about the end times.
  
- **Ezekiel**, on the other hand, is a **canonical prophet**, and his end-time visions are integrated into rabbinic **Midrash**, **Talmud**, and **liturgical readings**.

---

### **In Summary**

Yes, **Ezekiel is considered part of Jewish end-times prophecy**, especially in traditional, mystical, and rabbinic literature. His visions of resurrection, the Gog-Magog war, and the future temple form **key components** of Jewish messianic expectation — though Judaism generally resists forming rigid timelines.

*Daniel*, while respected, plays a **less central role** in Jewish eschatology than in Christian apocalyptic frameworks.
