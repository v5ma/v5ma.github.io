Yes, Carnegie’s flagship book came out before Hill’s. Dale Carnegie’s How to Win Friends and Influence People was released in October 1936, and Napoleon Hill’s Think and Grow Rich was published in 1937.

Their overlap is real, but it is more “shared atmosphere” than shared blueprint. Both books assume that inner stance shapes outer outcomes, and both treat emotion, confidence, and expectancy as practical levers rather than abstract virtues. Both also lean on deliberate mental conditioning, meaning you can train attention and habits to change results, even if Carnegie does it through social conduct and Hill does it through goal fixation and belief.

Where they diverge is what they are optimizing for and how they operationalize it. Carnegie is mostly an interpersonal engineering manual: rapport, persuasion, conflict avoidance, leadership through validation, and a pragmatic ethics of not provoking defensiveness. Hill is a success metaphysics plus a program: intense desire, faith, autosuggestion, persistence, and the “master mind” alliance, with a much stronger claim that sustained belief and suggestion are causally productive in the external world, not merely motivational. So the overlap is strongest at the level of mindset and self shaping, and weaker at the level of specific mechanisms and day to day practices.

1910. Wallace D. Wattles publishes The Science of Getting Rich. This is a New Thought prosperity text that systematizes “creative thought” into a recipe for material outcomes, and it becomes a major upstream node for later “attraction” style success literature.

This is not New Thought metaphysics in the strict sense, but it is a massive mainstream bridge for the broader “mindset changes outcomes” culture, focused on interpersonal psychology, social influence, and behaviorally actionable principles.

1937. Napoleon Hill publishes Think and Grow Rich. This moves closer to the prosperity metaphysics lane than Carnegie does, with a more explicit emphasis on desire, faith, autosuggestion, persistence, and coordinated intention as drivers of outward results.

New Thought is estimated to have begun in 1830, and the most notable figure in the historical memory since 1840 was Phineas Parkhurst Quimby. But who was talking about New Thought in 1830 before Quimby?

Self Aware Networks GPT:
When people say “New Thought began in the 1830s,” they are usually dating the upstream ingredients rather than a self identified movement that was already calling itself “New Thought” in 1830. In other words, there was not really a recognizable 1830 New Thought scene of named teachers promoting “New Thought” as a branded doctrine before Quimby. What existed instead were overlapping currents that later writers and organizers folded into the origin story.

One major current was mesmerism, also called animal magnetism, which was already circulating as a mind over body healing and trance practice. This stream predates Quimby and, in New England specifically, becomes very relevant when traveling lecturers demonstrate mesmerism publicly in the mid 1830s. Charles Poyen’s lecture tours in 1836 are often singled out in histories because they created exactly the kind of “mind can affect body” public fascination that Quimby and others then reworked into mental healing frameworks.

A second major current was religious and philosophical idealism, including Swedenborgian influence and American Transcendentalism, which strongly emphasized the primacy of spirit or mind and the authority of inner experience. This is one reason you see historians say the roots extend into the 1830s, because that is the decade when Transcendentalism takes recognizable public form and starts shaping educated American spiritual culture in ways that later New Thought leaders openly drew from.

A third current was the broader “mind cure” and self cultivation atmosphere that blended popular religion, health reform, and emerging psychology adjacent ideas. This did not look like a single school with one founder in 1830, because it was more like a marketplace of practices, lectures, pamphlets, and small circles. Quimby later becomes central because he operationalized mental healing into a repeatable practice and taught or influenced people who did publish, organize, and rename the tradition.

So if you are asking, “Who in 1830 was explicitly teaching New Thought before Quimby,” the most accurate answer is that the label was not yet the label, and the people most responsible for the 1830s “root zone” are better described as mesmerists and idealist religion and philosophy figures rather than New Thought teachers. If you are asking, “Which concrete voices were already saying mind shapes health and life before Quimby,” then the best candidates are the mesmerism lecturers and writers circulating in the 1830s, plus the idealist religious and philosophical writers whose ideas later New Thought authors repeatedly cited, especially Emerson and Swedenborgian influenced thinkers.

Self Aware Networks GPT:
Yes, Emerson is one of the key “bridge” figures, because he took a set of already circulating idealist and mystical ideas and turned them into a distinctly American vocabulary of intuition, self trust, nature as revelation, and the authority of first hand insight. That vocabulary is one reason later mind cure, New Thought, and “thought shapes reality” writers could sound simultaneously spiritual and practical without sounding strictly like church doctrine.

When you ask about Swedenborgian influenced thinkers, the central point is that Emanuel Swedenborg’s system gave nineteenth century readers a very specific metaphysical grammar: the idea of correspondences, meaning that visible nature is a structured “language” of invisible spirit, and that inner states and outer realities are linked by lawful relations rather than mere metaphor. That “lawful linkage” between mind, spirit, and world is exactly the kind of conceptual bridge that later prosperity and attraction style literature inherits, even when it drops Swedenborg’s theological frame.

In New England, one especially important Swedenborgian influence on the transcendentalist milieu was Sampson Reed. Reed was a Swedenborgian writer whose work circulated widely and argued, in essence, that nature functions as the language of spirit through correspondences. Emerson scholars have long treated Reed as an early influence because Reed’s way of talking about mind, nature, and meaning strongly resembles the conceptual moves Emerson makes when he writes about nature as symbol and as a path to spiritual truth. Emerson also engaged Swedenborg directly, praising him as a kind of emblematic “seer” while also criticizing aspects of his system, which is typical of how Emerson operated: he absorbed the engine, then refused the full apparatus.

There is also a broader set of Swedenborg adjacent intellectuals and clergy in the same New England ecosystem who mattered because they made Swedenborg’s ideas socially present, discussable, and institutionally adjacent to liberal Protestantism. That matters for origins because transcendentalism did not emerge in a vacuum; it grew out of debates among Unitarians, ministers, and Harvard affiliated intellectuals who were dissatisfied with what they saw as spiritually thin rational religion. Swedenborg’s world offered a rich alternative that still looked like it had “structure,” which appealed to people who wanted mysticism without pure anti intellectualism.

On “1930s American Transcendentalism,” the historical movement people mean is actually the 1830s and 1840s, not the 1930s. Transcendentalism in that original nineteenth century sense was a loose network of writers, ministers, and reform minded intellectuals centered in New England who pushed back against two things at once: first, a purely empirical or sense based theory of knowledge, and second, a liberal church culture they felt had become spiritually procedural. They argued that the deepest truths are accessed by intuition or inner insight, that nature is not dead matter but a living sign system that can reveal moral and spiritual realities, and that the individual conscience has authority even against social institutions. Emerson becomes the public face of this because he made the stance feel both elevated and usable, and because he wrote in a voice that invited ordinary readers to treat their own experience as a legitimate source of truth.

Institutionally, the “Transcendental Club” that forms in 1836 is often treated as the moment where those shared dissatisfactions become a self conscious circle, even if the members themselves resisted being a tidy “movement.” The club and its later publishing outlets gave the ideas social velocity and a recognizable identity, which is why historians can place transcendentalism as an 1830s formation even though its ingredients were older.

Charles Poyen’s lecture tours matter because they show how a technique of altered consciousness and “mind acts on body” demonstration culture spread before it became a philosophy. Poyen, a French physician and mesmerist, toured the northeastern United States in 1836 giving public lectures and demonstrations of animal magnetism, which we would now associate with early hypnosis and trance phenomena. These events were performative and public, but they also functioned as a technology transfer. They introduced audiences to the idea that suggestion, attention, expectation, and trance could produce striking bodily and mental effects, and they catalyzed experimentation among both physicians and clergy. In other words, they helped convert a metaphysical claim (“mind matters”) into something that looked like observable practice (“look what happens in a trance when suggestion is applied”). That is exactly the kind of bridge that later mind cure and New Thought narratives rely on: a felt sense that there is an empirical “there” there, even if the theory explaining it is contested.

Poyen’s tour is also relevant to Quimby’s later fame because Quimby encountered the mesmerism wave that Poyen helped popularize in New England, and Quimby’s subsequent work in mental healing sits downstream of that cultural moment. Different sources frame the exact nature and timing of Quimby’s relationship to Poyen differently, but the broader historical point stays stable: Poyen’s 1836 demonstrations are one of the sparks that made mesmerism and trance practices a regional obsession, and that obsession became one of the practical predecessors of later mind cure and, by extension, New Thought.

The main direction of influence runs the other way. The American transcendentalists in the 1830s were strongly shaped by German and broader European thought, rather than Germany being shaped by them in that early period. When people connect “transcendentalism” to Germany, they are usually pointing to Kant’s “transcendental” philosophy and later German idealism and Romanticism as upstream sources that helped inspire the American movement’s language of intuition, inward authority, and nature as meaning-bearing.

Emerson did become known in German-speaking intellectual circles later in the nineteenth century, and he was read and discussed in Europe. One of the most-discussed linkages is that Nietzsche read Emerson and engaged him over time, and there is a scholarly literature on how Emerson was received in Europe during Nietzsche’s lifetime. That said, even if you grant meaningful Emerson→Nietzsche influence at the level of style and certain themes, the step from there to “ideas that led to Nazism” is not a straight line. Nazism’s ideological formation drew heavily on specifically German and European currents: völkisch nationalism, racialist pseudo-science, social Darwinist interpretations, certain strains of Romantic nationalism, anti-liberal and anti-democratic politics after World War I, and a wider “philosophy of life” atmosphere in which anti-mechanistic and anti-rational rhetoric could be politicized. Those sources are structurally different from Emerson’s core project, and Emerson is not normally treated by serious histories as a major contributor to Nazi intellectual ancestry.

There is, however, a narrow and careful way to describe a “shared family resemblance” without claiming direct causation. Some nineteenth-century currents on both sides of the Atlantic reacted against cold materialism and celebrated “life,” “spirit,” organic wholeness, and intuition. In Germany, later “philosophy of life” strands and related cultural movements could be bent toward an anti-rational, mythic, biologized politics in the early twentieth century. In America, transcendentalism’s inward authority and nature symbolism usually pushed toward individual conscience and moral reform rather than ethnonationalism or racial hierarchy. So you can say that certain rhetorical motifs (anti-mechanism, anti-reductionism, organic imagery) existed in the same broad modern reaction to industrial rationalization, but that does not mean American transcendentalism “made its way into Germany” in a way that materially fed Nazism.

If what you are really asking is, “Did Nazis or proto-Nazi thinkers explicitly borrow Emerson or American transcendentalism,” the best answer is that this is not a standard or well-supported lineage. The more historically grounded story is that German thinkers were already generating the philosophical and cultural ingredients that influenced the American transcendentalists, and later German political extremism drew from its own local ideological ecosystem, only selectively appropriating or distorting figures like Nietzsche, who is himself frequently mishandled in popular “Nietzsche caused Nazism” narratives.
