# Legal Precedents on Plagiarism-Equivalent Claims

## Overview: Plagiarism vs. Legal Standards of Copying  
In law, “plagiarism” as an ethical concept does not map neatly onto a specific legal cause of action. Instead, courts evaluate unauthorized appropriation through frameworks like copyright infringement, patent infringement, or (occasionally) defamation or contract claims in academic settings. Notably, these legal frameworks can recognize copying of *ideas or concepts* even without verbatim text overlap – but only under certain doctrines and evidentiary standards. In academia and publishing, disputes over priority or uncredited idea appropriation are often handled as ethical or reputational matters, though they can spill into legal arenas (e.g. lawsuits for defamation or breach of contract following plagiarism accusations). Below, we survey U.S. and international cases and precedents addressing situations akin to “plagiarism-equivalent” claims – where two works share conceptual, theoretical, or functional similarities rather than identical text – and examine how substantial similarity, striking similarity, and functional equivalence have been treated in the absence of direct copying. We also discuss standards of proof and the legal risks of publicly asserting such “plagiarism-equivalent” claims.

## Copyright Law: Substantial Similarity Without Verbatim Copying  
**Idea–Expression Distinction:** Copyright law fundamentally distinguishes protected expression from unprotectable ideas or concepts. As established in *Baker v. Selden* (1879), the *idea* or method underlying a work is not protected – only the author’s specific expression of that idea is【21†L99-L107】. Courts have repeatedly echoed that abstract themes, concepts, or scientific theories cannot be monopolized by copyright, no matter how original. For example, in *Nichols v. Universal Pictures* (1930), Judge Learned Hand explained that similarities at the level of general plot premise or stock characters are too abstract to constitute infringement – protection “narrows as one zooms out” from expression to idea. Similarly, the UK High Court in the *Da Vinci Code* case (2006) found that even if Dan Brown had copied the *central themes* or architecture of an earlier historical book (*Holy Blood, Holy Grail*), those themes were “too general or too low a level of abstraction to be capable of protection by copyright law”【33†L203-L212】. In short, merely appropriating someone’s *idea* or theory (even if using different words or notation) is not *copyright* infringement – though it would be considered plagiarism in academia.

**Non-Literal Copying and Substantial Similarity:** Crucially, copyright infringement does **not** require verbatim copying. Under the doctrine of substantial similarity, a work can infringe “even if the wording of text has been changed or visual or audible elements are altered”【15†L202-L209】. What matters is that a substantial amount of the plaintiff’s *protectable expression* (the original selection, arrangement, or development of ideas) was appropriated. U.S. courts have developed various tests – often an “extrinsic test” analyzing specific similarities in protectable elements, and an “intrinsic test” asking if an ordinary observer would find the overall *“total concept and feel”* substantially similar. For example, in *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn* (2d Cir. 1936), the court found a film infringed a play even without identical dialogue, because numerous plot points, scenes, and sequence of events were closely paralleled – essentially the same expressive *structure* in a different form. Likewise, *Rogers v. Koons* (2d Cir. 1992) held that a sculptor who recreated the composition and concept of a photograph (a couple holding puppies) in three-dimensional form infringed the photographer’s expression, despite the change of medium.

**“Striking Similarity” as Evidence of Copying:** In many cases, direct evidence of copying (a “smoking gun”) is absent, so plaintiffs rely on inference. Typically, a copyright plaintiff proves copying by showing **(1)** the defendant had access to the original work, and **(2)** the defendant’s work is *substantially similar* to protected elements of the original. In rare scenarios, however, courts allow *similarity alone* to infer copying – this is the doctrine of **“striking similarity.”** If two works are so uncannily alike in *specific* details that independent creation is virtually impossible, the trier of fact may infer the defendant must have copied, even without proof of access【15†L226-L234】. Factors supporting striking similarity include the presence of the same complex or unique errors and patterns in both works, or “obvious or crude attempts to give the appearance of dissimilarity”【15†L239-L247】. For instance, if two research papers or software codebases share an identical unusual mistake or the same highly specific data pattern, a court might conclude it’s not coincidence. Notably, *Arnstein v. Porter* (2d Cir. 1946) established that once copying in fact is shown (via access + similarities, or striking similarity alone), the court must then assess whether the copying appropriated protected expression (i.e. was an unlawful appropriation)【21†L151-L160】【21†L165-L168】.

**Limits of Similarity Evidence:** While striking similarity can support an inference of copying, U.S. courts have also cautioned that *statistical or conceptual overlap alone* is not conclusive if the overlap is in unprotected ideas. A landmark example is *Selle v. Gibb* (7th Cir. 1984), involving a songwriter who accused the Bee Gees of plagiarizing his melody. The songs had several notes in common, and a jury initially found infringement. But the trial judge set aside the verdict, and the appellate court agreed: although the melodies were alike, there was no evidence the Bee Gees ever had access to the plaintiff’s unpublished song, and the similarity – a relatively simple sequence of notes – might have been coincidental【34†L19-L27】【34†L31-L35】. The case established that even *striking similarity between works is not enough* if the plaintiff cannot show that the similarity involves originality or that independent creation was implausible【19†L5-L13】. In other words, courts will not leap to find “plagiarism” legally without a rigorous showing that the similarity points to actual copying of protected expression rather than common ideas or coincidence. This principle was tested again in recent music cases. In *Williams v. Gaye* (Blurred Lines case, C.D. Cal. 2015), a jury found infringement based on the *overall style and feel* of Marvin Gaye’s song being appropriated, even though many musical elements differed – a controversial outcome seen as stretching the substantial similarity test. By contrast, in *Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin* (9th Cir. 2020) involving the song *Stairway to Heaven*, the court emphasized that *generic chord progressions and musical ideas* are not protectable, rejecting the infringement claim. Across these cases, the trend is that courts require a *high degree of overlap in creative choices or errors* to sustain a “non-literal” copying claim – otherwise, the claim fails because it amounts to an appropriation of ideas, not expression【33†L203-L212】.

**International Copyright Perspectives:** Other jurisdictions mirror these principles. For example, UK law, as seen in the *Da Vinci Code* trial, will not find infringement for borrowing *hypotheses or theories* from a prior work. The High Court noted there was “no *architecture* or *structure*” in the claimants’ historical book protectable as such, and that novelists remain free to draw on ideas and research of others【33†L200-L209】【33†L208-L214】. Similarly, Canadian and Australian courts require substantial reproduction of the form of expression. In practice, this means that an academic or author who repackages another’s theory in new language might be morally guilty of plagiarism, but legally, without copying the original text or unique expressive elements, there is likely no copyright infringement. Thus, the legal notion of substantial similarity can sometimes vindicate “plagiarism-equivalent” complaints (when the similarity extends to expressive detail or structure), but it also sets a boundary: **conceptual equivalence alone usually isn’t actionable**. 

## Patent Law: Functional Equivalence and Conceptual Overlap  
In patent law, the analogy to plagiarism is a competitor designing around the literal text of a patent claim while *appropriating the same inventive concept*. Patent doctrine explicitly guards against mere colorable changes through the **Doctrine of Equivalents (DOE)**. Under the DOE, even if an accused product or process does not literally infringe (i.e. it does not copy every element word-for-word from the patent claim), it may still infringe if it performs “**substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve the same result**” as the patented invention【28†L89-L96】. This test, dating back to *Graver Tank & Mfg. Co. v. Linde Air Prods. Co.* (1950), allows courts to find infringement based on functional or conceptual equivalence. For example, if a patent claim is for a **“metallic filament in a glass bulb producing light”**, a competitor who uses a **carbon filament in a plastic bulb** cannot evade liability – since the material changes are insubstantial to the core invention of an electric lightbulb. As one explainer notes, *“a plastic bulb could infringe on the patent because of the doctrine of equivalents”* – the key inventive idea (an enclosed filament producing light) is taken, even though superficially the bulb is different【29†L47-L55】. In essence, patent law recognizes **plagiarism of an invention’s concept**: copying the novel function or solution with only trivial or cosmetic alterations still violates the patent holder’s rights.

It’s important to note the evidentiary burden in proving infringement by equivalence. The patentee must typically show, often through expert testimony, that each element of the patent is met either literally or equivalently by the accused design【28†L89-L96】. Courts may consider whether the differences are obvious or whether someone skilled in the art would see them as interchangeable【29†L47-L55】. The standard of proof in patent infringement is *preponderance of the evidence* (a civil standard), but if the defense raises patent invalidity, clear and convincing evidence is required to prove the patent is invalid. When arguing equivalence, patentees must also be wary of the **doctrine of prosecution history estoppel** (they cannot reclaim via DOE what they gave up in patent office proceedings). Still, the very existence of DOE in U.S. law (and similar concepts like “immaterial variation” in Europe, recently bolstered by the UK Supreme Court’s Actavis decision) shows a legal commitment to catching “plagiarism” of inventive ideas. In effect, **functional equivalence in patent law is treated like plagiarism** – the copier cannot escape by using different notation or implementation if the core inventive contribution is taken.

## Academic and Scientific Priority Disputes: Conceptual Similarity as Evidence  
Outside formal IP law, academia has long grappled with how to adjudicate claims of idea theft or uncredited conceptual repackaging. Historic priority disputes often resemble “plagiarism-equivalent” battles. A classic example is the **Newton–Leibniz controversy** over the invention of calculus. In the late 17th century, Sir Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz independently developed calculus using different notations. When it became known that Leibniz published his work after Newton’s early (unpublished) developments, Newton’s supporters accused Leibniz of plagiarism – essentially claiming that *despite using different notation and phrasing, Leibniz’s calculus was conceptually identical to Newton’s* earlier method【1†L27-L34】. A tribunal (influenced by Newton) even issued a report in 1712 suggesting Leibniz had seen Newton’s manuscripts and “disguised” the ideas in new notation. Ultimately, historians concluded that **Leibniz discovered calculus independently**, and both men are now credited as co-inventors【31†L157-L165】. The Newton–Leibniz saga highlights a key point: shared theoretical frameworks or results can trigger plagiarism allegations, but proving intentional copying is tricky without direct evidence. The “calculus war” was essentially fought on circumstantial evidence of conceptual overlap and timing, rather than any verbatim text, analogous to modern cases built on statistical or categorical similarities rather than literal copying. It serves as a caution that **simultaneous or independent creations do occur**, so even striking conceptual similarity does not conclusively prove misconduct.

Modern science has seen similar rows. In what we might call a **“Blumberg–Miller comparison”** (a reference to a hypothetical or anonymized scenario akin to Newton–Leibniz), imagine two researchers – Dr. Blumberg and Dr. Miller – who publish papers with different wording and notation but virtually identical hypotheses, experimental designs, and results. If Miller published second and was aware of Blumberg’s work, an accusation of plagiarism-equivalence might arise: Miller *“copied”* Blumberg’s ideas with only superficial changes. In real life, such disputes often surface during peer review or in competing patent filings rather than court. For instance, there have been cases of a peer reviewer secretly using an author’s unpublished ideas, or a collaborator publishing a result without credit – scenarios resolved by retraction or institutional inquiry more often than litigation. In these investigations, evidence of conceptual duplication (e.g. **methodological equivalence or statistical fingerprinting of data/results**) can be pivotal. One high-profile example involved **statistical genetics**: a researcher noticed that a later paper’s data patterns were *too congruent* with his own earlier study’s (down to unusual statistical aberrations), strongly suggesting the later author had reused the data or method without credit. Such evidence of “uncommon overlap” in underlying approach often leads journals or universities to find misconduct – essentially treating it as plagiarism of ideas or methods.

Notably, tools and expert analyses are increasingly used to detect these non-literal reuses. Plagiarism detection software can flag high textual overlap; more sophisticated analyses (including for mathematics and code) look for structural similarity or identical errors. For example, in mathematics, researchers have developed a *taxonomy of mathematical plagiarism* to classify when two papers share unusual lemmas, notation choices, or proof structures despite different wording【35†L30-L39】【35†L53-L61】. The Office of Research Integrity defines *“plagiarism of ideas”* as *“appropriating someone else’s idea (an explanation, theory, hypothesis, or metaphor), in whole or in part, **with superficial modifications**, without credit”*【24†L25-L33】. Evidence of such idea-plagiarism might include nearly identical experimental setups or the same novel analogy used in two works. Courts have not frequently had to decide pure “idea plagiarism” between scientists (since ideas per se aren’t property), but they *have* been willing to consider **expert statistical evidence** in related contexts. For instance, in *Selle v. Gibb*, the plaintiff’s musicologist argued the probability of the Bee Gees’ song matching Selle’s melody by chance was astronomically low【19†L25-L32】. Although that wasn’t ultimately enough legally, it demonstrates the kind of *statistical equivalence argument* that might be made to infer copying when direct proof of intent is absent. Similarly, if an academic priority dispute went to court (perhaps as a breach of fiduciary duty or contract claim), one could imagine an expert showing that two scientific papers have an improbably high congruence in experimental design and data – a signal of copying. In summary, **courts and tribunals are open to circumstantial evidence based on conceptual and methodological overlap**, but they will scrutinize whether the similarity truly exceeds what independent development or coincidence might produce.

## Standards of Proof and Evidence in Such Claims  
**Copyright and Patent:** In U.S. civil cases like copyright or patent infringement, the standard of proof is generally *preponderance of the evidence* (more likely than not). This relatively lower threshold means a plaintiff can prevail by convincing the judge or jury that copying is more probable than independent creation, given the substantial similarities【21†L151-L160】. That said, courts often require *persuasive* proof of linkage when direct copying isn’t shown. For copyright, a pattern of striking similarity plus an opportunity to access can meet the burden【21†L158-L166】. For patents, showing each element is met exactly or equivalently by the accused product satisfies the burden【28†L89-L96】. Some aspects invoke higher standards: e.g. to rebut a presumption of a valid copyright or patent, a defendant may need clear and convincing evidence (as in proving a patent is invalid). But for proving the fact of copying or infringement itself, preponderance suffices. One consequence is that **statistical analyses or expert comparisons often play a key role** – they need to be strong enough to tilt the balance of probabilities. If an analysis shows a <0.001% chance that two long strings of code or two complex scholarly works align by accident, a court might find that persuasive as more likely than not indicating copying (barring an alternative explanation like common source or standard method).

**Academic Misconduct Proceedings:** Outside the courtroom, university disciplinary bodies handle plagiarism findings using their own standards – often *“preponderance of evidence”* as well, though some institutions opt for *“clear and convincing”* for serious research misconduct. These are quasi-judicial processes where statistical plagiarism reports (like a Turnitin score or textual similarity index) may be considered alongside qualitative expert judgment. The **Newton–Leibniz analogy** suggests caution: even a strong circumstantial case needs critical scrutiny, and independent discovery is a real possibility. Thus, academic panels often look for *additional evidence of access or intent*, such as one researcher having reviewed the other’s grant proposal or manuscript. Absent such evidence, they may refrain from a definitive “plagiarism” label and instead call it a dispute over independent development. The standard of proof in academia is generally lower than “beyond reasonable doubt,” and there is no requirement to prove *intent* to plagiarize – uncredited copying, even if “innocent,” can violate policies. For example, in a Harvard Law School plagiarism disciplinary case, the student argued she hadn’t *intended* to submit the copied material as final, but the school enforced its strict policy that **“[a]ll work submitted by a student… is expected to be the student’s own”**, even unintentional copying【36†L121-L128】【36†L133-L142】. The First Circuit upheld the discipline, indicating that by the school’s contract-like handbook, the finding of plagiarism (by a preponderance standard) was proper and not an “unfair” application of the rule【36†L139-L148】【36†L149-L157】.

**Defamation and “Plagiarism” Accusations:** When plagiarism allegations end up in court, it’s often via defamation suits. Someone publicly accused of plagiarism may sue, claiming the accusation is false and damaging. Here, the standard of proof can become a pivotal issue. If the accuser is a media outlet, or the matter is of public concern, U.S. law may require the plaintiff to prove *actual malice* (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth) by **clear and convincing evidence**. This is a higher bar than preponderance. For example, a recent case in Arizona involved a professor (*Rodriguez v. Taylor & Francis*, 2024) who sued a journal publisher after it retracted his article for plagiarism of another’s work【26†L83-L92】【26†L119-L127】. The retraction notice cited **“substantial overlap… particularly in title, references, and ideas”** and declared that *“as plagiarism is a serious breach of publishing ethics, we are retracting the article”*【26†L119-L127】. The professor denied wrongdoing and filed a libel claim. The court dismissed the suit, finding that he failed to plausibly allege *actual malice* on the part of the publisher【26†L137-L145】. Notably, the judge observed there was “no apparent dispute” that the two articles *did* share a title and some content, and that *“plagiarism does not require a complete identity between articles”* – minor differences don’t immunize one from the charge【26†L141-L149】. In other words, the essence of the plagiarism-equivalent claim (conceptual and reference overlap) was basically true, or at least not made with reckless disregard for truth. The court concluded that the author’s general denials were insufficient to show the publisher knew its plagiarism finding was false【26†L142-L150】. This case illustrates that if a plagiarism accusation has a factual basis (e.g. documented conceptual similarities), a defamation claim will be hard to sustain, especially for a public figure or academic where First Amendment protections and the **opinion defense** come into play. U.S. courts often consider accusations of plagiarism in academic contexts as potentially “opinion” or subjective evaluative statements, which get latitude if they have some grounding in disclosed facts (here, the overlapping content was the disclosed fact)【26†L141-L149】.

Internationally, defamation laws vary, but truth remains a universal defense. For instance, in the UK an accuser would need to show the allegation is *substantially true* to avoid liability. In cases of genuine plagiarism evidence, that may be feasible. Moreover, many countries recognize a qualified privilege for communications in academic or evaluative settings. If an academic journal, university committee, or grant board voices a misconduct finding, that statement might be privileged from defamation claims so long as it was made in good faith and through proper channels. For example, Germany saw high-profile plagiarism scandals (e.g. defense minister Karl-Theodor zu Guttenberg’s 2011 resignation after his doctoral thesis was found plagiarized). While the consequences were largely political and academic (his PhD was revoked)【37†L23-L30】, had he sued a professor or wiki community for libel, they likely could have proven the extensive verbatim overlap in his thesis – truth as absolute defense. In sum, the **legal risk of publicly accusing plagiarism lies primarily in defamation**, but if one has solid evidence of substantial similarity or idea appropriation, and frames the accusation in factual terms, the risk is mitigated. Courts have been clear that calling something “plagiarism” isn’t actionable if the core facts (significant overlap without credit) are accurate or expressed as an opinion based on disclosed facts【26†L141-L149】.

## Legal and Evidentiary Implications of a “Plagiarism-Equivalent” Framework  
Using a framework of *statistical and categorical equivalence* to call out plagiarism – essentially saying “Work B is in the same category or has the same statistical fingerprint as Work A, therefore B is plagiarism-equivalent to A” – is a bold approach that straddles law and public discourse. Such a framework might borrow methods from copyright’s substantial similarity test or patent’s doctrine of equivalents, but apply them to broader contexts (e.g. comparing academic papers for idea theft). Legally, this approach must navigate the following:

- **Evidentiary Support:** The credibility of a plagiarism-equivalent claim will depend on the evidence behind the statistical or categorical analysis. If one can demonstrate, for example, that every section of Author B’s paper corresponds to a section in Author A’s earlier paper – with the same sequence of arguments and even the same unique missteps – that *categorical mapping* is powerful evidence. It mirrors the idea of showing “the same errors or idiosyncrasies” in both works, which courts have cited as hallmarks of copying【15†L239-L247】. Such evidence can bolster a case or a public allegation. However, if the analysis is more abstract (e.g. “both works address the same broad categories in psychology”), it may not satisfy legal standards and could be attacked as mere conjecture. In any legal setting, an expert would need to explain that the probability of such extensive conceptual overlap arising independently is extremely low – essentially using statistics as circumstantial proof of copying intent or access.

- **Standards of Proof:** As noted, plagiarism-equivalent claims in court would usually be civil matters with a preponderance standard. That means the trier of fact must be convinced that copying is more likely than not. Statistical arguments can help quantify “more likely than not.” For instance, an expert might say “there is a 95% likelihood these two works were not independently created.” But courts will also consider alternative explanations (common sources, constraints of the field, etc.). In academic misconduct hearings, the bar might be met if reviewers simply find the works “strikingly similar” in the unique approach – a more relaxed and discretionary determination.

- **Intent vs. Impact:** One tricky aspect of plagiarism-equivalence framing is that it sidesteps direct evidence of *intent*. It treats *outcome* similarity as a proxy for copying. Legally, copyright infringement doesn’t require wrongful intent (even subconscious copying is infringement【34†L19-L27】), and academic plagiarism policies likewise don’t require intent. So focusing on equivalence in content is acceptable; one need not prove the second author meant to cheat, only that the work is effectively the same intellectual product. However, publicly accusing someone of plagiarism does carry the implication of intentional dishonesty. This is where legal risk can increase – an accused person might admit similarity but vehemently deny intent, arguing it was coincidence or at most negligence. To mitigate this, accusations could be phrased in terms of factual overlap (“Work B reproduces the core ideas and structure of Work A without credit”) rather than moral condemnation. Sticking to what can be objectively shown (e.g., using statistical measures or side-by-side comparisons) makes it easier to defend the statement as true or as a fair opinion.

- **Defamation and Fair Comment:** As discussed, if a plagiarism-equivalent claim is made publicly, the speaker should be prepared to defend it with evidence. In many jurisdictions, calling someone a plagiarist is defamatory *per se* (it impugns their integrity in their profession). So one must either prove the truth of the allegation or fit it into a protected category of speech. In the U.S., a well-founded plagiarism comparison presented as an *opinion (“In my analysis, these works are so alike that this is plagiarism”) backed by disclosed facts* can fall under the **opinion defense** or fair comment on a matter of public interest【11†L15-L23】【11†L31-L39】. The Rodriguez case mentioned above demonstrates that courts look at whether you had a reasonable basis: the journal’s statement was presumably deemed a fair and substantiated comment, not a reckless smear, given the documented overlap【26†L141-L149】. Additionally, academic speech may sometimes be shielded by *common-interest privilege* (e.g. a confidential tenure review alleging plagiarism would be privileged among the committee).

- **Potential for Countersuits (SLAPP considerations):** If a powerful figure is accused, they might file a lawsuit to intimidate (a SLAPP – Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation). Many jurisdictions now have anti-SLAPP statutes that allow early dismissal if the speech was on a public issue and the plaintiff can’t show probable validity of their claim. Accusations of research misconduct could be seen as commentary on a matter of public importance (academic integrity), potentially invoking anti-SLAPP protection. This adds a layer of protection for those raising plagiarism-equivalent concerns in good faith.

- **Moral Rights and International Wrinkles:** In some countries, authors have *moral rights* including the right of attribution. Presenting another’s ideas as one’s own can violate moral rights even if no verbatim text was copied. For instance, French copyright law might treat certain egregious instances of idea plagiarism as an affront to the author’s *droit d’auteur*, especially if the original author’s reputation is harmed by lack of credit. While uncommon, there have been cases in continental Europe where scholars pursued legal remedies for uncredited use of their research – framed not as copyright infringement (if no text copied) but as a violation of their personal authorial rights or unfair competition law. These avenues remain narrow, but they underscore that the legal landscape abroad can sometimes accommodate claims closer to plagiarism (as opposed to strict U.S. idea-expression doctrine). Nonetheless, even in those systems, the **evidence must show a substantial appropriation of intellectual value** – essentially the same threshold of conceptual equivalence.

In conclusion, a “plagiarism-equivalent” claim – say, asserting that *“Professor Miller’s 2021 paper is categorically and statistically equivalent to Dr. Blumberg’s 2019 article, despite different wording, indicating plagiarism”* – would find support in various legal concepts: **substantial similarity** in copyright (if expression overlaps), **striking similarity** as circumstantial evidence of copying, and the patent-like notion that merely rephrasing an invention or idea doesn’t escape liability. Courts have shown willingness to entertain these arguments when properly supported. At the same time, they remain cautious: they require that the similarity be truly *probative* of copying and not merely reflective of common ideas or simultaneous discovery. Using such a framework publicly carries some risk, but that risk can be managed by grounding the claims in concrete evidence and truth. As the cases and examples above demonstrate, when plagiarism-equivalent allegations have a firm basis – *unusual similarities that cannot be explained away* – the law tends to side with the accuser (or at least shield them), whether through finding infringement or dismissing defamation claims. Conversely, where the similarities dwell in the realm of theory or idea without unique overlapping expression, the law provides a shield to the accused, effectively saying: *you may have a case of academic plagiarism, but not a legal one*.
