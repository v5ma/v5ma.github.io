---

### **1. Was James the Just a Gnostic?**
**Not in the traditional sense of Gnosticism** that flourished in the 2nd and 3rd centuries CE. However:

- **Proto-Gnostic Elements:** Some of the **Nag Hammadi texts**, such as *The Apocryphon of James* and *The First Apocalypse of James*, portray James as a key recipient of secret teachings from Jesus. These texts are Gnostic or Gnostic-adjacent, which suggests **James held a special place in some Gnostic traditions**.
- **Jewish-Christian Gnosis:** The **earliest followers of Jesus**, including James, practiced a form of Jewish Christianity that emphasized **inner spiritual transformation**, **strict ethical conduct**, and **revelation through mystical insight** — themes which **overlap with later Gnostic beliefs**, but without the radical dualism of matter vs. spirit found in Valentinian or Sethian Gnosticism.

👉 **Conclusion:** James was not a Gnostic in the classical sense, but some early Gnostic groups revered him, and his teachings may have contained proto-Gnostic themes.

---

### **2. Was James the Just a Nazarene?**
**Yes.** James was a Nazarene in the **historical and sectarian sense**:

- The **Nazarenes** were a **Jewish-Christian sect** who followed Jesus, observed the **Torah**, and rejected Pauline Christianity.
- Early Church Fathers (e.g. Epiphanius) describe the Nazarenes as **Torah-observant Christians** who believed Jesus was the Messiah but not divine in the same sense Paul taught.
- James, as **leader of the Jerusalem Church**, represented the **Jewish Christian faction** that upheld circumcision, kosher laws, and Temple attendance — key traits of the Nazarenes.

👉 **Conclusion:** James was arguably the **primary leader of the Nazarene movement** in Jerusalem after Jesus’ death.

---

### **3. Was James the Just a Vegan?**
Probably **not a vegan in the modern sense**, but possibly a **vegetarian** or followed **ascetic dietary restrictions**:

- **Hegesippus**, an early Christian chronicler cited by Eusebius, writes that James was **holy from birth**, drank no alcohol, and **ate no meat**, suggesting a form of vegetarianism.
- The **Epistle of the Apostles** and other Jewish-Christian writings portray James as **extremely ascetic**, possibly in line with the **Essenes or Ebionites**, some of whom practiced vegetarianism.
- The **Gospel of the Ebionites**, a lost Jewish-Christian gospel known only from Epiphanius’ quotes, describes Jesus as rejecting animal sacrifice — a theme that likely influenced James as well.

👉 **Conclusion:** James was very likely **vegetarian**, perhaps for **ritual purity or ascetic reasons**, but not vegan in the full dietary sense we understand today.

**"The First Messiah"** is a phrase often used to refer to **James the Just**, especially in the work of **Robert Eisenman**, whose book *The New Testament Code* and earlier *James the Brother of Jesus* argue that **James — not Jesus — was the real historical leader of the original messianic movement in Jerusalem**.

Here’s a detailed breakdown of what **“The First Messiah”** means in this context:

---

## 🕊️ Who Was "The First Messiah"?

### 🔹 James the Just — *Not Jesus*?
- Eisenman and other scholars suggest that **James**, not Jesus, was the **true founder of the Jerusalem Jesus movement** as it was known in history — and that **Jesus’ legacy was reshaped** through the lens of Paul and later Gentile writers.
- James, being Jesus’ **biological brother** (as attested by sources like Josephus and early Church Fathers), **inherited leadership** of the early church — not Peter or Paul.
- **James was the public face** of the earliest "Messianic" movement: Jewish, Torah-observant, and nationalistic (possibly aligned with Zealots or Essenes).

### 🔹 What "Messiah" Meant in Context
- The **Hebrew word “Messiah” (Mashiach)** meant “anointed one,” typically a **king, priest, or prophet** chosen to restore Israel.
- James fit the mold of a **righteous priestly leader** more than a suffering savior.
- In the **Book of Acts**, James is shown in charge of the Jerusalem Church — overseeing disputes with Paul and enforcing Torah-based rulings (*Acts 15*).

---

## 📜 Sources Supporting James as Messiah

### 🧱 1. **Josephus (Antiquities 20.9.1)**  
- James is described as **“the brother of Jesus who was called the Christ”** and **a righteous man** unjustly executed.
- His **death sparked public outrage**, possibly contributing to the First Jewish-Roman War.

### 📖 2. **Eusebius & Hegesippus**  
- Report that James wore **priestly garments**, prayed so much his knees resembled camel’s knees, and was allowed in the **Holy of Holies**, which only priests could enter.
- James was martyred around **62 CE** by being **thrown from the Temple** and stoned — considered by many to be the **first martyr of the Christian faith**, not Stephen.

### 📘 3. **Dead Sea Scrolls Parallel**  
- Eisenman sees parallels between the **Teacher of Righteousness** (in DSS) and **James**, rather than Jesus.
- James’ teachings, asceticism, and martyrdom resemble the **Qumranic sect’s vision** more than Paul’s Hellenistic theology.

---

## ⚖️ Why Was James Forgotten?

### ✝️ 1. **Pauline Christianity Took Over**
- Paul shifted focus from **Jewish law to faith in the crucified and risen Christ**.
- As Gentile Christianity grew, **James and the Jewish-Christian leaders were minimized**.

### 📜 2. **Gospels Written Later**
- By the time the Gospels were written (Mark c. 70 CE, John c. 100 CE), **the institutional church had already been Hellenized**.
- James was sidelined in favor of Peter and Paul.
