The oldest strata of the Hebrew Bible are poetic fragments that appear to pre-date the monarchy.  Verses such as the Song of Deborah in Judges 5, the Blessing of Jacob in Genesis 49, and Moses’ Blessing in Deuteronomy 33 are often placed in the late twelfth to early tenth centuries BCE, when the text was still transmitted orally.  During the tenth century—traditionally the era of David and Solomon—scribes in Judah produced material now called the Yahwist, or “J”, source, while a generation later northern scribes produced the related Elohist, or “E”, source. In the eighth century BCE the first writing prophets—Amos and Hosea in the north and the author of Isaiah 1–39 in Judah—began to circulate short scrolls of oracles.  The late seventh century saw the composition of Deuteronomy and the compilation of the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua through 2 Kings) in Jerusalem, closely tied to the reforms of King Josiah.  The Babylonian conquest (587 BCE) produced exilic additions: Jeremiah’s prose sermons, the Priestly legislation in Exodus 25–Numbers 36, Ezekiel’s visions, and the great exilic poet now called Deutero-Isaiah (Isaiah 40–55).  A third Isaian collection (chapters 56–66) reflects conditions in the early Persian period.  

Under Persian rule (fifth to fourth centuries BCE) Ezra–Nehemiah, 1–2 Chronicles, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, much of the Psalter, and Wisdom books such as Job and Proverbs reached their final forms.  Ecclesiastes and the Hebrew portions of Daniel belong to the early Hellenistic era; Daniel’s Aramaic visions and the apocalyptic tales of Susanna, Bel and the Dragon, and 1 Enoch cluster in the second century BCE, the Maccabean crisis that also produced 1–2 Maccabees, Judith, and Tobit.  

During exactly that period Jewish scholars in Alexandria began translating the Law into Greek.  The Pentateuch was rendered first, probably between about 280 and 250 BCE, followed over the next two centuries by the Prophets, the Writings, and the newer Greek compositions just mentioned.  Collectively these Greek scriptures came to be called the Septuagint. citeturn0search1  

At Qumran on the Dead Sea, a break-away priestly community copied almost every biblical book—Esther is the lone exception—along with dozens of sectarian writings such as the Community Rule, the War Scroll, the Thanksgiving Hymns, and pesharim (commentaries) on Habakkuk, Isaiah, and other prophets.  The surviving manuscripts span the mid-third century BCE through the first century CE and preserve our earliest physical witnesses to most biblical books. citeturn0search4  

Within living memory of those latest Qumran scrolls, Saul of Tarsus—better known as Paul—wrote letters to Thessalonica, Corinth, Galatia, Rome, Philippi, and other congregations between about 49 and 67 CE.  His authentic correspondence predates every canonical Gospel.  Around 70 CE Mark’s Gospel was composed, followed a decade later by the sayings-rich Matthew and the historiographical Luke–Acts; John and Revelation closed the first-century canon, although the Pastoral Epistles, 2 Peter, and the Johannine letters probably belong to the early second century.  

That same second century produced the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip, the Secret Book of John, the Apocalypse of Peter, and dozens of other mystical or philosophical dialogues.  Composed largely in Greek and Syriac, they circulated among diverse Jewish-Christian and proto-Gnostic circles.  Sometime after 350 CE a monastic library in Upper Egypt copied Coptic translations of fifty-two of these treatises into thirteen codices. Back in the Greek-speaking church, the fourth and fifth centuries produced uncial codices such as Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, which fixed the Septuagint and the New Testament in the broad form still printed today.  In the Latin west, Jerome’s Vulgate (c. 382–405 CE) replaced competing Old Latin versions.  Meanwhile rabbinic scholars in Tiberias, Sura, and Pumbedita devoted themselves to an authoritative Hebrew recension.  The Tiberian Masoretes finalised vowel pointing, accentuation, and marginal notes between the seventh and tenth centuries CE; their standard text, exemplified by the Aleppo (c. 930 CE) and Leningrad (1008 CE) codices, underlies every modern Hebrew Bible. citeturn0search2  

Thus the written story begins with fragments of pre-monarchic poetry and ends a full two millennia later with vowel marks that stabilise pronunciation. The Earliest Hebrew Poetry emerged during the Late Bronze Age, roughly between 1200 BCE and 1000 BCE.  Verses such as the Song of Deborah in Judges 5, the Blessing of Jacob in Genesis 49, and the Blessing of Moses in Deuteronomy 33 represent fragments of an oral tradition that pre-dates Israel’s monarchy and later became written scripture.  These poems are the oldest identifiable layer of what later Jews and Christians regarded as Bible.  

The First Great Narrative Sources of the Pentateuch were woven in the United Monarchy and early divided-kingdom period, between about 1000 BCE and 800 BCE.  Southern scribes produced the “Yahwist” cycles, northern writers the related “Elohist” cycles; together they supplied core stories now found in Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, and Samuel–Kings.  

The Deuteronomistic History and Deuteronomy themselves crystallised in the late seventh century BCE, probably under King Josiah, when court scholars edited Joshua through 2 Kings into a single theological narrative and prefaced it with the law code of Deuteronomy.  

The Exile and Early Persian Period (587–450 BCE) yielded the Priestly source of the Torah, Ezekiel’s visions, large blocks of Jeremiah, and Isaiah 40–55 (Deutero-Isaiah), with Isaiah 56–66 following as Jews returned from Babylon.  

The Later Persian Era (fifth–fourth centuries BCE) finalised Ezra-Nehemiah, 1–2 Chronicles, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, much of the Psalter, and Wisdom collections such as Job and Proverbs.  Ecclesiastes and the Hebrew chapters of Daniel belong at the dawn of Hellenistic rule.
