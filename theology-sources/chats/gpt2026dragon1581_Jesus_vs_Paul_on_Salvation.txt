You're right to highlight the tension between Jesus's direct teachings in the Synoptic Gospels and Paul’s theological framework in his letters. This tension is especially apparent on the question of *how one is saved*.

When Jesus is asked how to inherit eternal life (e.g. in Matthew 19:16–22, Mark 10:17–22, and Luke 10:25–28), his responses typically align with Jewish ethical teaching and the Prophets: keep the commandments, love God, and love your neighbor. For example, in Luke 10, Jesus endorses the Shema and Leviticus 19:18 as the answer.

By contrast, Paul, especially in letters like Romans and Galatians, emphasizes *justification by faith apart from works of the law*. For Paul, salvation comes through faith in the death and resurrection of Jesus as a divine savior figure—often sidelining ethical commandments in favor of what he saw as a universalized, grace-based redemption.

Scholars like James Tabor, John Dominic Crossan, and E. P. Sanders (though with differing views) have pointed out that Jesus and Paul were speaking in different contexts. Jesus was addressing fellow Jews within the framework of Second Temple Judaism, while Paul was constructing a new theology for Gentile converts after Jesus’s death.

So your observation that Jesus’s message aligns more with prophets like Micah—"What does the Lord require of you but to do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with your God"—is historically grounded. Paul's gospel, by contrast, became the theological foundation for much of Christianity, especially after the canonization of his letters.

You’re tapping into a deep and unresolved conflict at the heart of Christian origins: the divergence between the historical Jesus’s Jewish prophetic voice and Paul’s cosmic Christ theology.

Was Isaiah a prophet?

Self Aware Networks GPT:
Yes, Isaiah is considered one of the major prophets in the Hebrew Bible (Old Testament). His writings are found in the Book of Isaiah, which is traditionally attributed to him and is one of the longest and most theologically rich prophetic books in the Bible.

The historical Isaiah likely lived in the 8th century BCE during the reigns of several kings of Judah, including Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah. He prophesied primarily in Jerusalem and addressed both political and spiritual issues, warning against idolatry, injustice, and alliances with foreign powers while also offering hope for redemption.

However, most modern scholars divide the Book of Isaiah into at least two or three major sections, which likely came from different time periods:

1. **First Isaiah (chapters 1–39)** – Generally attributed to the historical prophet Isaiah of Jerusalem.
2. **Second Isaiah (chapters 40–55)** – Often seen as written during the Babylonian exile, by an anonymous prophet in the spirit of Isaiah.
3. **Third Isaiah (chapters 56–66)** – Believed to be from the post-exilic period, addressing the return to Jerusalem and the community’s restoration.

Despite these layers, all sections are traditionally unified under the name Isaiah, and the figure of Isaiah is revered as a prophet in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

How did Isaiah, Micah, and Jesus agree?

Self Aware Networks GPT:
Isaiah, Micah, and Jesus all share a consistent prophetic voice rooted in **ethical monotheism**, **social justice**, and a **heart-centered relationship with God**—often in contrast with ritualistic or performative religion. Here's how their teachings align:

### 1. **Justice, Mercy, and Humility**
**Micah 6:6–8** famously distills God’s expectations:
> “He has shown you, O man, what is good.  
> And what does the Lord require of you?  
> To act justly and to love mercy  
> and to walk humbly with your God.”

This echoes throughout Isaiah and Jesus’s teachings. It shifts the focus from ritual sacrifice to ethical living and genuine humility before God.

---

### 2. **Condemnation of Hypocrisy and Injustice**
**Isaiah 1:11–17** critiques empty religious rituals:
> “I have had enough of burnt offerings...  
> Stop bringing meaningless offerings!  
> ...Learn to do right; seek justice.  
> Defend the oppressed.  
> Take up the cause of the fatherless; plead the case of the widow.”

Jesus channels this same energy in Matthew 23:
> “Woe to you, teachers of the law and Pharisees, you hypocrites!  
> You give a tenth of your spices... but you have neglected the more important matters of the law—justice, mercy and faithfulness.”

---

### 3. **Love of God and Neighbor**
Jesus in **Mark 12:28–31**:
> “The most important [commandment]... ‘Love the Lord your God with all your heart’... The second is this: ‘Love your neighbor as yourself.’ There is no commandment greater than these.”

This aligns with the **core of Isaiah’s and Micah’s critique**—that without love and justice, religious observance is meaningless.

---

### 4. **A Vision of Peace and Unity**
**Micah 4:1–4** and **Isaiah 2:2–4** share nearly identical visions of future peace:
> “They will beat their swords into plowshares  
> and their spears into pruning hooks.  
> Nation will not take up sword against nation,  
> nor will they train for war anymore.”

Jesus echoes this peaceful kingdom in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5):
> “Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God.”

---

### 5. **Concern for the Marginalized**
Isaiah, Micah, and Jesus all emphasize **care for the poor, the outcast, and the oppressed**. Jesus’s ministry is filled with healing, inclusion, and dining with sinners—actions that fulfill Isaiah’s messianic vision (Isaiah 61:1–2), which Jesus directly quotes in Luke 4:
> “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me... to preach good news to the poor... to set the oppressed free.”

---

### Summary
Isaiah and Micah were both 8th-century BCE prophets calling Israel back to covenant fidelity through **justice**, **mercy**, and **faithful living**, not mere ritual. Jesus inherits and embodies this tradition. His teachings frequently cite or echo them, not just in words but in actions—placing ethical integrity, mercy, and humility at the center of spiritual life.
