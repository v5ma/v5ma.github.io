In Daniel 9:27, the reason most readers default to “Jerusalem temple sacrifices” is not because the verse names the temple explicitly, but because the surrounding context and the vocabulary are tightly coupled to temple-cult language.

Daniel 9:24–27 is framed by Daniel’s prayer about Jerusalem, the holy city, and the desolation of the sanctuary. The prophecy itself keeps that frame: it speaks of “your people,” “your city,” and outcomes that, in the plainest reading, land on the fate of Jerusalem and its sanctuary. Inside that frame, “sacrifice and offering” is not generic religious devotion language. The phrase is a technical pairing in biblical Hebrew for the sacrificial system: “sacrifice” is the slaughter offering (zevach), and “offering” is commonly the grain offering (minchah), which older English Bibles often render as “oblation.” That pair functions like a shorthand for the authorized altar service described in Torah and practiced at the sanctuary. Since the Torah restricts sacrificial worship to the one legitimate sanctuary/altar (in practice, the Jerusalem temple in the Second Temple period), readers infer that if someone “causes sacrifice and offering to cease,” the most straightforward referent is the temple service being stopped by force.

That said, “how do we know” has a real answer: we do not “know” in the sense of having an explicit label in the verse. What we have is an inference from context, genre, and intertext. Daniel elsewhere uses similar cessation language in passages that are commonly read as a concrete historical interruption of the temple’s “regular” service (especially in Daniel 8, 11, and 12), and later New Testament discourse about “the abomination of desolation” points readers back into the same conceptual neighborhood of sanctuary profanation and Jerusalem crisis. Those links make the temple reading a very strong default, but they do not make alternative readings impossible.

If you stipulate your hypothetical exactly as you stated it, namely that the “end-times governance architecture” is being set up but there is no Jerusalem temple, then you are basically forcing one of three interpretive moves, and which move you choose depends on your theological tradition.

One move is literal futurism with a missing premise: the prophecy is taken literally, so the missing temple must appear. In other words, the “no temple” condition is treated as a temporary absence, not a permanent constraint. This is why many futurist systems expect either a rebuilt temple, an authorized sacrificial altar, or some reinstated priestly sacrificial regime, because otherwise the “sacrifice and offering” language has no clean literal target. In that framework, your hypothetical would be internally inconsistent on purpose, and the resolution would be “a sacrificial system will be created (somehow) before it is terminated.”

A second move is to keep the Daniel language concrete but broaden what counts as “sacrifice and offering” from “Jerusalem temple animal sacrifices” to “an instituted public cultus with offerings.” In the ancient world, “sacrifice and offering” can function as a synecdoche for an officially recognized worship system that involves rituals, gifts, and priest-like mediation, even if the exact physical site is not the Solomonic/Herodian temple. Under this move, your “cessation” could refer to a political authority terminating a legally tolerated, organized worship economy. If there is no temple, the referent could become something like the suppression of a revived Jewish sacrificial movement (even if improvised), the criminalization of certain forms of worship, or the replacement of permitted worship with state-mandated rites that are “abominable” from the text’s standpoint. The point becomes less “a specific building on a specific mount” and more “the public covenant-worship order gets cut off midstream and replaced by a desecrating substitute.”

A third move is symbolic or ecclesiological: “temple” and “sacrifice” become theological metaphors for covenant fidelity, prayer, sacramental life, or the people of God as the dwelling place of God. On this reading, “causing sacrifice and offering to cease” can mean forcibly stopping the community’s ordinary worship in a sustained, regular way. The “middle of the week” then functions as a timed rupture in religious life rather than a shutdown of literal animal offerings. If you want this to map cleanly to Revelation-style “beastly” governance without a Jerusalem temple, this move often gives the best fit because it does not require rebuilding infrastructure. It can mean the regime interrupts normative worship rhythms, bans assemblies, controls liturgies, coerces idolatrous civil religion, or makes faithful practice impossible without compromise. In that symbolic frame, the “abomination” is the installation of a rival object of devotion in the space where devotion properly belongs, whether that “space” is a sanctuary, an institution, or the community’s worship itself.

If you want a more technical “brainstorm” mapping that stays within biblical imagery, there is also a tight structural clue: Daniel and Revelation both use a 3.5-ish motif (half a week; time, times, and half a time; 42 months; 1,260 days). If you are not allowed a rebuilt temple, then the shared 3.5 motif can be treated as the core invariant, and “sacrifice and offering” can be treated as the variable that is being expressed in Daniel’s cultic vocabulary and in Revelation’s persecution/economic-control vocabulary. In plain terms, that would mean: the same kind of mid-course rupture is being described, but Daniel expresses the rupture as “the worship-system stops,” whereas Revelation expresses it as “the saints are pressured, and buying and selling and public allegiance are controlled.” That does not prove the symbolic reading, but it gives you a principled way to argue that the cultic phrasing might be Daniel’s way of naming what later apocalyptic describes with different surface features.

There is one more interpretive pressure point that matters for your hypothetical: in Daniel 9:27 the agent “causes sacrifice and offering to cease” in connection with “abominations” and “desolation.” Even if you detach this from a literal temple, the logic remains that something regarded as legitimate worship is interrupted, and something regarded as desecrating replaces it or overshadows it. So if there is no Jerusalem temple, the cleanest alternatives are still of the same type: a forced termination of legitimate worship, paired with an imposed counterfeit, especially one tied to political power and public allegiance.
