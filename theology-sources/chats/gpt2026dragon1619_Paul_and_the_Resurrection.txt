Most of the movements we call “Gnostic” did practice a rite they called baptism, but they re-imagined it.  In the New Testament communities baptism was a single immersion in water marking entry into the messianic people.  Gnostic circles kept the outward act but layered it with additional stages and a different theology.

Sethian texts such as *The Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit* and *The Trimorphic Protennoia* speak of “the Five Seals.”  The initiate is washed—or anointed—five times while sacred names are recited.  Each seal marks passage through a heavenly gate; the whole sequence is meant to clothe the soul in light-bodies so that, after death, hostile archons cannot detain it.

Valentinian teachers also performed water baptism, yet insisted that its power lay not in the element itself but in the hidden descent of the Spirit.  The *Gospel of Philip* says ordinary baptism is for bodies, whereas the true bridal-chamber baptism unites the soul with its heavenly counterpart.  Water is a symbol; the real cleansing happens when the inner self remembers its origin.

Some Gnostic strands went further and treated material water as irrelevant.  A few Cathars in medieval Europe, whose theology preserved late Gnostic themes, rejected baptism outright, replacing it with the *consolamentum*, an imposition of hands and prayer for the Holy Spirit.  Even in antiquity some docetic teachers argued that if Christ only “seemed” to be flesh, then literal washing could not affect salvation.

So the short answer is yes: most Gnostics did baptize, but they interpreted the rite as a multi-level drama of awakening rather than a one-time washing away of sin.  Where the broader church saw a public entry into the community, Gnostic liturgies aimed at equipping the initiate’s consciousness for its return journey through the cosmic spheres.
