The Anunna (often spelled Anunnaki in modern works) were a group of deities in Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, and Assyrian religion. Cuneiform texts from the third and second millennia BCE describe them as gods who presided over cosmic order, the netherworld, and—later in Babylonian sources—fate. These texts never depict them as extraterrestrial beings descending to Earth in spacecraft. They appear in myths such as “Inanna’s Descent to the Netherworld” and the Akkadian “Epic of Atrahasis,” where their roles are entirely supernatural, not technological.

The idea that the Anunnaki were a race of flesh-and-blood aliens originates in twentieth-century popular literature, most prominently the books of Zecharia Sitchin (beginning with *The 12th Planet*, 1976). Sitchin reinterpreted Mesopotamian vocabulary—translating words that refer to “heaven” or “sky” as literal “planets” and reading routine divine epithets as references to rocket ships—then combined those reinterpretations with modern UFO lore. His translations are rejected by assyriologists; they bear little resemblance to what standard Sumerian and Akkadian lexicons provide.

No peer-reviewed archaeological, linguistic, or astronomical evidence corroborates the claim that advanced visitors shaped early Mesopotamian civilization. Excavated city layers, administrative tablets, and stratified technological remains all point to a continuous, human-driven cultural development from the Ubaid period onward. Where Sumerian myths mention gifts from the gods—plows, canals, or kingship—those passages fit the symbolic language of Near-Eastern theology rather than eyewitness testimony.

In scholarship, therefore, the Anunnaki remain a pantheon of ancient Near-Eastern deities. The notion that they were aliens belongs to modern pseudohistory and is not supported by historians, archaeologists, or philologists who work with the primary sources.
