Paul likens resurrection to a seed that *dies* and rises as a plant of another “splendour” (15 :36–38).  The husk does not re-form; something new grows.  The metaphor undercuts the idea that God stitches together the same flesh. citeturn0search0

## 2.  Platonic Echoes and Jewish Parallels

* In Middle-Platonism the **“astral” or “pneumatic” body** is a subtler substance that survives death; Origen later reads Paul exactly this way, speaking of a “spiritual, non-fleshly body.” citeturn0search8  
* Second-Temple Jewish apocalypses (e.g., 1 Enoch 104; 4 Ezra 7) expect the righteous to shine like stars—glorified, but not reanimated corpses.  Paul’s language of “glory” (*doxa*) and “power” (*dynamis*) for the risen body echoes these texts more than it does Luke’s fish-eating Jesus. citeturn0search5

## 3.  The Silence of the Earliest Witnesses

* Paul never mentions an empty tomb, healed wounds, or shared meals; his creed (1 Cor 15 :3-7) lists only *appearances*. citeturn0search6  
* Mark (c. 70 CE) gives an empty tomb but *no* corporeal appearances.  Only after 80 CE do Luke and John add touch-and-taste proofs. citeturn0search3turn0search4  
* This trajectory fits Tabor’s thesis: a visionary resurrection first, increasingly “corporealised” in later apologetics aimed at placating sceptics and proto-Docetic critics. citeturn0search1turn0search6

## 4.  Why Wright’s “One Early Consensus” Falters

| Wright’s claim | Earliest textual reality |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| All early Christians thought the **same corpse** was raised | Paul contrasts two **different** modes (psychic vs. pneumatic) and calls the old “flesh and blood” unfit for the kingdom. citeturn0search4 |
| Physical proofs are implied by Paul’s Damascus vision | Acts’ lightning vision is Luke’s rewrite; Paul himself never describes wounds or meals. citeturn0search2 |
| Jewish resurrection always meant corpse revival | Intertestamental texts allow transformation or star-like embodiments, not necessarily reanimation. citeturn0search5 |
| No early Christian embraced a non-fleshly resurrection | Paul’s language spawned later Platonising readings (Origen, Philoponus) that the church later had to combat. citeturn0search8 |

Wright therefore overstates his case.  The *majority* view among later fathers certainly insisted on a revivified corpse, but the **earliest recoverable voice—Paul—uses language fully compatible with a non-physical, heavenly embodiment** and never supplies the physical demonstrations Luke and John develop.

---

## 5.  Conclusion

Paul distinguishes mortal flesh from an immortal “spiritual body” precisely to mark an ontological shift from corruptible matter to a new, heavenly mode of existence.  His lexicon, seed-analogy, and flat exclusion of “flesh and blood” all pull against Wright’s claim that embodiment always meant reassembling the corpse.  Luke and John’s fish-eating scenes look like second-generation apologetics, not like clarifications Paul forgot to mention.  This does not “prove” Tabor right on every point, but it **does** show that a sophisticated, non-physical resurrection concept lies at Christianity’s earliest textual core—and that N. T. Wright’s sweeping consensus claim is at best an extrapolation, not an established fact.
