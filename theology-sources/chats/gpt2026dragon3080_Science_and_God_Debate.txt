It is basically a viral “science proves God” mashup that has been circulating on Facebook, Instagram, X, and similar pages. The core claim usually traces back to remarks by Dr. Willie Soon, who is often described online as “Harvard” or “Harvard-affiliated,” from an interview appearance where he frames antimatter and “beautiful math” as pointing to intentional design rather than randomness.

What the claim is trying to do is stitch together three different things into one punchline.

First is Paul Dirac’s 1928 relativistic equation for the electron. A real, important historical point is that the mathematics led to the existence of antiparticles (and the positron was later observed in 1932). People then tell the story as “math predicted something physical before we saw it, therefore reality is written in math.”

Second is the matter–antimatter asymmetry problem. In broad strokes, the early universe should produce matter and antimatter in roughly equal amounts, and yet the observable universe is overwhelmingly matter. That mismatch is a real open question in physics, but it is not “fixed” by saying “God wanted matter to win.” Physics has candidate mechanisms (CP violation and baryogenesis ideas), but nothing like a settled, single-line explanation.

Third is a philosophical move: the fine-tuning argument. This is the step where someone says that the laws/constants look “too precise” for life to emerge by chance, so the best explanation is a designer. That is not a mathematical proof in the way mathematicians use the word “proof.” It is an inference, and you can disagree with it without disagreeing with the physics.

So “what’s the story” is that the meme takes a legitimate physics history point (Dirac’s equation and antiparticles) plus a genuine cosmology puzzle (why matter dominates) and then brands a particular philosophical interpretation (fine-tuning implies design) as if it were a formal theorem. That branding is the misleading part. Even if you like the fine-tuning argument, it is still not the same thing as “math proves God exists.”

Also, you should treat the “Harvard scientist” framing as marketing shorthand. In this specific viral cycle, the name most commonly attached is Willie Soon, who has been associated with the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, but he is not “Harvard proving God” in any official institutional sense, and he is also a controversial figure for separate reasons unrelated to this meme’s argument.
