If we are debating “Judaism is nearly 4,000 years old,” the first move is to separate three different claims that often get mashed together.

One claim is about the age of the biblical narrative’s own timeline, where the patriarchs are placed roughly around the early second millennium BCE. That is a claim about sacred history inside the tradition. Another claim is about the age of “Israel” or early Israelite religion as a people in the Levant. That is a claim about ancient history and archaeology. A third claim is about the age of “Judaism” as a recognizable religion with a Torah-centered legal identity and the institutions that later become Second Temple and then rabbinic Judaism. That is a claim about religious history and texts.

Your second sentence, “The Torah does not appear in history before 300 BCE,” becomes much more precise if we restate it as, “We do not have surviving physical manuscripts of the Pentateuch earlier than the third century BCE.” In that narrow sense, you are basically pointing at the Dead Sea Scrolls as the earliest substantial manuscript witnesses, and that is broadly right as a statement about extant copies. The strongest version of your point is not that “the Torah did not exist earlier,” but that “the surviving manuscript record is late, and the public, datable, external footprint of the Torah-as-a-book becomes unmistakable in the Hellenistic period.”

But the moment you say “does not appear in history,” the claim becomes stronger than “no surviving manuscripts,” because history includes inscriptions and paraphrases, not just full book copies. On that broader standard, there is evidence that specific lines that later appear in the Torah were circulating centuries before 300 BCE. The most famous example is the Ketef Hinnom silver amulets, commonly dated to the late 7th or early 6th century BCE, which contain a version of the Priestly Blessing that parallels Numbers 6:24–26. That does not prove the Pentateuch already existed in its final five-book form then, but it does undermine a blanket claim that “Torah material” does not show up before 300 BCE.

There is also the Elephantine evidence in the 5th century BCE, which shows a self-identified Judean/Jewish community with Yahwistic practice and festival observance in Egypt. That evidence cuts in two directions in this debate. It supports the idea that a Jewish identity and recognizable practices existed well before 300 BCE. At the same time, many scholars note that Elephantine practice does not look like strict Deuteronomic centralization, and it does not clearly demonstrate that the community was operating under the full Pentateuch as an authoritative, binding written law in the way later Judaism imagines it. So Elephantine can be read as evidence that “Judaism” as later standardized was still forming, even while “Judean religion” or “Yahwistic Judaism(s)” already existed.

Now, about the “4,000 years old” slogan. If someone means “the people and cultic roots behind later Judaism go back deep into the Bronze Age,” that is more of an identity-continuity argument than a text-chronology argument, and it will always be partly philosophical because it depends on what counts as “the same religion.” If instead they mean “Torah-centered Judaism as a public law code and communal constitution is 4,000 years old,” that is very hard to sustain historically. Even many traditional internal chronologies do not map cleanly to external evidence, and modern critical scholarship often places the Torah’s final redaction and publication much later than 2000 BCE, frequently in the Persian period (and with some scholars arguing for later Hellenistic shaping).

A useful way to sharpen the debate is to ask what you will accept as a decisive “appearance in history.” If you require a near-complete, physically surviving Torah manuscript, then the third century BCE neighborhood becomes the practical floor. If you accept shorter textual witnesses that overlap with Torah passages, then you have pre-300 BCE “Torah-like” textual material. If you accept social-historical evidence of Judean/Jewish communities and Yahwistic worship, then you are well before 300 BCE again. And if you accept the earliest extra-biblical mention of “Israel,” then you are far earlier still, but that is no longer “Judaism” in the later, Torah-defined sense.

My own bottom line in debate form is this. Your claim is strongest and most defensible when you restrict it to “our earliest substantial manuscript witnesses of the Pentateuch are from the Second Temple period, and the Torah’s external footprint becomes clear in the Hellenistic era.” Your claim becomes much weaker if it is taken to mean “Torah content did not exist or circulate before 300 BCE,” because specific lines and practices are attested earlier, even if the five-book Torah as a finalized, authoritative corpus may not yet have been fixed in the way later Judaism treats it.
