Yes, some of the Dead Sea Scrolls line up with the Samaritan textual tradition, but it is important to be precise about what “line up” means.

What scholars usually mean is that at Qumran there are Torah manuscripts that look “pre-Samaritan,” meaning they share a recognizable profile with the Samaritan Pentateuch: lots of harmonizing edits (where one passage is adjusted to match another passage), smoothing of grammar, and occasional expansions that make laws or narratives read more consistently across Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. This matters because it shows that the Samaritan-style way of editing the Torah was not invented late; it was already a live textual option in the late Second Temple period.

That said, that does not automatically mean those Qumran manuscripts endorsed Mount Gerizim as the chosen temple site. The “Gerizim vs. Ebal” variant in Deuteronomy 27:4 is the famous example where the Samaritan Pentateuch reads “Gerizim” and the standard Jewish tradition reads “Ebal.” The key point is that “pre-Samaritan” does not equal “Samaritan theology.” Some pre-Samaritan manuscripts preserve harmonizing and editorial features similar to the Samaritan Pentateuch without carrying the distinctively Samaritan, identity-defining changes that lock the chosen place onto Gerizim. In other words, the Dead Sea Scrolls evidence is strongest for an early textual family that resembles the Samaritan Pentateuch in scribal method and many readings, and it is weaker (and in some discussions contested) as direct proof that a Qumran Torah manuscript explicitly said “Gerizim” in the crucial places.

On whether Samaritans are a race or a religion, the best fit is “ethnoreligious community.” They see themselves as a continuous Israelite people with a shared ancestry, a shared covenant, and a shared ritual life centered on the Torah and their sanctuary tradition. In practice they have historically been endogamous and cautious about outsiders joining. Conversion is not their normal mode of growth, and they do not do missionary outreach. That said, joining is not absolutely impossible. Modern reports and Samaritan-facing explanations generally describe conversion as rare, demanding, and community-controlled, with special attention to strict Torah observance and family integration. People sometimes join through marriage, but it is not comparable to “easy entry” religions.

On Samuel, David, and Solomon, Samaritans do not accept the books that contain those narratives as scripture, because their canon is basically Torah-only. That automatically means Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Psalms, and the prophetic books are not part of their Bible in the way they are in Jewish and Christian canons. Beyond that canonical fact, Samaritan tradition has long rejected the Jerusalem-centered monarchy-temple storyline as the legitimate continuation of Israel’s faith, because it competes directly with their claim that the true chosen place is their mountain. So even when Samaritans know of David and Solomon as figures in wider Israelite lore, they do not treat the Jerusalem kings and the Jerusalem temple as the authorized center.

Your question about how Gnostics might have interpreted them is harder, because we have very little direct evidence of “Gnostics” writing about Samaritans as a group in a detailed, consistent way. If you mean the broad family of late antique movements we label gnostic, many of them read Hebrew Bible material through a radical allegorical lens, often treating the creator figure and conquest narratives as morally problematic or as texts that need reinterpretation. In that style of reading, a community outside Jerusalem that insists the central sanctuary claim is wrong could have been used as a symbol for “the outsiders who see through the official story,” but that is an inference, not something you can safely assert as a documented, standard gnostic position. The more documented angle is early Christian literature, which uses “Samaritan” figures and settings as boundary-crossing symbols, but that is not the same as gnostic interpretation.

On the kingdom split and who conquered whom, your memory has one key swap. In the biblical narrative, after Solomon the kingdom divides into a northern kingdom (usually called Israel) and a southern kingdom (Judah). Historically, the northern kingdom falls to the Neo-Assyrian Empire around 722 BCE (often given as 722 or 720 BCE depending on how events are summarized), and the southern kingdom of Judah falls later to the Neo-Babylonian Empire, with Jerusalem’s destruction commonly dated to 586 BCE. The Samaritans are not simply “the entire northern kingdom renamed,” but they are strongly associated with the population that remained in the Samaria region after the Assyrian conquest, alongside complex later developments (including resettlements and identity politics) that shaped the community over time.

On the Mt Ebal versus Mt Gerizim question, there are three main kinds of evidence people lean on: the internal logic of Deuteronomy itself, the manuscript history, and the archaeological and historical record.

Inside Deuteronomy, the “blessings and curses” scene is staged on two neighboring mountains near Shechem, with blessings associated with Gerizim and curses associated with Ebal. That creates an intuitive pull in two directions. If the altar is imagined as a place of covenant renewal and communal celebration, readers often expect it to land on the “blessing” mountain, which favors Gerizim. But Deuteronomy 27 also frames the event as a legally weighty covenant ratification with curses pronounced, and later interpreters have argued that an altar on the “curse” mountain fits a logic of atonement and warning, which favors Ebal. In other words, the literary context is not a slam dunk either way; it gives you reasons that can be argued both directions, which is exactly the kind of environment where a famous variant can survive and become identity-defining.

The manuscript evidence is where the debate becomes concrete. In the Jewish Masoretic tradition, Deuteronomy 27:4 reads “Mount Ebal.” The Samaritan Pentateuch reads “Mount Gerizim,” and it also contains other changes that push “the chosen place” language toward a settled, already-known choice and toward Gerizim specifically. The Septuagint situation is messy because there is a difference between “what most surviving Septuagint manuscripts say” and “what the earliest Greek translation may have said in its oldest layer.” Many discussions note that the majority of extant Greek witnesses align with Ebal, but some scholars argue that the Old Greek could have had Gerizim at some point and was later corrected or harmonized toward Ebal in much of the transmission. The Dead Sea Scrolls add another layer: they show that multiple textual families of the Torah were circulating in the Second Temple period, including a “pre-Samaritan” style of Torah text that shares lots of harmonizing editorial moves with the Samaritan Pentateuch. That is important because it undermines the idea that “Samaritan-like Torah” equals “late invention.” However, that “pre-Samaritan” label mostly speaks to scribal habits and broad textual profile, not automatically to the specific, identity-defining Gerizim readings. For the specific Deuteronomy 27:4 word, the clearest, least controversial takeaway is that Ebal is very solidly attested in the Jewish textual stream and is also attested among the Qumran Deuteronomy material as commonly presented in standard DSS translations, while the case that Qumran explicitly preserves the Gerizim reading is discussed but not as universally accepted as the general point that pre-Samaritan textual forms existed. So, manuscript history strongly confirms that the Gerizim reading is ancient within the Samaritan tradition and that Samaritan-style textual editing is ancient in the wider Judean textual world, but it does not force the conclusion that the earliest recoverable Deuteronomy must have read Gerizim.

Archaeology and history do not settle the exact word in Deuteronomy 27:4, but they do frame what each community could plausibly claim. Mount Gerizim has substantial archaeological evidence for a major Samaritan sacred precinct and sacrificial activity in the Persian and Hellenistic periods, and it is widely accepted that a Samaritan temple complex existed there and was later destroyed by the Hasmoneans. Mount Ebal has an excavated cultic installation that Adam Zertal famously identified with Joshua’s altar, and there are continuing debates about that identification. On top of that, more recent headlines about a “curse tablet” from Mount Ebal generated backlash and critical re-evaluations, which is a reminder that the Ebal archaeology is contested and that sensational claims tend to outrun scholarly consensus. The safest summary is that Mount Gerizim has strong, direct evidence for being a long-term Samaritan cult center, while Mount Ebal has evidence for a cultic site that some connect to the biblical narrative but many scholars dispute, and neither site can, by itself, decide a textual variant in Deuteronomy.

On your second question, if you only keep the first five books, you do remove a lot of what later readers often find objectionable in the Prophets and Writings: royal politics, prophetic polemics, court intrigues, some extreme rhetoric, and large stretches of violence tied to monarchic expansion and national conflict. You also remove Samuel and Kings, which is where many readers meet the rise of monarchy, dynastic violence, and the Jerusalem-centered temple story in its fullest form.

But it does not “cleanse” the Bible of the core problems that motivated many gnostic-style critiques or that bothered later moral and rationalist readers. The Torah contains the flood story, Sodom and Gomorrah, harsh legal penalties, divinely sanctioned warfare frameworks, and episodes that read like extermination or total destruction commands in the conquest and herem logic, especially in Deuteronomy’s war laws and some Numbers narratives. It also contains a strong depiction of a deity who judges, commands, and punishes in ways that many later readers find morally difficult. If your concern is “the creator God looks violent, jealous, or arbitrary,” the Torah is actually the primary source of that picture. Many gnostic movements aimed their critique precisely at the Torah’s God and Torah’s narratives, so restricting the canon to Torah-only does not remove their main target; in some ways it intensifies it, because you lose later interpretive counterweights that some people use to soften, contextualize, or reframe the earlier material.

On whether the first five books are the books “most closely associated with Babylonian texts,” the short version is that the Torah shows some of the clearest literary parallels to Mesopotamian traditions, and modern scholarship widely associates the final shaping of the Pentateuch with exilic and post-exilic editorial activity, when Judean scribes were in close contact with Mesopotamian culture and libraries. The famous examples are where Genesis narratives resemble or echo motifs known from Mesopotamian literature, such as flood traditions and certain creation-story patterns. But that does not mean “the Torah is just Babylonian.” It is more accurate to say that the Torah likely preserves a layered mix of older Israelite/Judahite traditions, legal materials, and narrative cycles that were edited and compiled over time, and that the period around the exile and after is a plausible and widely discussed context for major stages of compilation and standardization. Also, important: prophetic and historical books can reflect exilic and post-exilic settings too, so “Babylon influence” is not limited to the Pentateuch; the Pentateuch is just where the classic cross-cultural parallels are most frequently discussed by non-specialists.
