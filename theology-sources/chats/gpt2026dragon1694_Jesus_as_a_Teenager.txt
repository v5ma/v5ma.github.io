Great set of questions—let’s unpack them one by one:

---

### **Where does the word "Christ" come from?**

The word **"Christ"** comes from the Greek word **Χριστός (*Christos*)**, which means **“anointed one”**—a direct translation of the Hebrew word **מָשִׁיחַ (*Mashiach* / Messiah)**. So “Jesus Christ” means **“Jesus the Anointed One.”**

---

### **Is it from Paul?**

Paul **did not invent** the term “Christ,” but he **did radically reinterpret it** and popularize it in a new theological framework. In Second Temple Judaism, "Messiah" referred to a future political or priestly liberator—often imagined as a Davidic king who would free Israel from foreign rule.

Paul redefined **“Messiah/Christ”** into a **cosmic, universal, and spiritual figure**—not just Israel’s king, but the *Logos*, the universal Savior, the embodiment of divine love and resurrection power. In his letters (which predate the written Gospels), Paul uses the word **“Christ” far more often than “Jesus” alone**, and often **as a cosmic title** rather than a human name.

So while Paul **did not coin the term**, he **did reframe it so radically** that one could argue the **Christ of Pauline theology** is **not quite the same** as the historical Jesus.

---

### **Did Jesus assign this title to himself in Pre-Paulinism?**

The Gospels offer **mixed evidence** about whether Jesus openly called himself the **Messiah (Christos)**. Some examples:

- In **Mark 8:29**, Jesus asks, “Who do you say I am?” Peter replies, “You are the Christ.” Jesus accepts the title but immediately tells them not to speak of it—a common literary motif in Mark called the **“Messianic Secret.”**
  
- In **John 4:25–26**, Jesus tells the Samaritan woman, “I who speak to you am he [the Messiah].”

However, **Jesus more often refers to himself as "Son of Man"**, a term from Daniel and apocalyptic Jewish texts, which carries a more cryptic meaning—possibly emphasizing humility, mystery, or divine authority without the political baggage of “Messiah.”

So while Jesus may have **implicitly accepted** the title of Messiah, he **rarely if ever emphasized it himself** in the way later Christians (especially Paul) did. And it’s not at all clear he would have used the **Greek word "Christos"** in his own Aramaic speech.

---

### **Is it applied in Gnosticism?**

Yes, but **very differently**.

In **Gnostic texts**, particularly from the Nag Hammadi library, “Christ” is often a **cosmic principle** rather than simply a historical person:

- In texts like the *Gospel of Philip* and *Gospel of Truth*, **“the Christ” is a spiritual force or aeon**—a divine aspect of the pleroma (the fullness of God’s emanations).
  
- The **Christ principle** sometimes **enters into Jesus** temporarily, or reveals itself *through* Jesus, but **Jesus and Christ are not always the same being** in these accounts.

In short, **Gnosticism treats “Christ” more like a divine archetype**—a revealer of hidden knowledge (*gnosis*), rather than a savior from sin in the Pauline sense.

---

### **Would Aaron Abke’s *The Jesus Way* program avoid the word Christ?**

Aaron Abke is part of a **New Thought / Gnostic-leaning / metaphysical Christian** current. He often **uses the word "Christ"**, but **not in a strictly Pauline or orthodox way**.

In Abke’s framework:

- **“Christ” is more of a universal consciousness** than a title for a single individual.
- He draws from **Gnostic Christianity, A Course in Miracles, the Law of One, and Eastern non-dualism**, where “Christ Consciousness” is **a state of divine awareness** available to all—not exclusive to Jesus.
- So no, he **does not avoid the word “Christ”**, but he **transcends its historical-theological use**, reinterpreting it as **a universal principle of love, unity, and divine identity**.

---

### In Summary:

- **“Christ”** is the Greek for “Messiah,” meaning “anointed one.”
- **Paul** did not invent the term but redefined it into a cosmic Savior.
- **Jesus** rarely used it for himself, preferring “Son of Man,” though others applied it to him.
- **Gnostics** used it as a symbol of divine knowledge and spiritual awakening.
- **Abke** uses it in a mystical, metaphysical sense—more like a universal identity than a person.

So the word **“Christ” has evolved** through history: from Hebrew political liberator → Greek spiritual redeemer → Pauline cosmic Savior → Gnostic revealer → New Thought universal consciousness.

Why is the Son of Man a veiled term?

Self Aware Networks GPT:
“**Son of Man**” is a veiled term because it carries **layered meanings**—some mundane, some mysterious, some apocalyptic—and Jesus **leverages that ambiguity** to speak powerfully without making overt, politically dangerous claims too early in his ministry.

Let’s break down the reasons:

---

### 1. **Multiple Possible Meanings in Jewish Tradition**

In **Hebrew and Aramaic**, *“son of man”* (בֶּן־אָדָם / *ben adam*) can simply mean:

- **"a human being"** (generic usage)
- **"mortal"** or **"lowly person"**
- But also: a **mysterious, exalted figure** in Jewish apocalyptic visions—especially in the Book of **Daniel**.

In **Daniel 7:13–14**, the prophet sees:

> "*One like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven... He was given authority, glory and sovereign power; all nations and peoples... worshiped him. His dominion is everlasting...*"

This “Son of Man” is **not just human**—he's a divine-like ruler who comes from heaven and is enthroned next to God. A powerful messianic and apocalyptic image.

So the phrase “Son of Man” could mean:
- Just “I, a human being...”
- Or “I, the prophesied cosmic judge…”

This **dual use** made it **intentionally ambiguous**.

---

### 2. **Strategic Ambiguity**

Jesus **uses the term to refer to himself**, but:
- It’s **not immediately inflammatory** the way “Messiah” or “Son of God” might be.
- It **invites interpretation** but doesn't directly confront Roman or priestly authorities with treasonous claims.
- It allows him to **reveal himself progressively**, depending on the audience.

For example:
- “The Son of Man has no place to lay his head.” → Sounds like "just a man."
- “The Son of Man has authority to forgive sins.” → A divine prerogative.
- “You will see the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven…” → A quote from Daniel 7, clearly eschatological.

By using “Son of Man,” Jesus can speak with **prophetic and divine authority** while leaving space for mystery—avoiding premature arrest or misunderstanding by people expecting a violent messiah.

---

### 3. **Veiling Divinity to Reveal it Over Time**

In a spiritual sense, the “Son of Man” title is **veiled** because it:
- **Hides his divine identity in plain sight.**
- **Prevents forcing belief through spectacle or title**—he wants followers to see and *choose* the truth, not just react to claims of grandeur.

This fits Jesus’s pattern of:
- Speaking in parables,
- Telling people not to announce miracles,
- Refusing political titles,
- And letting understanding emerge through lived relationship, not just labels.

---

### Conclusion:

The term **“Son of Man” veils and reveals** at the same time. It:
- Signals humility and humanity on one level,
- Echoes Daniel’s divine messianic figure on another,
- Allows Jesus to communicate deeply **without provoking immediate political backlash**, and
- Invites listeners into a journey of discernment.

It is precisely **because it's veiled** that it functions as Jesus’s favorite self-description: it protects the mystery, while opening the door for revelation.
