The Gospel of John never names “the disciple whom Jesus loved,” so later readers had to deduce an identity from external tradition and internal clues. From the late second century onward writers such as Irenaeus said this beloved disciple was John the son of Zebedee, and that view became traditional. Within the narrative the figure uses masculine participles, sits among the Twelve at the Last Supper, races Peter to the empty tomb, and is shown fishing with the other male disciples after the resurrection. Those scenes, together with the close pairing of the beloved disciple and Peter throughout the book, still persuade most specialists that the author means a male insider, very likely John. citeturn2search1turn2search0  

A key difficulty for identifying the beloved disciple with Mary Magdalene is that the Gospel regularly distinguishes the two figures. On Easter morning Mary finds the empty tomb and then runs to tell “Simon Peter and the other disciple, the one Jesus loved,” who in turn run back to the tomb ahead of her. At the crucifixion Mary Magdalene is listed among the women while the beloved disciple stands nearby and receives Jesus’ mother into his care. The text therefore presents them as separate witnesses in the same scenes, which most scholars take as decisive. citeturn2search1  

Nevertheless a minority proposal argues that the Gospel originally celebrated Mary Magdalene as the beloved disciple and that later editors masked her role by substituting an unnamed, ostensibly male figure. Ramon K. Jusino’s 1998 essay “Mary Magdalene: Author of the Fourth Gospel?” is the best-known statement of this view; it notes that several second-century Gnostic writings call Mary the disciple Jesus loved most and that she was the first to proclaim the resurrection, matching the privileged status of the beloved disciple in John. Advocates suggest that Mary’s name could have been replaced with anonymous language at a redactional stage when female leadership became uncomfortable for some communities. citeturn1search0turn1search9  

Most Johannine scholars remain unpersuaded. They point out that every surviving manuscript already contains both Mary Magdalene and the beloved disciple, so the hypothesised textual suppression would have had to occur before our manuscript tradition began, leaving no trace; they also note that the masculine grammar, the presence at the all-male supper, and the beloved disciple’s consistent pairing with Peter all weigh against the Magdalene hypothesis. For these reasons the identification of the beloved disciple with John (or, in some recent arguments, another male associate such as Lazarus or a Jerusalem priest) still commands majority support. citeturn2search1turn2search3  

In short, Mary Magdalene’s unparalleled prominence in early Christian memory makes the idea intriguing, but the internal evidence of the Fourth Gospel and the state of the manuscripts leave it, for now, a speculative minority view rather than an established alternative to the traditional attribution to John.
