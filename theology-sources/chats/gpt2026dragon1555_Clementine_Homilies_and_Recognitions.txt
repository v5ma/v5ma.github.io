The word “Clementine” is a modern label scholars give to a cluster of early Christian writings that pretend to be the autobiography of Clement of Rome, a first-century bishop who is otherwise known only from a short letter.  The original narrative, now lost, seems to have been called the “Circuits of Peter.”  It told how the young pagan seeker Clement meets the apostle Peter, follows him through a series of debates with pagans, magicians and philosophers, and in the end discovers and reunites with his long-lost family.  Nothing in the surviving manuscripts was actually written by Clement, so historians refer to the anonymous composer as “Pseudo-Clement.” citeturn0search0

Two independent fourth-century recensions of that lost work survive.  One is preserved in Greek and is known as the Clementine Homilies.  It runs to twenty books and is cast as a sequence of long philosophical “homilies,” or discourses, delivered by Peter, interrupted by travel scenes and by Clement’s family drama.  The other is the Clementine Recognitions, which survives only in the Latin translation made by Rufinus of Aquileia in 406 CE.  It is somewhat shorter and reshapes the same story into ten books that focus on Clement’s gradual “recognition” of each member of his family.  Although the two versions overlap scene-for-scene for much of their length, each also contains material absent from the other, making it clear that they are parallel rewritings of the same earlier romance rather than one being a direct abbreviation or expansion of the other. citeturn0search0turn0search1turn0search2

Both recensions promote a theology that modern scholars call “Jewish-Christian” or sometimes “Ebionite.”  Jesus is portrayed as a human prophet empowered by God, the true successor to Moses, and Peter defends strict monotheism and observance of the Torah; Paul is thinly disguised and attacked under the name “Simon Magus.”  Because of that polemical edge, the Clementines are an important source for reconstructing the variety of beliefs that circulated on the margins of the emerging catholic (“universal”) church in the third and fourth centuries. citeturn0search5

In short, “Clementine” denotes the whole pseudo-Clement corpus; the Homilies and the Recognitions are its two competing versions.  Both works weave theological dialogue, adventure narrative and family-reunion melodrama into one of the most substantial Christian novels that survives from antiquity.

Within the Clementine Homilies and Recognitions the figure who debates Peter is called Simon Magus, but many passages echo Paul’s language, itinerary and conversion-vision so closely that the author is plainly turning Paul into a dramatised villain. A fourth-century Jewish-Christian editor, hostile to the apostle’s Gentile mission, could thus attack “Paul” without naming him. Nineteenth-century Catholic scholars noticed the device: the Catholic Encyclopedia bluntly states that “Simon Magus never existed; it is a nickname for St Paul.” citeturn3view0

Because that polemic is incompatible with the canonical Acts of the Apostles and with the Catholic view of Paul as a saint, the Clementine novels were never admitted to the biblical canon or to liturgical reading. Late-antique heresiologists such as Origen and Epiphanius mention the writings only to show what certain Ebionite or other Jewish-Christian groups were circulating, and the sixth-century Gelasian list of “books to be avoided” files the “books of Peter and Clement” among spurious works. Medieval copyists preserved the romance for its adventure plot, but mainstream theology treated it as imaginative fiction, not as a doctrinal threat, so there was little to “refute” beyond what those early fathers had already said. Modern Catholic and Protestant scholarship still studies the Clementines as valuable evidence for second- and third-century intra-Christian disputes, yet ordinary church teaching and preaching continue to pass over them in silence for the same reason: they are apocryphal novels whose theology conflicts with the catholic consensus on Paul. citeturn1view0turn6view0
