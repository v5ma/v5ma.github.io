Jewish apocalyptic texts emerged particularly during oppressive periods (such as Babylonian exile, Seleucid oppression, Roman occupation), using symbolic and often violent imagery to reassure believers that oppressive regimes would ultimately fall:

Imagery from ancient Near Eastern warfare—particularly the idea of leaving the defeated dead unburied and eaten by scavengers—symbolized total victory and humiliation of enemies. This was a common theme not only in Hebrew tradition but broadly across Near Eastern cultures.

John of Patmos, author of Revelation, deliberately invoked this well-known symbolic imagery to communicate clear theological and political messages:

- **Divine Justice:** Assurance that oppressive, violent empires (like Rome, symbolically "Babylon") would ultimately face divine judgment.
- **Hope for the Persecuted:** Violent imagery affirmed the reversal of roles—oppressors would be humiliated and destroyed by God, providing hope to oppressed early Christians enduring suffering and persecution.

Thus, Revelation doesn't invent this violent symbolism but relies upon and reinterprets a well-established Jewish literary and theological tradition.
