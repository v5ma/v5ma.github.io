### 1. Riverine Assaults
For cities on navigable rivers, the waterway was both lifeline and vulnerability.  
- **Sumerian Expansion**: The Uruk expansion period (c. 3600–3200 BCE) planted enclaves at key river choke-points. Boats could bring grain in bulk, move troops quickly, and, in a siege, land forces behind defenses.  
- **Epic Echoes**: In the Gilgamesh cycle, journeys by boat (to Utnapishtim, across the Waters of Death) carry a sense of overwhelming passage. On a practical level, boat-borne troops could arrive en masse inside or just outside a city’s walls if the river ran through.  
- **Historical Cases**: Later Mesopotamian warfare often hinged on river access—Assyrians and Babylonians floated siege engines and armies along rivers to disembark suddenly.

### 2. Ambushing the Water Source
If a city had no internal river, the spring or cistern outside its walls was its Achilles’ heel.  
- **Ambush Tactics**: Raiders could wait for water-bearers, cut them down, and force the city to parley. Without water, even the strongest wall crumbles.  
- **Biblical Parallel**: Jerusalem before David’s capture depended on the Gihon spring. Later tradition says the city was taken through the *ṣinnôr* (water channel/shaft). This reflects the strategic importance of water access.  
- **Sumerian & Akkadian Texts**: In omen texts and royal inscriptions, “cutting off water” is a frequent siege motif. To destroy irrigation dikes, canals, or block springs was to strangle a city.

### 3. Broader Pattern
What you’ve phrased as “Sumerian expansion methods 101” is actually the universal Near Eastern playbook:
- **With a river:** mass by boat and strike fast.  
- **Without a river:** deny the enemy water and wait until thirst forces surrender.  

That’s why Mesopotamian cities were so often built *directly on* rivers and spent enormous labor on canals and cisterns—because water was both their strength and their most obvious vulnerability.
