Paul’s letters were written in the 50s CE, whereas the literary and organisational features that scholars label “Gnosticism” take shape only in the early- to mid-second century, roughly 120-180 CE. Valentinus, for instance, claimed that his master Theudas had received from Paul a “secret wisdom” and he built a mythic system around that idea, but this is at least seventy years after Paul’s death. citeturn0search4  

Because Paul was already revered as the apostle to the nations, second-century teachers such as Valentinus and Marcion could present their distinctive messages as the true inner meaning of his epistles. Marcion went so far as to publish the first known Christian canon consisting almost entirely of ten Pauline letters plus a modified gospel, arguing that only Paul had understood the unknown God revealed in Christ. citeturn0search1turn0search5  

The Roman church did not reject Paul while rejecting Gnosticism; it fought for its own way of reading Paul. The Pastoral Epistles—pseudonymous letters written a generation after Paul—warn Timothy and Titus against “myths and endless genealogies,” language most interpreters read as aimed at gnosticising reinterpretations of Paul’s words, not at Paul himself. Late-second-century bishops such as Irenaeus and Tertullian kept Paul’s letters in the emerging catholic canon yet wrote lengthy refutations of Marcion’s and Valentinus’s “hidden wisdom” commentaries. citeturn0search3turn0search7  

Paul’s own theology is not gnostic. He affirms bodily resurrection, the goodness of creation, and the public proclamation of the gospel; those themes cut against core Gnostic dualisms that treat matter as a prison for the spirit. What attracted later Gnostics was his language of “mystery,” “fullness,” and revelation “caught up to the third heaven,” which they could allegorise into their cosmologies, but those moves were second-century inventions layered onto a first-century text. citeturn0search8  

Nor did the Roman church embrace Paul because his letters conveniently subjected humanity to a central religious empire. In fact, the same epistles were deployed by rival groups in opposite ways: Marcion used them to dismantle the authority of Jewish scripture and ecclesial hierarchy, while catholic bishops used them to defend a rule of faith, apostolic succession, and moral discipline. The controversy shows not that Paul founded Gnosticism, but that his letters were a prized, malleable resource fought over by every faction within early Christianity.  

Gnostic schools often rejected Jewish law and, in some cases, earthly authority, yet they were not uniformly libertarian. Many taught strict ascetic renunciation of sex and meat, others tolerated antinomian behaviour, and a few observed elements of the Mosaic code. Conversely, Paul’s own teaching on grace still demanded concrete moral obligations toward one’s neighbour. The sharp differences between Paul and later Gnosticism, and the simultaneous appeal his letters held for both proto-orthodox and heterodox circles, make it historically unlikely that he initiated the movement. Rather, second-century Gnostics retroactively claimed him, and the Roman church responded by insisting that only its public, episcopal reading of Paul preserved the apostle’s intent.
