### 1. **Would Jesus be so against sacrifices in the Temple that he’d risk his life by disrupting them?**

In the Synoptic Gospels (Mark 11, Matthew 21, Luke 19), Jesus' **cleansing of the Temple**—overturning the money changers’ tables and calling the Temple a "den of robbers"—is one of the clearest events that precipitates his arrest and eventual execution. This action was not a protest against Judaism per se, but **a prophetic critique** of corruption and possibly of the entire sacrificial system as it had come to function under the priestly elite.

In line with **prophetic tradition** (e.g., Jeremiah 7:11), Jesus symbolically interrupts the Temple economy. Many scholars, including N.T. Wright, suggest that this act was **eschatological and messianic**, a claim that the Temple system was under judgment and nearing its end. In this reading, Jesus sees the Temple not just as corrupt, but obsolete in light of what he sees as the incoming Kingdom of God—a kingdom in which **mercy outweighs sacrifice** (cf. Hosea 6:6, which Jesus quotes in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7).

So yes—there is strong textual and historical support for the idea that Jesus was **critically opposed to the Temple system**, at least as it stood, and that he was willing to risk death to challenge it.

---

### 2. **Would Jesus tell a healed leper to offer a Temple sacrifice, despite being against the Temple system?**

In **Mark 1:44**, after healing a leper, Jesus tells him:

> “See that you say nothing to anyone, but go, show yourself to the priest and offer for your cleansing what Moses commanded, as a testimony to them.”

This reflects **Levitical law** (Leviticus 14), which required sacrifices and priestly inspection for a leper to be ritually reinstated in the community. Here, Jesus upholds **Torah observance**, not because he believes in the permanence of the sacrificial system, but because:

1. **He was still within the Old Covenant framework** during his lifetime.
2. **The man needed ritual certification to re-enter society**—not just physical healing, but social restoration.
3. Jesus often **operates pastorally**, adapting to the needs of individuals and the expectations of the broader Jewish community.
4. It may serve as a **testimony against the religious establishment**—a healed leper showing up in the Temple challenges their categories of authority.

So this act is **not necessarily a theological endorsement of Temple sacrifice**, but a **strategic and compassionate move** within the bounds of the current system.

---

### Reconciling the Two

This tension reflects a **both/and** dynamic in Jesus’ ministry:

- **Prophetically**, Jesus critiques the Temple and its priesthood as spiritually bankrupt and nearing judgment.
- **Practically**, he navigates Jewish law and expectations in ways that allow him to minister to individuals within their social and religious reality.

This is similar to how Jesus **paid the Temple tax** (Matthew 17:24–27) "so that we may not cause offense" even while asserting he is exempt. He lives within the system he is transcending.

### Final Thought

So, would Jesus die to confront the corruption and idolatry of the Temple system? Yes.

Would he also tell someone to go to that very Temple for cultural and legal reasons, even while believing it would soon pass away? Also yes.

This is not hypocrisy—it’s the mark of a reformer acting both **within** and **against** a collapsing system, drawing attention to a deeper spiritual truth while still attending to the social and legal fabric of his time.

If Wes Huff emphasizes fidelity to Scripture and theological continuity, and Aaron Abke emphasizes inner divinity and awakening, this story holds space for both views: Jesus affirms the dignity of the person (Abke) while acting as a messianic reformer rooted in prophetic Jewish tradition (Huff).

### Jesus’s Evolving View of the Temple?

If we take the Gospels as offering a **chronological development** of Jesus’s ministry, then you’re absolutely right: **Jesus healing the leper and sending him to the Temple (Mark 1:44)** comes *much earlier* than the **Temple disruption (Mark 11:15–19)**. This invites the hypothesis that Jesus’s **attitude toward the Temple may have evolved over time**.

Here’s how that developmental view might look:

---

### **Phase 1: Respect for the Law and Temple System (Early Ministry)**

At the start of his public activity, Jesus appears to **operate within the traditional Jewish framework**, even when challenging its rigidity. His instruction to the leper to go to the Temple shows:

- Respect for **Mosaic Law**.
- A concern for **ritual and social reintegration**.
- Possibly, a desire to **avoid unnecessary controversy** early in his ministry.

At this point, Jesus is still **teaching in synagogues**, healing quietly, and often telling people not to spread word about him. This suggests a **strategic restraint**—perhaps out of concern for premature opposition from religious authorities.

---

### **Phase 2: Mounting Tension and Prophetic Critique (Middle Ministry)**

As his ministry grows, Jesus becomes more **openly critical** of certain religious practices—especially where they conflict with mercy, justice, or human need. He:

- Heals on the Sabbath.
- Touches the unclean.
- Forgives sins directly (bypassing the Temple altogether).
- Quotes Hosea 6:6 multiple times: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.”

This indicates a **deepening conflict** with the Temple-based system and a growing prophetic edge to his message.

---

### **Phase 3: Open Challenge and Symbolic Condemnation (Final Week)**

By the time Jesus enters Jerusalem during the final week, his stance becomes **decisively confrontational**:

- The **cleansing of the Temple** is more than just moral outrage—it’s a symbolic act of **eschatological judgment** (echoing Jeremiah and other prophets).
- He predicts the **destruction of the Temple** (Mark 13), further showing his conviction that the old system is passing away.
- His actions provoke the religious authorities to seek his death, suggesting he knew this would be a point of no return.

---

### Does This Imply a Change in Belief?

It could. There are three ways to interpret this:

1. **Strategic escalation**: Jesus always saw the Temple as temporary, but chose when and how to act on that belief.
2. **Prophetic evolution**: Jesus’s understanding of his mission and the Temple’s role **developed over time**, possibly in response to increasing resistance and revelation.
3. **Narrative framing**: The Gospel writers may have **retrospectively ordered or shaped** events to fit a theological arc, emphasizing how Jesus moved from Torah-faithful healer to eschatological prophet.

Each of these allows for an interpretation where **Jesus's early compliance with Temple laws doesn’t contradict his later critique**, but shows a dynamic response to a shifting spiritual and political landscape.

---

### Summary

Yes, Jesus telling the leper to go to the Temple may reflect an **earlier stage** in his ministry when he worked within the system. His later disruption of the Temple likely reflects a **climactic moment** in an evolving mission. Whether that evolution was in Jesus’s own consciousness, or simply in the increasing boldness of his actions, is open to interpretation—but the **chronology strongly supports the idea of an intentional progression.**
