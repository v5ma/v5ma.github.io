If you zoom in on hydrocarbons, the story is much more “natural gas” than “oil,” and that difference matters because it changes what “control” can realistically mean.

Israel and Gaza are not sitting on a Persian Gulf–scale oil prize. The big oil reserves in the region are overwhelmingly in Gulf states, Iraq, and Iran. What Israel does have is significant offshore natural gas in the Eastern Mediterranean, and it has already converted that gas into geopolitical leverage by exporting to neighbors and by tying itself into regional energy infrastructure. In other words, the lever is less “owning the region’s oil” and more “being a critical node in the gas-and-electricity supply chain that some neighbors have come to rely on.”

That is also why Gaza specifically matters in a narrower way than people sometimes assume. Offshore Gaza has the Gaza Marine gas field, which has been talked about for decades and is widely described as roughly on the order of 1 trillion cubic feet, meaning about 30–35 billion cubic meters. That is meaningful for Palestinian fiscal independence and for Gaza’s reconstruction economics, but it is not a world-changing pool of hydrocarbons. By comparison, Israel’s Leviathan field alone is often described in the hundreds of billions of cubic meters, and it has already been the basis of long-term export agreements and proposed expansion.

So where can a “showdown” actually come from. It is less likely to be a traditional oil-grab and more likely to be a conflict over five practical control points: who has legal and physical control over offshore development, who provides security for rigs and pipelines, who gets the revenues and on what escrow or governance terms, who controls the export route (for example, selling via Egypt’s LNG facilities versus other regional options), and what political conditions get attached to investment and production. If one side controls the sea space and security environment, it can effectively veto development even if it does not “own” the resource on paper. If another side controls the money and reconstruction architecture, it can shape the revenue model and beneficiaries even without controlling the coastline.

That is where your Trump angle plugs in, but “wants to control the oil” is probably too blunt. What the U.S. can plausibly control is the framework: who is allowed to invest, what political conditions get attached to reconstruction, how revenues are handled, and what security arrangements are imposed. This week’s reporting around the Trump administration’s “New Gaza” pitch and the related “Board of Peace” initiative fits that pattern: it emphasizes development, private capital, and security conditions, while being comparatively vague on sovereignty, property rights, and who ultimately governs. In a setup like that, offshore gas becomes less a prize to seize and more a financing chip that different parties try to route through their preferred governance and security structure.

Finally, it is worth separating two kinds of “control” that get blurred in public debate. There is ownership control, meaning internationally recognized rights to extract and sell. Then there is operational control, meaning who can actually permit, protect, finance, insure, and export the molecules. In the Eastern Mediterranean, operational control often decides outcomes long before legal ownership gets cleanly resolved. That is why even a relatively modest field like Gaza Marine can become strategically charged: it is one of the few plausible “self-funding” mechanisms for Gaza’s future, and every actor involved has reasons to prevent that mechanism from empowering the “wrong” political authority.

If you want to pressure-test your hypothesis, the cleanest question to ask is not “who controls the oil,” but “what concrete mechanism would convert offshore gas into enforceable political power, and who holds each dependency in that mechanism.” Once you map those dependencies, you can see where a future confrontation would actually bite: security perimeters, maritime access, licensing, escrow and revenue governance, and export chokepoints.
