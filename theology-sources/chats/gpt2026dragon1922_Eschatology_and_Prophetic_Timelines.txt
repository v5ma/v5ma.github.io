Apocalyptic literature in both Judaism and early Christianity is marked by symbolic visions, pseudonymous authorship, and a deliberately “imminent” tone designed to spur ethical vigilance and hope, not to pin a precise calendar date to every cosmic event. Revelation likewise distinguishes between the protagonist’s present context and events yet to unfold. John is told to write “the things that you have seen, the things that are, and the things that will take place after this,” clearly bifurcating what was contemporaneous (the messages to the seven churches) from a series of visions extending into the consummation of history and the new creation citeturn3search1turn3search0. Major interpretive traditions reinforce this open‑ended horizon. Because both Daniel and Revelation employ apocalyptic immediacy as a rhetorical tool, explicitly seal their visions until a distant “time of the end,” and structure their revelations to encompass far‑future fulfillment, they cannot be pressed into the mold of predictions confined to their authors’ own generations. Their open‑ended design allows them to speak both to their original audiences and to every subsequent era awaiting the final consummation.

James Tabor’s reconstructed “pre‑Christian” Revelation excises every overtly Christian element, including the Lamb and its blood‑ransom language.
