## 1. Math proof vs. court ruling (from a journalist’s standpoint)

For genocide you’ve got three layers:

1. **Legal definition**  
   The binding standard is still Article 2 of the 1948 Genocide Convention: genocide = *any of five acts (killing, serious bodily/mental harm, life-destroying conditions, preventing births, forcible transfer of children) committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a protected group (national, ethnic, racial, religious).* citeturn6search0turn6search2  

2. **Fact-finding**  
   What actually happened on the ground: numbers killed, targeting patterns, starvation, destruction of infrastructure, official rhetoric, orders, internal documents, etc.

3. **Adjudication**  
   A court (ICJ, ICC, or domestic) applies (1) to (2) and makes a **formal** determination, with procedural and evidentiary rules.

A “mathematical proof” sits in a different slot:

- It’s a **formal argument** of the form  
  `If (premise set P) and (genocide definition D), then conclusion C = “This is genocide” follows.`
- Its authority depends entirely on:
  - whether `D` matches the *actual* legal standard, and  
  - whether `P` is factually true (which is *not* something math alone can decide).

So:

- For a **journalist**, a rigorous, transparent, formalized argument can be *very* valuable: it clarifies assumptions and shows where the dispute really lies.
- But it **doesn’t have the same kind of authority** as an ICJ judgment, because it doesn’t come with subpoena power, cross-examination, evidentiary rules, etc. It’s more like a very careful expert report or amicus brief.

I’d phrase it as: a good “math proof” can give you **epistemic authority** (“if you accept these premises, the genocide conclusion follows”), but not **legal authority**. A responsible journalist can absolutely rely on such work *alongside* other expert and institutional sources, but shouldn’t pretend it *is* a court.
