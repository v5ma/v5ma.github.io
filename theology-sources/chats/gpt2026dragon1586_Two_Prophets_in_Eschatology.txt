Your intuition about an unbroken line of contesting spiritual visions—from the nonviolent prophets of early Israel through the separatist Essene camp, into the pre-Pauline Jesus movement, and onward to the Gnostic authors—is not wrong in principle, but the actual story is indeed more tangled and fragmentary.  In the Hebrew Scriptures, voices like those of Isaiah or Micah argue that righteousness requires mercy to the powerless and even prescribe care for animals (for example, the injunction against muzzling an ox as it treads grain).  Yet elsewhere Leviticus and Deuteronomy lay out animal sacrifice as the very locus of divine presence.  That discrepancy never fully dissolves, and every generation of interpreters leans toward one pole or the other.

By the second century BCE, the Qumran community felt that Jerusalem’s priesthood had betrayed Torah’s pure vision and withdrew into the desert under their “Teacher of Righteousness.”  Their scrolls envisage a final redemption led by two figures, a priestly and a royal messiah, who restore both temple purity and Davidic rule.  Although they tolerated sacrificial ritual within their own “New Covenant” calendar, they sharply critiqued the Jerusalem altar, and their ascetic meals avoided Temple offerings.  From this ferment emerges the movement around John the Baptist.  He inherits Qumran’s disavowal of Jerusalem’s corrupt cult, offers a baptismal rite that echoes the Community’s purifying waters, and preaches an imminent kingdom without animal-blood atonement.

Jesus of Nazareth appears within that same spirit circle—call them Nazarenes or Jessians—and in every Gospel his ethics tilt more toward mercy than sacrifice.  His table fellowship includes those the Temple system shuns; his parables reimagine shepherd-and-flock language to favor compassion; and his greatest ritual innovation, the Last Supper, replaces Passover slaughter with the sharing of bread and wine.  His earliest followers, what later writers label Ebionites, continue to observe Torah but reject Temple sacrifice in favor of communal meals.  They refuse Paul’s teaching that permits eating meat offered to idols—seeing it instead as profaning the feast of divine fellowship and perpetuating violence against vulnerable creatures.

As these Jewish-Christian sects spread into the Greek-speaking world, they encounter Hellenistic ideas about hidden divine realms and the corrupt material cosmos.  Out of that contact rise the many Gnostic systems of the second and third centuries.  In those mythologies Yaldabaoth, the ignorant demiurge who fashions our fleshly world, becomes the cosmic counterpart to the Temple-priestly power that John and his heirs once repudiated.  The demiurge’s demand for animal sacrifice echoes the historical Jerusalem cult, and his refusal to reveal the transcendent Father mirrors the way Romanized Judaism and then proto-orthodox Christianity closed the older prophetic dissent.

Gnostic texts therefore retell Genesis as the demiurge’s clumsy act of creation, condemn the eating of sacrificial flesh as perpetuating bondage, and elevate the antipathy to violence that marked the Teacher of Righteousness, John the Baptist, and the Nazarenes.  By invoking Yaldabaoth, they fuse the rejection of Paul’s mission—seen as collusion with the meat trade and imperial temple economy—with the older prophetic insistence that true worship must be lived by mercy.  Yet even within Gnosticism this thread frays.  Some Gnostic schools accept certain rituals or contemplative “sacrifices,” and others abandon all direct ethical demands in favor of purely inner ascent.

Over the centuries the mainstream church re-absorbs portions of these debates—reinstating animal sacrifice only in symbolic liturgy, insisting that Christ himself is both high priest and lamb, then gradually marginalizing any literal blood-atonement.
