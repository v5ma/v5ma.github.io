In Greek Paul writes that “one believes he can eat everything, but the one who is asthenḗs—‘infirm’ or ‘weak’—eats only lákhanon,” a word that means garden-produce or vegetables.  The issue he is addressing is not nutrition but scruples of conscience.  Some Roman believers refused meat because most of the city’s slaughtered animals had first been offered on pagan altars; others kept kosher out of loyalty to Jewish law.  Paul treats those constraints as a sign of limited confidence in the gospel’s universal freedom.  At the same time he tells the meat-eaters (the “strong”) not to look down on the abstainers, and he tells the abstainers not to judge the meat-eaters, because “God has welcomed them both.”

In later Christianity that verse became a stock proof-text against compulsory vegetarianism, yet it never settled the question.  Jewish-Christian groups such as the Ebionites continued to reject meat and animal sacrifice, arguing that Jesus’ cleansing of the Temple overturned the whole cult of slaughter.  Second-century writers like Clement of Alexandria praised vegetarian ascetics, while Augustine and others cited Romans 14 to say that diet is indifferent so long as it is received with thanksgiving.  The argument has echoed ever since: some read Paul as defending liberty to eat whatever is sold in the market, others hear him rebuking both sides for letting food define righteousness.
