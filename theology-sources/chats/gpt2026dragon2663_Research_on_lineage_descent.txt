**Y-DNA testing** is a type of genetic test that examines the **Y chromosome**, which is passed almost unchanged from father to son. It’s used to trace **direct paternal lineage** — the same line by which tribal status (like Kohen, Levi, or Davidic descent) would traditionally be inherited in Jewish tradition.

Here’s what it does and why it matters:

### 🧬 What it measures
- The test analyzes **short tandem repeats (STRs)** or **single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)** on the Y chromosome.  
- Since only men have a Y chromosome, only **male descendants** can take it (for women, a male relative from the same paternal line can test).  
- The results identify your **Y-haplogroup**, a genetic branch that reflects deep paternal ancestry — often tens of thousands of years old.

### 🔍 Applications for lineage
- **Cohen Modal Haplotype (CMH):** Many men who identify as *Kohanim* share a specific cluster of Y-DNA markers, suggesting a common paternal ancestor roughly 3,000 years ago — consistent with Aaronic descent.  
- **Levitical lineages:** Levites also show some genetic clustering, though it’s more diverse.  
- **Davidic descent:** Because King David lived ~3,000 years ago and there’s no verified DNA reference sample, Y-DNA can’t confirm Davidic lineage directly. However, if one day a well-documented rabbinic Davidic line is genetically profiled, it might allow indirect comparison.

### 🧩 What you’d get from a test
1. Your Y-haplogroup (e.g., **J1**, **J2**, **E1b1b**, **R1a**, etc.).
2. A list of genetic matches — other men who share the same paternal ancestor.
3. An estimate of how many generations ago you share a common ancestor.
4. Optional matching against specific Jewish heritage projects (e.g. *Cohen DNA Project*, *Jewish Heritage Project*).

### ⚖️ Limitations
- It **can’t prove** you are a Kohen or Davidic descendant; it can only show whether your DNA pattern is *consistent with* those lineages.
- Results depend on comparison databases; conclusions are **statistical**, not absolute.
- If your paternal line converted to Judaism or changed names centuries ago, it might not match traditional clusters even if your family is long Jewish.
