2. **Archaeological and Historical Criticism**:

### Elephantine Jews (500–400 BCE):
The Elephantine community provides a fascinating historical puzzle. Elephantine, an island in Egypt’s Nile river near modern-day Aswan, had a significant Jewish community between roughly 525–400 BCE. Archaeological evidence—most notably, the Elephantine papyri—show a fully functioning Jewish community, with a temple distinct from Jerusalem’s.

2. **Narrative as a Reaction or Response**:

2. **Elephantine Community and the Absence of the Exodus Narrative**:

### Summary of the Scholarly Position:
- **Mainstream scholarship** does not date the Exodus or Moses to after 700 BCE.
- **Minimalist positions** argue no historical Exodus event occurred, instead dating the composition of these stories to the later Judean monarchy or exile/post-exile periods (around or after 700–400 BCE).
- **Elephantine Jews** existed independently as a culturally distinct group in Egypt during the Persian period (5th century BCE), possibly without full knowledge of or adherence to the Judean Torah and Exodus traditions.
- Reconciling these positions requires viewing the Exodus story as primarily theological literature or cultural memory composed or reshaped significantly after 700 BCE, rather than as literal history conflicting with Jewish existence in Egypt later on.

Thus, there isn't direct archaeological or textual evidence explicitly dating Moses after 700 BCE. The scenario above rather emerges from literary criticism and debates about historical memory, collective identity formation, and the theological functions of scripture rather than specific historical events.

### Moses as Literary and Cultural Construction

The story of Moses echoes older Mesopotamian and Egyptian myths in striking ways:

- **Birth Narrative Motif**: Moses’ infancy narrative (being set adrift on water and rescued by royalty) parallels stories like the Akkadian legend of **Sargon of Akkad**, who was likewise placed in a basket in the river.
- **Lawgiver Motif**: Moses as the lawgiver who brings divine commandments to the people parallels Hammurabi, the Babylonian king associated with divine law.
- **Heroic Archetype**: Moses represents a classic mythic hero archetype, who undergoes exile, spiritual enlightenment, and returns to liberate his people. This mirrors heroes from various cultures: Gilgamesh, Osiris, or Sargon, whose narratives provided templates for heroic leaders.

Thus, Moses likely embodies a combination of Near Eastern literary traditions and motifs, crafted during and after the Babylonian Exile (586–538 BCE) when Jews were in direct contact with Babylonian culture and mythology. During this period, Judaism and its texts underwent deep reflection, editing, and redaction, leading to the inclusion or reinterpretation of familiar Near Eastern themes into distinctively Hebrew stories and identities.

### Historical Memory and Cultural Antagonism with Egypt

Moses also strongly reflects the complicated, often antagonistic historical relationships between the ancient Israelites and Egyptians:

Egyptians maintained prolonged control and influence over the Levantine region from about the 16th to the 12th centuries BCE. Memories of oppression, forced labor, or military campaigns could have been symbolically captured and intensified within Exodus narratives.
