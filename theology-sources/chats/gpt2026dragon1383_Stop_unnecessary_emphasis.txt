Self Aware Networks GPT:
That line is a pithy critique of how we approach complex systems. Here’s what I read it to mean:

1. **Qualitative analysis preserves role-differences**  
   - When you do a qualitative study, you start by asking, “Who is the predator? Who is the prey?”  You identify their *distinct* roles, behaviors, and interactions.  You might map out a food-web, note that predators hunt and control prey, prey hide and reproduce, and so on.  You keep those categories (predator vs. prey) front and center.  

2. **Quantitative analysis flattens those distinctions**  
   - By contrast, a purely quantitative approach asks, “What are their population numbers? What are their growth rates? What are their interaction coefficients in the equations?”  You end up with two variables—​let’s call them \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\)—​and you write down something like the Lotka–Volterra equations:
     \[
       \begin{cases}
         \dot x = \alpha x - \beta x y,\\
         \dot y = \delta x y - \gamma y.
       \end{cases}
     \]
     Mathematically, both species become just “functions of time” with parameters, and if you focus only on the numbers you can easily lose sight of *which* is predator and *which* is prey—​they’re just two interacting populations.  

3. **Why that “confusion” matters**  
   - If you don’t keep the qualitative roles in view, you might (for example) swap the species in your data, misinterpret an outbreak of predators as an overgrowth of prey, or fit models that treat both species symmetrically when in reality one preys on the other.  You lose the causal story.  

So Raj’s quip is reminding us:  
> **“Don’t let your love of numbers blind you to the fundamentally different natures of the things you’re measuring.”**  

—​If you only look at counts and rates, you’ll miss the fact that one population *hunts* and the other *flees and reproduces*. Qualitative insight (who is who, what they do) is what keeps that straight.
