In mainstream Jewish thought, the Messiah is not God. He is a human king, usually understood as a descendant of David, who restores Israel, gathers the exiles, strengthens Torah observance, and brings an era of justice and peace. In many classic rabbinic formulations, he also rebuilds the Temple and restores Jewish national life. So on the Jewish side, yes, the Messiah is generally imagined as a man, not a divine incarnation.

That said, the Jewish messianic picture is not simply “a strongman who kills Israel’s enemies.” Victory over enemies can be part of the picture, but the larger test is whether he actually accomplishes the redemptive program. In classical Jewish criteria, the Messiah is recognized by what he concretely establishes: ingathering of exiles, restoration of Davidic rule, Torah-centered renewal, Temple restoration, and an enduring age of peace. A merely useful foreign ruler, even a very pro-Israel one, would not normally qualify.

There is an important biblical complication here. In the Hebrew Bible, a non-Jew can be called God’s “anointed” in a limited political sense. Cyrus the Great is the classic example, because he enabled the Jews’ return and the rebuilding of the Temple. That means Jewish scripture does have a category for a foreign ruler being used by God for Israel’s benefit. But that is not the same thing as being the final Davidic Messiah. So a non-Jewish leader could be seen by some Jews as an instrument, helper, protector, or even a Cyrus-like figure without being the Messiah proper.

So on the narrow question, could Trump try to present himself as a kind of non-Jewish redeemer of Israel? Politically and rhetorically, yes, that is imaginable. There has already been public rhetoric around him as uniquely chosen, unusually favorable to Israel, or Cyrus-like. But in classical Judaism, that still would not make him the Messiah in the full rabbinic sense, because the Messiah is usually expected to be a Jewish Davidic king and to fulfill a much broader set of redemptive conditions than defeating enemies or backing Israel militarily.

Your deeper point is more interesting. If a figure you interpret as the Antichrist wanted to secure Jewish or Christian loyalty, one plausible strategy would be to appear as Israel’s defender, avenger, or deliverer. That idea does fit a broader apocalyptic logic: a deceptive figure would not usually arrive saying, “I am your destroyer.” He would arrive looking like a savior. So as a thought experiment, yes, a false messianic figure could try to win trust by doing things that seem to align with prophetic hopes, national security desires, or covenantal expectations.

But there is still a major distinction between Jewish and Christian frameworks. In Judaism, a messianic claimant is judged by concrete covenantal outcomes in history. In apocalyptic Christianity, the Antichrist is often understood precisely as someone who weaponizes appearances, symbolic fulfillment, and false rescue in order to gain allegiance. So the overlap you are noticing is real: the same public acts could be read by one community as redemptive and by another as a seductive counterfeit. That is one reason these discussions become so charged.

Where I would be careful is this: saying “therefore Trump might claim to be the non-Jewish Messiah” is more a theory about political symbolism than a statement that Judaism itself would accept. Most rabbis would not collapse “friend of Israel” into “Messiah.” The bar is much higher, and the category is different. The more historically grounded Jewish analogue would be “Cyrus-like instrument,” not “non-Jewish Messiah” in the full redemptive sense.

So the cleanest answer is this. Jewish rabbis generally expect a human Messiah, not a god-man. They usually expect him to be Jewish and Davidic. A foreign ruler can sometimes be viewed as an instrument of God for Israel, but not the final Messiah in the classical sense. In a Christian apocalyptic thought experiment, a deceptive end-times ruler could absolutely try to look like Israel’s savior. But that would be a strategy of counterfeit redemption, not proof of genuine messiahship.
